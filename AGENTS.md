# Project guidance

Help build a sustainable, hands-on K–5 math circle in Bellingham. Read `README.md` for background. Prioritize enjoyment, exploration, and substantive mathematical ideas over routine arithmetic practice or accelerated schoolwork.

Keep downloaded references in `external-resources/`, the organizer's 2025–26 material in `lowell-math-circle-year-1/`, and our printable PDFs in `lowell-math-circle-year-2/week-NN/` (combined sets in `combined/`). Editable worksheet sources and builders belong in `lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/`; plans, notes, and their mathematical data stay in `plans/`; build/render intermediates go in `tmp/`. Keep archived versions separate from current outputs. Update links and build paths when moving files.

- **Plan for returning children.** Aim for a varied six-year progression at twenty sessions per year, across rough K–1, 2–3, and 4–5 activity levels. This is about 120 sessions along a child's path. Revisit themes through different problems, representations, and deeper questions; track prior use and related activities as the library grows.
- **Level by prerequisites.** Grade labels are approximate. State reading and arithmetic requirements and the abstraction or reasoning involved. Provide accessible entry points and extensions so children can work at an appropriate level regardless of age.
- **Start concretely.** Use objects, drawings, movement, or games. Allow time to explore manipulatives before adding rules or questions. Favor doing, noticing, conjecturing, and explaining; keep introductions brief and let abstraction grow from examples.
- **Make activities usable.** When developing an activity, include its mathematical idea, prerequisites, materials and preparation, suggested timing, launch prompt, exploration questions, hints, extensions, and facilitator reasoning or solutions. Use the previous hour-long meetings as a starting point for session planning. Keep preparation realistic for a parent volunteer with a full-time job.
- **Learn from the collection.** Consult relevant downloaded sources for both suitable mathematics and effective session formats. Use `lowell-math-circle-year-1/lowell-math-circle/` as the organizer's prior material; the strongest reported lesson is children's need for concreteness and time to learn by doing. Record source titles and page or problem references when adapting material.
- **Revisit teaching lessons.** When working on pedagogy or broader curriculum planning, review relevant parts of `external-resources/msri-math-circle-books/` for additional lessons about successful circles. Distinguish lessons actually found in a source from suggestions or inferences.
- **Check mathematical substance.** Verify solutions and examples, and ensure prompts support discovery and age-appropriate explanation. A proof may be an argument with objects or a drawing; formal notation is not a prerequisite for serious reasoning.

## Launch together

Begin a session with a brief whole-group demonstration of the concrete action everyone will use before distributing different starting tasks. Let children handle the materials first, then show how to make a legal attempt and what they are trying to find or record. For tiling, demonstrate placing blue rhombi on a sheet and trying to fill its outline. Keep the launch short; let the mathematical explanation grow from children's attempts.

## Student-page format

A student worksheet page should contain only:

- A consistent header: `Week 1 / Tiling lab / Grades 2–3`, with the week, topic, and level adjusted for that packet.
- A consistent footer: `Bellingham Math Circle / Week 1 / <id>`.
- `Problem N: <problem description, graphics, space for their answer, etc.>`

Remove Name/Date fields. Do not add a second page title above a numbered problem, generic encouragement, separate “build first” directions, or a “go further” label in place of a clear task. Put essential rules and expected actions directly in the numbered problem, in the same ordinary text style. Include the diagrams and recording space children actually need. Keep preparation, timing, hints, proof follow-ups, and teaching commentary in the facilitator guide.

## Design and review from classroom evidence

- **Give each idea enough concrete work.** Use several purposeful, contrasting examples and enough physical space to try them. Before introducing a second obstruction or a new representation, give children time to encounter and use the first. More examples should reveal a distinction, not just repeat a procedure.
- **Make the next action clear.** Say what to build, change, compare, count, draw, or record. Demonstrate unfamiliar recording conventions. A child should not have to infer the task from a title, teaser, or enrichment label.
- **Sequence representations around their purpose.** Let children act, record, and compare before asking them to organize or explain what happened. Introduce a map, code, or ribbon when it helps answer a question they have encountered; give that representation its own concrete work.
- **Let observed experience change the design.** Record what children tried, understood, found unclear, and wanted to continue. Expand ideas that need more examples, resequence instructions that required repeated adult rescue, and remove or redesign elegant questions that did not land. Distinguish observations from hypotheses about why they happened, and distinguish tested material from new revisions.
- **Preserve depth and flexible pacing.** Keep worthwhile explanation and proof opportunities, using objects, drawings, and adult conversation as appropriate. Put discretionary follow-ups such as “How do you know for sure?” in facilitator notes when they would interrupt the printed task. Choose starting points and further questions by prerequisites and observed readiness; finishing every page is not the goal.
