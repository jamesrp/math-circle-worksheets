# Project guidance

Help build a sustainable, hands-on K–5 math circle in Bellingham. Read `README.md` for background. Prioritize enjoyment, exploration, and substantive mathematical ideas over routine arithmetic practice or accelerated schoolwork.

Keep downloaded references in `external-resources/`, the organizer's 2025–26 material in `lowell-math-circle-year-1/`, and our printable PDFs in `lowell-math-circle-year-2/week-NN/` (combined sets in `combined/`). Editable worksheet sources and builders belong in `lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/`; plans, notes, and their mathematical data stay in `plans/`; build/render intermediates go in `tmp/`. Keep archived versions separate from current outputs. Update links and build paths when moving files.

- **Plan for returning children.** Aim for a varied six-year progression at twenty sessions per year, across rough K–1, 2–3, and 4–5 activity levels. This is about 120 sessions along a child's path. Revisit themes through different problems, representations, and deeper questions; track prior use and related activities as the library grows.
- **Level by prerequisites.** Grade labels are approximate. State reading and arithmetic requirements and the abstraction or reasoning involved. Provide accessible entry points and extensions so children can work at an appropriate level regardless of age.
- **Start concretely.** Use objects, drawings, movement, or games. Allow time to explore manipulatives before adding rules or questions. Favor doing, noticing, conjecturing, and explaining; keep introductions brief and let abstraction grow from examples.
- **Make activities usable.** When developing an activity, include its mathematical idea, prerequisites, materials and preparation, suggested timing, launch prompt, exploration questions, hints, extensions, and facilitator reasoning or solutions. Use the previous hour-long meetings as a starting point for session planning. Keep preparation realistic for a parent volunteer with a full-time job.
- **Learn from the collection.** Consult relevant downloaded sources for both suitable mathematics and effective session formats. Use `lowell-math-circle-year-1/lowell-math-circle/` as the organizer's prior material; the strongest reported lesson is children's need for concreteness and time to learn by doing. Record source titles and page or problem references when adapting material.
- **Revisit teaching lessons.** When working on pedagogy or broader curriculum planning, review relevant parts of `external-resources/msri-math-circle-books/` for additional lessons about successful circles. Distinguish lessons actually found in a source from suggestions or inferences.
- **Check mathematical substance.** Verify solutions and examples, and ensure prompts support discovery and age-appropriate explanation. A proof may be an argument with objects or a drawing; formal notation is not a prerequisite for serious reasoning.

## Facilitator mathematical overview

Every facilitator guide must open, before individual solutions, with a short theorem-first mathematical overview. State the actual facts, invariants or theorems, their assumptions and limits, what children are meant to discover, and a brief map to the grade bands. Distinguish experiments, conjectures and explanations. A vague mathematical-purpose sentence is insufficient: an adult should understand the mathematical destination without reading every solution.

## Launch together

Begin a session with a brief whole-group demonstration of the concrete action everyone will use before distributing different starting tasks. Let children handle the materials first, then show how to make a legal attempt and what they are trying to find or record. For tiling, demonstrate placing blue rhombi on a sheet and trying to fill its outline. Keep the launch short; let the mathematical explanation grow from children's attempts.

For unfamiliar objects, procedures, conversions or traversal codes, put a small explicit worked visual before first use: input, a meaningful intermediate step, and output, with matching labels. Move a suitable existing example forward. Use a non-task instance to demonstrate the procedure without revealing the main theorem or complete target enumeration. Do not add examples mechanically when the task is already visually clear or an ordinary board demonstration suffices. Essential convention examples belong with the shared rules or relevant problem; optional solution scaffolding stays with adults.

## Student-page format

A student worksheet page should contain only:

- A consistent header: `Week 1 / Tiling lab / Grades 2–3`, with the week, topic, and level adjusted for that packet.
- A consistent footer: `Bellingham Math Circle / Week 1 / <id>`.
- Optional brief, unnumbered shared rules at the beginning of the activity.
- `Problem N: <problem description, graphics, space for their answer, etc.>`

Remove Name/Date fields. Do not add a second page title above a numbered problem, generic encouragement, separate “build first” directions, or a “go further” label in place of a clear task. State activity-wide rules once in the opening guidance. Put task-specific rules and the requested action directly in the numbered problem, in the same ordinary text style. Include the diagrams and recording space children actually need. Keep preparation, timing, hints, discretionary proof follow-ups, and teaching commentary in the facilitator guide.

## Substantial problems, concise presentation

- **Give children something substantial to work on.** A numbered problem should usually support five or more minutes of experimenting and thinking without another instruction. Several purposeful, contrasting examples can belong under one shared question. Let the choice and sequence of problems carry the mathematical development.
- **State the goal and essential rules; leave the method open.** Children should understand what they are trying to accomplish. Choosing an approach, noticing patterns, and organizing their work should usually be part of the mathematics. Avoid turning an investigation into tiny directed steps or printing the intended discovery as a procedure. Keep questions open: ask “Can a code describe two different trees?” rather than prescribing the expected conclusion.
- **State shared rules once.** Put brief rules that apply throughout the activity in unnumbered guidance at the beginning. Each numbered problem should contain only what is needed for that particular task. Add constraints only when they are essential to the intended mathematics.
- **Choose the representation that suits the mathematics.** Use words, numbers, symbols, diagrams, objects, or a combination. Logic puzzles, number problems, and other primarily textual activities are equally welcome. Use pictures when they clarify the problem or support exploration. When a diagram already supplies information, avoid repeating it in prose. Concision means removing unnecessary directions and repetition; retain the clues, definitions, and context needed for a rich problem.
- **Keep optional scaffolding with the adults.** Hints, worked solutions, suggested intermediate steps, and prompts directing attention to particular features belong in the facilitator guide, to use when needed. Do not routinely append instructions to count, record, compare, try again, find another method, or explain after every small action.
- **Reduce reading without reducing depth.** Finding all solutions, finding the fewest moves, inventing a difficult puzzle, explaining impossibility, and discovering or proving a rule can each be the central problem. Keep these substantial questions when they are the point of the activity; move discretionary teaching follow-ups to the facilitator guide.
- **Size diagrams for their use.** Use compact, legible diagrams when children will work with separate materials, paper, or whiteboards. Provide large working boards and answer areas when children need to work directly on the printed page. Draw intended regular polygons with equal scaling in both axes. Keep labels close and consistent across large and small versions; give arrows a clear role or remove them.
- **Review for independence and brevity.** Once children understand the rules and the problem, can they get absorbed in the work without continually returning for instructions? Remove sentences that repeat rules, narrate diagrams, supply optional facilitation, or tell children how to solve the problem. Remove disconnected subparts unless their mathematical role is clear. Label additional blank boards as extra workspace, not duplicate numbered problems.

Use [week-02-shared-catalog.pdf](lowell-math-circle-year-2/week-02/week-02-shared-catalog.pdf) and [week-02-shared-catalog-upper.pdf](lowell-math-circle-year-2/week-02/week-02-shared-catalog-upper.pdf) as approved examples of brief directions, substantial tasks, and room for independent thinking. Their diagram-heavy format suits those particular activities; other topics may call for a different presentation. Number problems consecutively within each packet.

## Design and review from classroom evidence

- **Give each idea enough concrete work.** Use several purposeful, contrasting examples and enough physical space to try them. Before introducing a second obstruction or a new representation, give children time to encounter and use the first. More examples should reveal a distinction, not just repeat a procedure.
- **Make the next action clear.** Say what to build, change, compare, count, draw, or record. Demonstrate unfamiliar recording conventions. A child should not have to infer the task from a title, teaser, or enrichment label.
- **Sequence representations around their purpose.** Let children act, record, and compare before asking them to organize or explain what happened. Introduce a map, code, or ribbon when it helps answer a question they have encountered; give that representation its own concrete work. Show bridges explicitly: physical boundary dots → grouping rings; block stacks → one height marker per column → number row; current partners/preferences → both checks for a blocking pair. For simultaneous conditions, use one unchanged reference state; do not turn a static consistency puzzle into sequential updating. Zero-valued inputs can still count as participants.
- **Keep bookkeeping light.** Externalize state and records so reasoning gets attention. Prefer one recoverable record, such as a running move word, plus the final state; derive counts later rather than requiring concurrent tallies. When complete case coverage matters, children can share cases and pool a checkable collection; each child need not reproduce the whole catalog. Use supplied movable cards when sorting is the mathematics. Avoid making transcription or memory the main demand.
- **Let observed experience change the design.** Record what children tried, understood, found unclear, and wanted to continue. Expand ideas that need more examples, resequence instructions that required repeated adult rescue, and remove or redesign elegant questions that did not land. Distinguish observations from hypotheses about why they happened, and distinguish tested material from new revisions.
- **Preserve depth and flexible pacing.** A theme need not fit a single hour. Retain rich mathematical material, especially in grades 4–5; use the facilitator guide to suggest a manageable first route and readiness-dependent continuations or return visits. Keep worthwhile explanation and proof opportunities, using objects, drawings, and adult conversation as appropriate. Put discretionary follow-ups such as “How do you know for sure?” in facilitator notes when they would interrupt the printed task. Finishing every page is not the goal.
- **Build encore investigations.** Follow the [Week 1 encore](lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-01-encore/README.md) model: revisit familiar materials with questions that deepen the mathematics or add a different mathematical direction, rather than merely changing numbers. Keep K–5 entry points by prerequisites, and make concrete starting tasks available alongside deeper work. Track a theme separately from the multiple investigations and visits it can support; record what was actually used so returning children can resume or explore something new.
- **Review age fit operationally.** Giftedness does not guarantee advanced reading, executive function or tolerance for simultaneous representation demands. After a normal launch, check whether children retain the mathematical choices. Distinguish routine adult reading or recording from adults operating the whole investigation. Separate the concrete core from readiness-dependent extensions, and state realistic adult staffing and material preparation. Keep advanced substance where children can act, observe and check. Treat unpiloted reviewer concerns as hypotheses, not bans or automatic redesign; respect the organizer's accepted activities and deferred concerns.

## Verification and version consistency

- **Track coverage per student packet.** For collection-wide changes, record each packet as actually changed, already sufficient with page evidence, or not yet checked. Guide edits do not count as student-example edits. Inspect actual final student PDFs and every relevant grade band and variant before claiming a property across the collection; inspect unknown history rather than assuming a previous pass covered it.
- **Verify the delivered outputs.** Before claiming completion, verify the mathematics, render and inspect changed outputs, and check clean rebuilds from extracted source packages. Check every relevant polygon side count, diagram size, grade band and variant in the rendered PDFs. Mathematical and digital checks do not establish physical readiness or classroom piloting: rehearse activities that depend on exact material fit or procedures, or honestly mark that rehearsal untested.
- **Keep versions consistent.** Synchronize current PDFs, guides, editable sources, source ZIPs, reference copies, combined sets and indexes as applicable. Keep archives separate and identify which version is current locally and in remote copies such as Drive; do not imply a local revision has been uploaded or that remote copies are current without checking.

## Workflow to generate worksheets

To draft a week's student worksheets, use [worksheet-workflow/](worksheet-workflow/README.md) rather than writing the pages in one pass yourself. It is the best of ten workflows tested in [experiments/worksheet-genlab-opus5.5/](experiments/worksheet-genlab-opus5.5/REPORT.md), and the organizer preferred its output to his own hand-edited Week 1 in a blind review.

1. Write the week's outline from `worksheet-workflow/outlines/TEMPLATE.md`: the mathematical kernels, a one-line emphasis per band, and the materials with their physical constraints. Leave the choice of problems to the writer stage.
2. Check that `worksheet-workflow/context.md` matches the current group.
3. Run `python3 worksheet-workflow/make_prompts.py --outline <outline> --run tmp/worksheet-runs/<week>-v1`.
4. Run the stage files in order, each in a fresh agent (a subagent in Claude Code, a separate `codex exec` run or session in Codex): `PROMPT.md` (writer), then `CRITIC.md` (adversarial review; add `CRITIC-MATH.md` for weeks whose answers come from enumeration), then `REVISE.md`. The reviewer must not be the writer.
5. If you were started with one of these stage files, do only that stage.
6. Present `final/*.pdf` for review. After approval, move the packet into `lowell-math-circle-year-2/` as described above and record it as unpiloted.

Keep the workflow's prompt files as a set. Change them only deliberately, and log each change in `worksheet-workflow/README.md`. `worksheet-workflow/blocks/spec.md` is the writer-facing version of the student-page guidance above; if either changes, update the other. The workflow produces student pages only; a facilitator guide is a separate, untested step.
