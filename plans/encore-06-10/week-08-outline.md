Week 8: Rook race and Nim - return visits

Mathematical kernels

1. Misere Nim: one move removes any positive number from one pile, but taking the final counter loses. If some pile is larger than1, ordinary xor strategy applies until the move that makes all piles of size1, at which point leave an odd number of one-counter piles. If all nonempty piles have size1, the next player loses exactly when their number is odd. For exactly two piles: losing starts are(1,0),(0,1),(a,a) for a>=2; (1,1) is winning. This flips the finishing logic in Week8 and requires a new strategy at the boundary, not just renamed winning/losing states. Do not assume no-move terminal is the same as normal play: the game ends on taking the last counter.

2. Silver-dollar row: counters occupy distinct numbered squares1,2,...; square0 is a left wall, not a playable square. A move slides one counter left one or more empty squares without jumping another counter; no legal move loses. For coins at positions x1<x2<...<xk, pair them from the right. Record empty gaps inside these pairs; if k is odd, the unpaired left coin contributes x1-1. Their xor determines losing positions. For two coins only the gap between them matters (adjacent coins lose regardless of how far they are from the wall); for three coins the relevant gaps are x1-1 and x3-x2-1. Children act before a gap record appears; pairing must be shown visually if used on student pages. Row gaps remain physical, no ordinary pile-removal assumption.

3. Two rook boards played as one game: on each turn select one board and make one ordinary rook move left or down; the player putting BOTH tokens at their stars wins. Independent boards have four coordinate piles and xor decides the outcome. Two identical winning starts combine to a losing game because the second player can copy on the other board; conversely two component games that separately favour the second player cannot be made winning by their sum. A substantive new question asks which distinct component starts cancel, including neither component being an ordinary diagonal loser. Example(1,0) and(2,3) cancel because1 xor2 xor3=0; a same-total criterion fails. Exact board orientation/distance-to-star matters. Do not turn this into the base three-pile enumeration.

Sources: finite game recursion and the pairing invariant for the coin game; current Week8 F08-v4 includes normal two/three-pile Nim, binary proof and queen game, so those are familiar tools, not new investigations.

Suggested emphasis by level: K-1: two-counter coin-slide games on large physical squares with adult support. Grades2-3: coin row and small reversed-ending Nim. Grades4-5: boundary strategy, gap invariants and two-board cancellation. Counting up to12, move legality checked by partner; xor formalism optional adult extension only.
