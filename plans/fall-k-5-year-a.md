# Fall math circle across K–1, 2–3, and 4–5

Revised September 19, 2026. Ten weekly meetings of about one hour. Weeks 2–10 now develop explicit undergraduate and research mathematics through concrete investigations, using revised Week 1 as the benchmark. The [redesign rationale](fall-weeks-02-10-mathematical-redesign.md) explains the progression; the [print index](../lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/fall-weeks-02-10/README.md) provides three student levels, a grades 6–7 extra page, and a facilitator packet for each week. The [companion activity guide](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/fall-k-5-year-a-activities.md) links full instructions, hints, and checked solutions. Record actual use in the [session log](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/fall-k-5-year-a-use-log.md).

Each week shares a mathematical idea, a launch, and most of its materials. The levels change how children enter the problem and how far they investigate it. All three use physical exploration. Explanations can be spoken, drawn, or demonstrated with objects.

Each week also has one **optional grades 6–7 page**. Offer it by readiness, with the additional prerequisites listed in the weekly guide. It is a reserve, not another required stage.

## Choosing an entry point

These labels guide preparation, not placement by age. A child can use picture messages while doing advanced code reasoning, or explore a small game deeply while another child plays on a larger board.

| Starting version | Reading and arithmetic | Reasoning to support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **K–1** | No independent reading or writing required. Match symbols, compare heights, count small collections with support. | Follow one rule at a time; test a choice; show what changed. Use large pieces, visible examples, and short rounds. |
| **2–3** | Instructions may be read aloud. Recognize the supplied letters or replace them with pictures; small counts and subtraction through play. | Coordinate constraints, classify small cases, and give a reason covering more than one example. |
| **4–5** | Counting, addition/subtraction, and odd/even reasoning support most tasks. Multiples, remainders, and binary bundles are introduced concretely where used. | Distinguish examples from proofs; establish uniqueness, impossibility, completeness, or optimality. Formal proof notation is unnecessary. |

The original feedback still guides the whole plan: shapes, ciphers, blocks, games, logic, and billiards were popular. Formal combinatorial counting is optional at every level. The older version should first offer a satisfying puzzle; its general explanation is a further invitation.

## A shared hour

**Current roster:** K, K, 1; 3, 3, 3; and one fifth grader, together in one room with the organizer and one supporting parent. Use the two triplets as working groups. Give the fifth grader a workspace beside the third graders, with access to their conversation and a suitable challenge of their own. Placement remains flexible as we learn what each child enjoys and can do.

The books support shared themes, different depths, independent attempts, and substantial adult help. They do not supply a tested arrangement for these seven children and two adults. The following is our starting proposal; see the [lesson-format source notes][lesson-format] for the evidence and its limits.

### Adult coverage and timing

Start with the supporting parent mainly anchored to KK1, helping with rules, materials, and short rounds. The organizer starts the third graders and fifth grader, then moves between them. Either adult can shift when a group is working comfortably, but keep an agreed next visit so no group has to compete for attention. Both adults should know the launch, a worked example, and one helpful hint before the session.

| Approximate time | Children | Adult attention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0–10** | Explore the week's materials; returning children can try a familiar kind of challenge. | Both adults observe and join in. Notice who needs help handling the materials. |
| **10–15** | One brief shared demonstration of the idea or rules. | Organizer demonstrates; supporting parent helps the youngest follow. Save level-specific extensions for the tables. |
| **15–20** | Begin the starting tasks. | Parent demonstrates a complete K–1 example. Organizer gets 333 started, then gives the fifth grader a personal launch. The fifth grader can start with the common puzzle while waiting. |
| **20–35** | Investigate, play, and compare attempts. KK1 may do several short rounds. | Parent stays mainly with KK1. Organizer alternates between 333 and 5, aiming to return within about five minutes rather than waiting for someone to ask. Leave a concrete next thing to try at each visit. |
| **35–40** | Brief movement/reset, with an optional discovery to show. | Check engagement and adjust the next task; no requirement for all levels to have reached the same point. |
| **40–55** | Continue a good investigation, change a constraint, or try an extension. | Same coverage; reserve some direct discussion or play with the fifth grader. |
| **55–60** | Each group can show one discovery, question, or failed attempt; tidy up. | Help explanations use objects or drawings. The older extension need not become a lesson for everyone. |

These times are flexible. A productive construction or game can run across a boundary; a stalled group needs a change sooner. The important rhythm is trying, talking about an attempt, and trying again.

The individual weekly plans and facilitator packets use this same default, including the **35–40 movement/reset**. Each group has a satisfying stopping point plus an optional proof continuation. A proof can wait until another visit; note whether a claim was tried, conjectured, checked in named cases, proved, or given as a theorem in the [use log](fall-k-5-year-a-use-log.md). For the general arithmetic in Weeks 4 and 9, the [shared divisibility scaffold](coprime-divisibility-scaffold.md) provides an adult-supported bridge beyond finite examples.

### Working in threes

- **Blocks, shapes, towers, and billiards:** provide enough pieces and work surfaces for all three children to make attempts, then compare them. A triplet need not share a single construction. Invite each child to show a move or observation before settling on a group answer.
- **Codes:** rotate sender, solver, and checker. In hidden color codes, the checker may see the secret and verify the feedback without revealing it to the solver. For KK1 the parent can handle scoring while children take turns setting and guessing.
- **Take-away and Rook's Move:** preserve two opposing sides, since three-player rules change the mathematics. Two children can play while the third checks legal moves and chooses the next starting position; rotate after every short round, regardless of who wins. If that role is unsatisfying, use one child against a team of two, alternating which teammate moves and rotating the solo role between rounds.

### Including the fifth grader

Start from the shared problem when it is interesting, then offer the deeper version according to readiness. Keep the fifth grader near 333 for shared discoveries and puzzle exchanges, with their own materials when the questions diverge. Independent work should alternate with real mathematical conversation; being the oldest does not mean being left alone or assigned to teach younger children.

For games, explicitly budget the organizer as an opponent. Between short games, the fifth grader can investigate a supplied position, find a response to each possible opponent move, or build a position to challenge the adult on their return. For secret-code games, use a prepared puzzle or have the adult hold the secret and give feedback during visits. Weeks 6–8 need more adult availability than independent construction weeks; when necessary, the other group continues a familiar round while the organizer plays or checks feedback.

A third parent would let each group have an adult, and would be especially useful as a regular opponent and discussion partner for the fifth grader. Until then, try this arrangement and note where children wait for attention. Also note whether the fifth grader enjoys the balance of shared and independent work; another fourth or fifth grader would add peer companionship as well as solving the opponent problem.

Gather everyone for a concrete demonstration of putting blues into the middle-level boards before handing out separate level tasks. Show the action, a filling, and an unfinished attempt; let children help place the pieces. This responds to the organizer's report that managing separate starts around two tables was too much at once. Keep free handling, the movement break, and time for explanations.

K–1 retains easy filling mats with direct numbered problems. Middle students now have four larger starting boards, side-2 through side-5 triangle packings, and six blue-versus-purple comparisons; pink triangles are removed. Upper students first build and record, then map flips directly on six cards, find routes, and investigate ribbons through their own sequence of problems. The ribbon's adjacent RL/LR swaps lead to a parity proof: the sum of the R positions changes parity at every flip.

Use 1× pieces and check actual printed scale. For the current 3/3/1 roster, gather 56 blues, 51 greens and 18 purples plus a few red/yellow K–1 pieces; reuse between mats. The larger middle triangles require ten blues per child. The complete facilitator guide has solutions, bounds and hints. ## Preparation and future reuse

One shared kit per theme should do most of the work. The new [weekly plans](fall-weeks-02-10-mathematical-redesign.md) supersede the earlier Weeks 2–10 proposals, which are preserved in [the archive](archive-pre-depth/fall-k-5-year-a.md). Vary the outlines, number of code positions, clue sets, starting piles, and board sizes through small task cards. Use visible icons and dot counts where possible. For concurrent groups, label the take-away move cards clearly so 1-or-2 and 1/3/4 games do not get mixed up.

Keep a record of the actual challenges each cohort encounters, not only the theme. Returning children should meet new diagrams, messages, clue sets, or questions when they move to another level. An older version need not repeat the youngest starter if a child already knows it. The saved Lowell materials contain only part of last year's history, so check additional prior use before choosing the final instances.

The common-theme approach is supported by [Math Circle by the Bay][bay], preface pp. viii–x (PDF pp. 9–11), which describes using themes across age groups with different pace and depth, manipulatives, and repeated practice explaining ideas. The specific level assignments here are our proposed adaptations, to be adjusted after observing the children. Relevant MSRI teaching notes and the broader source review remain in the [original plan][year-a].
