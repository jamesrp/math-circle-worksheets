## Mathematical overview: facts, assumptions and limits

Let the fixed population have N distinct individuals. The first catch is a set A of size M; the second is a set B of size n; their intersection has size R. Each catch has no within-catch replacement, and all of the first catch has returned before the second begins.

1. **Exact support and union.** The number of different individuals seen is M+n−R. Thus, for fixed valid M,n,R, a population is possible exactly when N≥M+n−R. For fixed N,M,n with 0≤M,n≤N, the possible overlaps are every integer from max(0,M+n−N) to min(M,n). Necessity follows from intersection size and the union fitting inside the population. Sufficiency follows by choosing R marked individuals and n−R unmarked individuals for the second catch. Adding any number of unseen individuals proves there is no finite upper bound when no outside population cap is imposed. These are deterministic statements, independent of random sampling.

2. **Mean overlap under the model.** If the second catch is a uniformly chosen n-element subset of the N individuals, each marked individual is selected with probability n/N. Write the overlap as the sum of M indicator variables; linearity of expectation gives E(R)=Mn/N without requiring those indicators to be independent. Equivalently, each possible overlap has probability C(M,r)C(N−M,n−r)/C(N,n). The first catch need not itself be random to establish this conditional expectation if the second catch really is uniform; uniform, mark-independent catches are a sufficient protocol used by the pages.

3. **Fraction matching is an estimate.** Setting M/N equal to R/n gives the proposed total Mn/R when R>0. Observed overlap need not equal its expectation. The result may be nonintegral, can be above or below the true total, and is not generally unbiased. When M,n>0 and R=0, no finite total makes the two fractions exactly equal, but every finite N≥M+n can produce this observation. Infinity is not a recovered population size. Even R=min(M,n) need not establish a census. For positive R, the estimate is at least the union bound because Mn/R−(M+n−R)=(M−R)(n−R)/R≥0; this does not make it the true total.

4. **Balanced sampling budget.** For fixed population N and budget s=M+n≤N, the mean overlap is M(s−M)/N. If two positive integer catch sizes differ by at least two, transfer one unit from the larger a to the smaller b: (a−1)(b+1)−ab=a−b−1>0. Repeating leaves sizes floor(s/2), ceil(s/2), which maximize the product; when s is odd the two orders tie. Equivalently, Mn=(s²−(M−n)²)/4. This maximizes expected overlap, not necessarily precision, bias, cost efficiency under another cost model, or every definition of estimator quality. All budgets in the pages are feasible because N=24 and 2≤s≤24.

5. **Marks and closure matter.** Interpreting detected marks as shared identities requires marks to remain visible and no false marks. A closed unchanged population, full return, mixing, no within-catch replacement and equal catchability justify the intended use. Preserving only the numerical total while replacing identities would not be closure. Lost marks can lower detected overlap while N stays fixed; a positive ratio using the original M then increases relative to the same catch with all marks retained. If exactly K of M marks survive before a uniform second catch, expected detected overlap is Kn/N; if each mark survives with probability q independently of capture, it is qMn/N. This is a statement about expected overlap; replacing R by its expectation inside a reciprocal is not a proof of estimator expectation. Without retained marks, M+n−observed R may cease to be a valid lower bound: N=M=n=6 with all marks lost gives observed R=0 although there are only six individuals.

Grades 2–3 meet the exact set-overlap mathematics through objects and distinguish feasible totals from guesses. Grades 4–5 add fraction matching, variation, an exact experimental-design optimum and tests of a model assumption. Live catches are experiments. A guess from them is an inference. The union construction and balanced-product argument are explanations establishing general facts.

## Grades 2–3: all task answers

**Page 1, Problem 1.** Three live rounds use the same N in 8…20, with M=n=4. Each round may have any R in 0…4. There is no fixed numerical answer for the guesses. Restore all objects, erase all marks and start afresh before each round. The population is revealed after the guesses, not interpreted as something the data necessarily force. The expected overlap is 16/N, ranging from 2 to 0.8, but this formula is adult context rather than a required younger-child method.

**Page 2, Problem 2.** First catch A,B,C,D,E; second B,D,E,F,G. The marked repeated identities are exactly B,D,E. Union A…G has seven individuals, so the smallest bag is 7. Among the offered totals 6,7,8,12,20, all except 6 are possible. Construct a larger compatible bag by adding unseen identities while keeping the shown catches. The earlier live-game bound 8…20 belongs to Problem 1 and is not a global bound for this new construction task.

**Page 2, Problem 3.** The disjoint five-object catches have union A…J, so the minimum is 10. There is no largest possible total with arbitrarily many cards. Add fresh unseen identities to the ten-object construction. No probabilistic argument is needed.

**Page 3, Problem 4.** The sequence R=2,1,3,1,2,0 with M=n=4 is possible for both N=8 and N=16. Under independent fresh uniform rounds, its probability is positive at each total. The sequence cannot logically identify either bag. A reasonable qualitative per-round guess is 8,16,8,16,8,16, since larger overlaps lean toward smaller populations, but a child's chosen guess is not a deterministic right-or-wrong answer.

For adult exact checking, the per-round likelihood ratio P(R=r|N=8)/P(R=r|N=16) for r=0,1,2,3,4 is respectively 26/495, 26/55, 26/11, 26/3, 26. These verify those single-round preference directions. For the printed six-round sequence, the product is 308915776/543547125≈0.5683. Thus these data weakly favor 16 over 8 under this model, roughly a 1.76:1 likelihood ratio. They do not prove 16. Likelihood is not a posterior probability unless a prior is also specified. Do not put a uniquely correct recovered total in a guide. The mean observed overlap is 1.5, between the means 2 and 1, which offers a child-accessible reason for uncertainty.

**Page 3, Problem 5.** Both bags can produce either R=0 or R=3. Fix first catch A,B,C,D. In the eight-object bag A…H, E,F,G,H gives zero and A,B,C,E gives three. The same catches are possible in a sixteen-object bag with extra unseen individuals. The existence task can be settled by deliberate arrangements; a finite run of random trials need not happen to display every possibility. The word “both” refers to different rounds: one second catch does not simultaneously have two overlap counts.

**Page 4, Problem 6.** With M=5,n=4, possible overlaps are: N=6: {3,4}; N=8: {1,2,3,4}; N=12: {0,1,2,3,4}. Fix marked objects 1…5; for a target r choose r of these and 4−r from the N−5 unmarked objects. This constructs every listed possibility. Fewer than 9−N overlaps would make the union exceed N, and more than four cannot fit in the second catch. Both necessity and sufficiency are established.

**Page 4, Problem 7.** Valid ranges for selected bag N=6,7,8 are {3,4}, {2,3,4}, {1,2,3,4}, respectively. Within the ordinarily plausible range 0…4, the impossible records are respectively {0,1,2}, {0,1}, {0}. Any valid/impossible pair works; R=4 versus R=0 works for all three choices. A reported overlap outside 0…4 is also impossible but yields a less substantial partner puzzle. Children may use explicit identity catches; the union test then detects impossibility even if each catch has the requested number of objects.

## Grades 4–5: all task answers

**Page 1, Problem 1.** Three live rounds have one fixed N in 12…24, M=n=6. Every R in 0…6 is possible for every allowed N. Guesses and live results vary; the keeper's final reveal is the actual total. The sampling mean is 36/N, from 3 to 1.5. No single positive overlap or three-round record establishes the exact population.

**Page 2, Problem 2.** Record A (R=2) needs at least 6+6−2=10. Example first 1…6, second 1,2,7,8,9,10. Record B (R=0) needs at least 12; use first 1…6, second 7…12. Neither has a finite maximum without an external cap. The task explicitly allows arbitrarily many cards; the range in Problem 1 is not global.

**Page 2, Problem 3.** The rule solves 6/N=2/6, giving N=18. The six marked objects and six second-catch objects, exactly two marked, are drawn correctly. The N=10 construction from Problem 2 is a different possible bag, so the record does not force 18. N=12,24 or any integer at least 10 also works. For a concrete comparison, the second-catch marked fraction 1/3 need not equal the whole-bag marked fraction 6/10; uniform sampling allows such a fluctuation.

**Page 3, Problem 4.** The printed rule values 36,18,12,18 and “No matching total” for R=1,2,3,2,0 are all correct. A single unchanged population N=18 can produce every record. More strongly, every integer N≥12 can produce the complete sequence; the zero-overlap round imposes the largest individual lower bound, 12. Do not sum unions across rounds, since the records do not track which individual identities occurred between rounds.

An explicit common-population construction keeps first catch 1…6 (it is permitted, though not required, for a fresh random first catch to repeat). For R=r, take second catch {1,…,r} together with {7,…,12−r}, with the first set empty at r=0. All five second catches fit in population 1…18. The checker verifies positive exact sequence likelihood 611490515625/1265766299207501 under independent uniform rounds. There is no unique correct report of the total. A well-supported report distinguishes “at least 12 is forced” from “18 is possible” and from a guessed total.

If children choose to combine overlap evidence by matching the observed mean 8/5, the resulting pooled ratio is 36/(8/5)=22.5, still only an estimate, and it need not be taught here. Averaging the four positive per-round guesses gives 21 but silently omits the zero record; that is a different procedure and should not be treated as a mathematically preferred or unbiased answer. A guide should welcome awareness of variability rather than prescribe an unsupported precise total.

For an exact example of ratio limitations, under N=18,M=n=6 the mean R is 2, but P(R=0)=11/221>0. The mean of 36/R conditional on R>0 is 305797/14700≈20.8025, not 18. This finite-model result demonstrates why replacing an observed overlap with the mean or averaging reciprocals is not an unbiasedness argument. It is adult checking mathematics, not a worksheet enumeration demand.

**Page 3, Problem 5.** Zero overlap rules out neither finite bags generally nor N=18. N=12 and N=18 give two concrete examples, with disjoint catches 1…6 and 7…12. Possible design improvements include larger feasible catches or additional properly reset rounds; both can increase the amount of evidence. Raising either M or n while holding the other positive and N fixed increases expected overlap. Neither more rounds nor larger samples guarantees a particular convenient observed overlap or exact recovery in a particular run. Ensure larger proposed catches can be drawn: the known lower bound here is only 12, so catches up to 12 are universally feasible, while larger catches need further knowledge of N. No fixed handling budget is imposed in this problem.

**Page 4, Problem 6.** Budget 12 and N=24 give unique balanced optimum (6,6), with mean 36/24=3/2. For splits (1,11),(2,10),(3,9),(4,8),(5,7),(6,6), products are 11,20,27,32,35,36; reversing a split leaves it unchanged. Even if empty catches were admitted, their zero mean does not alter the optimum. The unit-transfer argument in the overview establishes no split does better. A finite sequence of rounds can have a different ranking through random variation; the page's supplied formula explicitly concerns the average over all equally likely second catches.

**Page 4, Problem 7.** The bag remains N=24 from Problem 6. Budget 13 has two ordered optima (6,7) and (7,6), each with mean 42/24=7/4. Budget 20 has (10,10), mean 100/24=25/6. For every whole budget 2…24 use floor(s/2),ceil(s/2) in either order. Even budgets have a single ordered optimum; odd ones have two. The largest mean for a given budget is floor(s²/4)/24. Integer and feasibility constraints are met throughout.

**Page 4, Problem 8.** The quoted “must be large” is an assertion to challenge. Small observed overlap can occur by chance even with intact marks; lost marks provide another explanation. With the same actual second catch and no false marks, R_observed≤R_true. If both are positive then Mn/R_observed≥Mn/R_true; if all detected overlap disappears, the ratio is undefined. Thus losing marks tends to make this particular guessing procedure suggest too large a population, without making that suggestion reliable.

A decisive concrete test uses the same unchanged population, the same sample sizes and the same actual catches, with permanent visible identities (not tactile cues) as an independent record. Before returning the first catch, record its identities and marks. On the second catch, count overlap once by identities and once by visible dots. A recaptured identity whose dot disappeared directly demonstrates undercounting due to mark loss in that very round. Counting all initially marked identities' retained dots afterward gives an additional retention audit. If no dots disappeared in that round, mark loss did not cause its small observed overlap; chance or another model failure can remain. A partial audit that misses unobserved lost marks is not proof of universal retention.

An alternative controlled experiment compares erasable and durable marking methods while keeping population, catch sizes, handling and capture procedure the same, using fresh resets and repeated rounds. Equal touch/visibility behavior during blind drawing is essential; clips, folds or differently shaped marks would introduce catchability bias. Population and mark retention cannot both be freely changed and then attributed separately. With independent retention probability q, the mean detected overlap qMn/N shows why the single observed statistic cannot by itself distinguish low retention from a larger N. A finite result cannot prove every alternative absent.
