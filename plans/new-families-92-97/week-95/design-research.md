## Week 94: Calibration lab

**Destination.** Measurement alone does not distinguish an object from its instrument. For a fixed additive instrument, `y = x + b`, the readings remain unchanged when every hidden length increases by `t` and the instrument offset decreases by `t`. Repeating the same measurement cannot distinguish those worlds. Differences of readings recover differences of lengths. One reference of known length determines the common offset; a second unknown object can then be recovered.

For an affine instrument `y = a + bx`, with fixed positive gain, one reference leaves multiple possible instruments. Two distinct reference lengths determine `b = (y2-y1)/(x2-x1)` and `a = y1-bx1`. With bounded reading errors of magnitude at most `e` and exact references separated by `D>0`, the raw admissible slope interval has width `4e/D`. Intersect it with positive slopes when required. Thus wider reference separation improves the worst-case slope information under this fixed affine/error model. More repeated readings need not improve an adversarial bounded-error guarantee; they are not automatically independent mean-zero errors. A third standard can refute the affine model, but agreement at finitely many standards cannot prove the instrument is affine everywhere.

**Concrete action.** Put rods or paper strips against a shifted scale, or have a partner operate a known affine reading machine behind a screen. The same instrument setting persists across a round. Calibrating objects must have genuinely known lengths: supply unit strips and a way to check them. For arbitrary-output machines, describe the machine as a mathematical model, not an actual physical ruler with unexplained behavior. Show input rod, shifted position, reported reading before any task asks children to interpret this convention.

**Prerequisites and route.** K–1 has a legitimate short entry: compare intervals on a scale whose zero is missing and make the same length at different positions, counting spaces rather than endpoint labels. This is a concrete sub-investigation, not the full parameter-inference theorem. Grades 2–3 can identify fixed shifts and choose a reference, with integer addition/subtraction within about 20 and oral reading. Grades 4–5 can distinguish shift from stretch, choose two standards and compare noisy calibration designs; elementary ratios and reasoning about several compatible explanations are the actual gates. No assumption that all fourth graders can use algebra. The upper material can occupy a return visit.

**Boundary from existing work.** Week 51 uses interval sums/differences and common-variable cancellation, including intersection of noisy range claims in the bonus. Week 94's question is which experiment identifies an unknown instrument and which nuisance parameters remain invisible. Do not devote its pages to redoing interval intersections. Week 6 and Week 25 bonus optimize noiseless questions among finite states; the new obstacle is a continuous family of object/instrument pairs with exactly the same readings. Week 18 corrects arbitrary discrete flips through code separation; this family concerns fixed systematic error and calibration standards, not another codebook. Atlas AP-05 gives repeated-sampling coverage, not calibration; AP-18/AP-19 have advanced physics inverse models, not elementary affine instrument design.

## Week 95: Count the unseen

**Destination.** Two samples can reveal information about objects not seen in either. From a closed population of `N` individually distinguishable objects, mark `M`, return them and mix, then take a uniform `n`-subset without replacement. Let `R` be the number of marked objects in that second sample. Exactly `M+n-R` different objects have been seen, giving a sharp lower bound on `N`, and every larger population remains possible. There is no finite upper bound based on a finite two-sample record alone.

Under the specified sampling model, `E(R)=Mn/N`: each of the `M` marked objects has chance `n/N` of appearing in the second sample. Matching observed overlap to its expectation suggests the Lincoln–Petersen estimate `Mn/R` for `R>0`; this is an estimate, not an identity or an unbiased estimator. `R=0` is a real failure of that formula, not evidence of an infinite population. The exact sampling law is `P(R=r)=C(M,r)C(N-M,n-r)/C(N,n)`; keep this as adult checking mathematics unless children independently want the counting argument.

**Experimental-design kernel.** Fix total handling budget `M+n=s`, with feasible sample sizes. Expected overlap is proportional to `M(s-M)`, maximized when the two sample sizes are as equal as possible. The rectangle/product proof connects this inference question to extremal area, but now the product measures opportunities to meet an already marked object. State the objective precisely: maximum expected overlap, not a universal minimum estimation error. The exact zero-overlap probability and comparison of candidate population sizes are optional adult-supported continuations. With `N>=s`, the balanced split also minimizes zero-overlap probability; this extra result requires its own independent check before inclusion.

**Concrete action.** Uniform same-size tiles or cards in an opaque bag; mark captured objects by turning their otherwise identical card to its marked face, or apply marks that cannot be felt during blind drawing. Return the entire first catch before the second catch; hold the entire second catch out until its overlap has been counted. A worked visual must show a small first catch, those same objects back in the full bag, and a second catch with repeated identities. Use one compact record `(M,n,R)` and a visible overlap arrangement, not concurrent tallies. Before an estimation task, demonstrate why a repeated identity is the same object, not an additional object.

**Substantial actions.** Children should build several populations consistent with a single pair of catches, compare population guesses using actual sampling, decide which extra sampling plan is useful, and construct a population concealed from a faulty sampling procedure. A few pooled trials can expose variability; no requirement to fill long random-data tables. Do not turn the packet into plugging numbers into `Mn/R`. Let sample-size design and the difference between a certain bound and a model-based estimate carry the upper work.

**Prerequisites and route.** Honest Grades 2–3/4–5 family. Grades 2–3 can identify repeated objects, build different consistent populations, compare low/high overlap with equal catch sizes, and use counters to reason about a concealed subgroup; addition and subtraction within 20, adult reading. Grades 4–5 can develop the proportion estimate and product design, with multiplication, simple division and discussion of chance versus certainty. No forced K–1 packet: acting out two catches can be an optional common launch but is not claimed as a full Kindergarten investigation. At least one anchored adult per active table, with an adult preparing concealed populations and helping preserve the sampling mechanism.

**Boundary from existing work.** Weeks 42–45 treat fairness, dependence, shuffles, conditioning and clue mechanisms; the population size here is unknown and sampling variability changes an estimate. Atlas AP-04 already establishes sampling-frame bias and indistinguishable populations; do not present the excluded-subgroup counterexample alone as new. Its role here is testing assumptions of a distinct overlap estimator. The new central structure is two-sample identity overlap, its expected value, and allocation of a sampling budget. No capture–recapture family was found in corpus/index searches. Week 51's repeated range observations are not sampling unseen individuals.

### Further overlap and exact caution

Week 51 bonus Problem 1 already maximizes rectangle area at fixed side sum. Here balanced rectangles supply a connected proof technique, not a separate novelty claim; the new object being optimized is expected recapture overlap and the new primary result is inference on unseen population size. For a clean exact demonstration that the ratio estimate is not unbiased even without zero-overlap outcomes, take `N=6, M=4, n=3`. Then overlaps `1,2,3` have probabilities `1/5,3/5,1/5`; estimates `12,6,4` have mean `34/5`, different from the actual population 6. This is an adult check, not a proposed student table.
