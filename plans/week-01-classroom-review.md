## Concrete launch to try next time

After a little time to handle the materials, gather everyone around a visible blue-only board. Place one blue rhombus over two whole triangular spaces and turn it into another legal position. Invite children to help continue the filling; keep the same simple rules visible through the demonstration: pieces stay inside the board, use whole spaces, and do not overlap. Compare an attempted filling with a gap against one that fills a board. Ask what they notice, then send children to their starting tasks with the first action already familiar.

This launch establishes how to work with the sheets; it need not reveal the up/down argument or settle an impossibility claim. A gap in one arrangement is something to investigate, not yet proof that no arrangement works. Keep different entry points and adult help available after the launch. This operating proposal responds to the organizer's report; it has not yet been tested with this group.

## Adopted AGENTS.md guidance

The following guidance was approved and adopted verbatim on September 27, 2026. It extends the existing “Start concretely” and “Make activities usable” guidance and applies to both authors and reviewers.

### Launch together

Begin a session with a brief whole-group demonstration of the concrete action everyone will use before distributing different starting tasks. Let children handle the materials first, then show how to make a legal attempt and what they are trying to find or record. For tiling, demonstrate placing blue rhombi on a sheet and trying to fill its outline. Keep the launch short; let the mathematical explanation grow from children's attempts.

### Student-page format

A student worksheet page should contain only:

- A consistent header: `Week 1 / Tiling lab / Grades 2–3`, with the week, topic, and level adjusted for that packet.
- A consistent footer: `Bellingham Math Circle / Week 1 / <id>`.
- `Problem N: <problem description, graphics, space for their answer, etc.>`

Remove Name/Date fields. Do not add a second page title above a numbered problem, generic encouragement, separate “build first” directions, or a “go further” label in place of a clear task. Put essential rules and expected actions directly in the numbered problem, in the same ordinary text style. Include the diagrams and recording space children actually need. Keep preparation, timing, hints, proof follow-ups, and teaching commentary in the facilitator guide.

### Design and review from classroom evidence

- **Give each idea enough concrete work.** Use several purposeful, contrasting examples and enough physical space to try them. Before introducing a second obstruction or a new representation, give children time to encounter and use the first. More examples should reveal a distinction, not just repeat a procedure.
- **Make the next action clear.** Say what to build, change, compare, count, draw, or record. Demonstrate unfamiliar recording conventions. A child should not have to infer the task from a title, teaser, or enrichment label.
- **Sequence representations around their purpose.** Let children act, record, and compare before asking them to organize or explain what happened. Introduce a map, code, or ribbon when it helps answer a question they have encountered; give that representation its own concrete work.
- **Let observed experience change the design.** Record what children tried, understood, found unclear, and wanted to continue. Expand ideas that need more examples, resequence instructions that required repeated adult rescue, and remove or redesign elegant questions that did not land. Distinguish observations from hypotheses about why they happened, and distinguish tested material from new revisions.
- **Preserve depth and flexible pacing.** Keep worthwhile explanation and proof opportunities, using objects, drawings, and adult conversation as appropriate. Put discretionary follow-ups such as “How do you know for sure?” in facilitator notes when they would interrupt the printed task. Choose starting points and further questions by prerequisites and observed readiness; finishing every page is not the goal.

## Answer to the organizer's ribbon question

Yes: the ribbon supplies a short parity proof that does not rely on inspecting the diamond in the six-state graph.

Number the four ribbon steps **1, 2, 3, 4**, from its fixed starting edge. Add the positions occupied by the two R steps. For example, `RRLL` has score `1 + 2 = 3`, while `RLRL` has score `1 + 3 = 4`.

Every legal flip swaps an adjacent `RL` with `LR`. Exactly one R therefore moves one position forward or backward. Its position sum changes by **one**, so its parity changes at every flip. Returning to the original tiling returns to the same ribbon and score. The number of flips must be even. This proves every closed walk has even length, and hence there is no odd cycle.

The point to establish beforehand is that the ribbon is determined by the tiling and every legal flip has precisely this adjacent-swap effect. Uniqueness is needed to assign the score consistently; the stronger fact that a ribbon determines the whole tiling is useful for counting the six tilings, but not necessary for this parity argument. The existing [research notes](week-01-research-level-notes.md), section “An elementary complete count and rank statistic, directly on the pictures,” justify the correspondence. The related L-before-R pair count also changes by one; summing R positions is a smaller arithmetic task here.

This is best an optional facilitator explanation after children trace ribbons and compare flipped pairs. It need not be added as a compulsory written proof on the route page.
