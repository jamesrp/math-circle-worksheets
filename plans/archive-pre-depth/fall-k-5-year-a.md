# Fall math circle across K–1, 2–3, and 4–5

Working plan, September 18, 2026. Ten weekly meetings of about one hour. The themes and grades 2–3 activities from the [existing Year A plan][year-a] are retained, with corresponding K–1 and 4–5 versions alongside them. The [companion activity guide](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/fall-k-5-year-a-activities.md) now supplies thirty group activity sequences, exact puzzles, hand-drawn setup instructions, hints, and checked facilitator solutions. Record actual use in the [session log](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/fall-k-5-year-a-use-log.md).

Each week shares a mathematical idea, a launch, and most of its materials. The levels change how children enter the problem and how far they investigate it. All three use physical exploration. Explanations can be spoken, drawn, or demonstrated with objects.

## Choosing an entry point

These labels guide preparation, not placement by age. A child can use picture messages while doing advanced code reasoning, or explore a small game deeply while another child plays on a larger board.

| Starting version | Reading and arithmetic | Reasoning to support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **K–1** | No independent reading or writing required. Match symbols, compare heights, count small collections with support. | Follow one rule at a time; test a choice; show what changed. Use large pieces, visible examples, and short rounds. |
| **2–3** | Most activities need little reading; letter ciphers need alphabet familiarity or a picture alternative. Small counts and subtraction through play. | Coordinate a few constraints, use earlier evidence, and explain a useful move. |
| **4–5** | Letter ciphers benefit from fluent reading, but picture versions remain available. Counting, addition/subtraction, and odd/even reasoning suffice for most tasks. | Compare explanations, distinguish one successful example from a strategy, and investigate uniqueness or impossibility. Formal proof notation is unnecessary. |

The original feedback still guides the whole plan: shapes, ciphers, blocks, games, logic, and billiards were popular. Formal combinatorial counting is optional at every level. The older version should first offer a satisfying puzzle; its general explanation is a further invitation.

## A shared hour

**Current roster:** K, K, 1; 3, 3, 3; and one fifth grader, together in one room with the organizer and one supporting parent. Use the two triplets as working groups. Give the fifth grader a workspace beside the third graders, with access to their conversation and a suitable challenge of their own. Placement remains flexible as we learn what each child enjoys and can do.

The books support shared themes, different depths, independent attempts, and substantial adult help. They do not supply a tested arrangement for these seven children and two adults. The following is our starting proposal; see the [lesson-format source notes][lesson-format] for the evidence and its limits.

### Adult coverage and timing

Start with the supporting parent mainly anchored to KK1, helping with rules, materials, and short rounds. The organizer starts the third graders and fifth grader, then moves between them. Either adult can shift when a group is working comfortably, but keep an agreed next visit so no group has to compete for attention. Both adults should know the launch, a worked example, and one helpful hint before the session.

| Approximate time | Children | Adult attention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0–10** | Explore the week's materials; returning children can try a familiar kind of challenge. | Both adults observe and join in. Notice who needs help handling the materials. |
| **10–15** | One brief shared demonstration of the idea or rules. | Organizer demonstrates; supporting parent helps the youngest follow. Save level-specific extensions for the tables. |
| **15–20** | Begin the starting tasks. | Parent demonstrates a complete K–1 example. Organizer gets 333 started, then gives the fifth grader a personal launch. The fifth grader can start with the common puzzle while waiting. |
| **20–35** | Investigate, play, and compare attempts. KK1 may do several short rounds. | Parent stays mainly with KK1. Organizer alternates between 333 and 5, aiming to return within about five minutes rather than waiting for someone to ask. Leave a concrete next thing to try at each visit. |
| **35–40** | Brief movement/reset, with an optional discovery to show. | Check engagement and adjust the next task; no requirement for all levels to have reached the same point. |
| **40–55** | Continue a good investigation, change a constraint, or try an extension. | Same coverage; reserve some direct discussion or play with the fifth grader. |
| **55–60** | Each group can show one discovery, question, or failed attempt; tidy up. | Help explanations use objects or drawings. The older extension need not become a lesson for everyone. |

These times are flexible. A productive construction or game can run across a boundary; a stalled group needs a change sooner. The important rhythm is trying, talking about an attempt, and trying again.

### Working in threes

- **Blocks, shapes, towers, and billiards:** provide enough pieces and work surfaces for all three children to make attempts, then compare them. A triplet need not share a single construction. Invite each child to show a move or observation before settling on a group answer.
- **Codes:** rotate sender, solver, and checker. In hidden color codes, the checker may see the secret and verify the feedback without revealing it to the solver. For KK1 the parent can handle scoring while children take turns setting and guessing.
- **Take-away and Rook's Move:** preserve two opposing sides, since three-player rules change the mathematics. Two children can play while the third checks legal moves and chooses the next starting position; rotate after every short round, regardless of who wins. If that role is unsatisfying, use one child against a team of two, alternating which teammate moves and rotating the solo role between rounds.

### Including the fifth grader

Start from the shared problem when it is interesting, then offer the deeper version according to readiness. Keep the fifth grader near 333 for shared discoveries and puzzle exchanges, with their own materials when the questions diverge. Independent work should alternate with real mathematical conversation; being the oldest does not mean being left alone or assigned to teach younger children.

For games, explicitly budget the organizer as an opponent. Between short games, the fifth grader can investigate a supplied position, find a response to each possible opponent move, or build a position to challenge the adult on their return. For secret-code games, use a prepared puzzle or have the adult hold the secret and give feedback during visits. Weeks 6–8 need more adult availability than independent construction weeks; when necessary, the other group continues a familiar round while the organizer plays or checks feedback.

A third parent would let each group have an adult, and would be especially useful as a regular opponent and discussion partner for the fifth grader. Until then, try this arrangement and note where children wait for attention. Also note whether the fifth grader enjoys the balance of shared and independent work; another fourth or fifth grader would add peer companionship as well as solving the opponent problem.

## Weekly planning notes

### 1. Tiling: what is possible, and how can one solution change into another?

**Revised September 19, 2026: F01-K-v2 / F01-M-v2 / F01-U-v2.** The central questions are tileability, optimal packing, enumeration, and shortest reconfiguration. Use the organizer's **21st Century Pattern Blocks**, including the purple chevron; no square is needed. Allow free construction before the brief launch: “Can you fill this purple outline with different pieces?”

**Kit and preparation:** use **1× pieces only** for the core tasks. The organizer measured a green edge at approximately one inch and also has many Upscale sets with 1×, 2×, and 3× green, red, blue, and yellow pieces. Print the [Week 1 packet](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-01/README.md) at Actual Size and check its calibration line against a physical 1× edge; the measurement does not resolve 25 mm versus 25.4 mm. Trace the actual 2× and 3× green triangles for exact T2/T3 boundaries if the print fit differs. Gather **26 1× blues** across the sets for simultaneous work; the twelve-rhombus paper sheet is an optional backup. Full preparation, kit sources, timing, and solutions are in the activity guide.

- **K–1:** fill a purple chevron outline with two blues or four greens. Then find the two fillings of a unit yellow-hexagon outline using three blues, keeping the page fixed. Change between them and back. **Hint:** compare which neighboring triangle spaces are paired. **Mathematical direction:** equal-shape replacements, local moves, and reversibility.
- **2–3:** compare a four-cell parallelogram with a side-two equilateral triangle, using blues only. Then minimize uncovered unit-triangle spaces in a side-three triangle, marking gaps with greens. **Hint:** compare up-pointing and down-pointing cells and what each blue covers. **Further:** allow purple chevrons; can they improve the answer? Optionally allow pink right triangles and test the side-two triangle again. **Mathematical direction:** invariants, optimality certificates, reductions between tile sets, and checking a proof's assumptions.
- **4–5:** use eight blues on a hexagon with consecutive side lengths 1,2,2,1,2,2. Transform reference tiling A into F using only three-rhombus hexagon flips. Record states and connect legal one-flip changes; seek a shortest route. **Hint:** compare possible moves from each state. **Further:** find two shortest routes and decide whether an odd number of flips can return to the start. **Mathematical direction:** finite state graphs, exhaustive counting, distances, height functions, and parity.

**Facilitator checks:** each blue covers one up and one down triangle. The side-two triangle has counts 3 and 1, so blue tiling fails; the parallelogram tiles. The side-three triangle has counts 6 and 3, and three blues plus three green fillers attain the minimum of three gaps. Purple is two blues, so adding it cannot repair a blue-impossible region or reduce the minimum fillers. A side-`n` triangle needs exactly `n` fillers, by the row imbalance and an explicit packing. Equal up/down counts are not sufficient for arbitrary regions; the balanced bottleneck counterexample is a later reserve.

Two pink 30–60–90 triangles **do** fill the side-two equilateral triangle along its altitude. Their sides are `1, sqrt(3), 2` in green-edge units. Pink crosses the interiors of the unit triangular cells, so the blue/purple pairing proof no longer applies. Introduce this changed rule only after children understand the first obstruction.

The upper board has six tilings with graph edges A–B, B–C, B–D, C–E, D–E, E–F. Its shortest A-to-F distance is four; levels `0,1,2,2,3,4` certify the lower bound and parity. Random legal flips are an optional adult continuation, with stationary weights proportional to the unequal degrees rather than uniform weights.

**Optional scale extension:** scaling both board and pieces by two preserves the puzzle: a 2× hexagon has two fillings with three 2× blues. Using 1× blues on that same larger hexagon changes the relative scale, giving twelve pieces and twenty tilings. Save this comparison for a later investigation if the main questions hold attention.

**Sources and future use:** the [undergraduate notes](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/week-01-undergraduate-notes.md) connect these questions to coloring invariants, Hall's theorem, counting, and the separate square-grid tromino induction. The [research notes](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/week-01-research-level-notes.md) connect the actual blue-rhombus flips to Thurston, height functions, cube piles, and random tilings. These connections guide the questions; children begin with blocks and drawings. The former P1/P2 disjoint-color tasks remain an **untaught v1 reserve** in the activity guide. They minimized piece count, not area, since their target outlines had fixed area. Record the revised exact instances and any extensions actually used.

### 2. Shape subtraction: what is missing?

**Shared kit and launch:** sticks or removable line-segment pieces on a fixed grid; drawings or overlays for those who want them. “What would we need to put back to make this picture?” Preparation: moderate, mostly reusable picture frames.

- **K–1:** copy a small line picture, remove a piece, and challenge a partner to restore it. Start with the complete picture visible for comparison. **Hint:** match the parts that remain first. **Further:** remove two pieces or offer several candidate missing parts.
- **2–3:** keep the existing combine/subtract/missing-picture investigation, beginning with nonoverlapping strokes. **Hint:** trace the known part on the target. **Further:** test a puzzle containing shared strokes.
- **4–5:** look for two different missing pictures that produce the same combined picture. Investigate whether “add B, then subtract B” always restores A. **Hint:** deliberately let A and B share a stroke. **Further:** state when restoration does work, or design an impossible missing-picture puzzle.

**Facilitator note:** treat a fixed set of allowed segments as pieces. Combining keeps each segment once; subtraction removes specified segments from the combined drawing. Shared endpoints do not count as shared segments. For example, if A has strokes {a, b} and B has {b, c}, the combination is {a, b, c}; subtracting B leaves {a}, not A. Physically lifting an overlay would leave shared strokes on the other sheet, so copy-and-erase or removable single-layer segments should demonstrate subtraction. Sources: the organizer's [shape-operation files][shape-source] and Lowell Handout 11, problem 11.3; ambiguity investigations are our extension.

### 3. Secret alphabets: agree on a key

**Shared kit and launch:** picture/action cards, distinct symbol cards, key strips, and writing tools. “Can you send a message your partner can understand without saying it?” Preparation: moderate; use new keys and messages.

- **K–1:** pair three or four symbols with pictures of actions or objects. Arrange a short symbol message for a partner to perform or reconstruct with picture cards. **Hint:** keep the key visible. **Further:** repeat a symbol in different positions or exchange sender/receiver roles. Decoding is the goal, not memorizing the key.
- **2–3:** create a one-to-one alphabet and exchange short messages. Notice that repeated letters make repeated symbols. **Hint:** mark repeated symbols together. **Further:** supply only part of a key.
- **4–5:** decode a partially known substitution using a short candidate-word list, repeated-letter patterns, and a few known matches. **Hint:** compare where repeats occur, not just word length. **Further:** devise a useful extra clue when several readings remain possible, then make a puzzle for another child or an adult.

**Facilitator note:** decide in advance that each letter has one distinct symbol throughout the message. A candidate word must preserve both repeated and different letters. Missing information can allow several decodings; do not insist on the author's intended answer without sufficient clues. Source: [Rozhkovskaya][elementary], Lesson 2, “Secret code,” especially problems 2.2 and 2.8. The nonreading action key and staged clue design are adaptations.

### 4. Breaking ciphers: find the wheel's setting

**Shared kit and launch:** two aligned movable alphabets or symbol rings and a known plaintext/code pair. “Someone turned the key. Can we work out where it belongs?” Preparation: moderate; prepare wheels and verify messages. The fixed order around each ring must stay visible.

- **K–1:** use four distinct pictures on one ring and four distinct secret symbols on the other. Show one known picture/symbol match; turn the wheel to restore that match, then decode a short picture message. **Hint:** physically pin the known match first. **Further:** change the setting and provide a new clue for a partner.
- **2–3:** keep the shift-cipher investigation with an alphabet wheel and partial clues. **Hint:** try a known letter or likely short word. **Further:** write a message with one useful clue and exchange it.
- **4–5:** solve an unknown shift, then encrypt a message twice with different shifts. **Hint:** track just one letter through both turns. **Further:** find one equivalent shift, find how to undo it, or investigate settings that undo themselves when used twice.

**Facilitator note:** a known input/output pair fixes a rotation when the rings have distinct symbols and fixed order. On a 26-letter alphabet, shifting 3 then 5 equals shifting 8; shifting 13 twice restores the original. Older children can show this with the wheel without modular notation. The K–1 task differs from week 3: children recover a rule setting, rather than follow a complete fixed key. Source: [Rozhkovskaya][elementary], Lesson 2, problems 2.3–2.7; the four-picture wheel and composition questions are adaptations.

### 5. Skyscrapers: what can you see?

**Shared kit and launch:** snap-cube towers, small square grids, and clue cards with numerals or dots. “Which buildings can this little person see?” Children check from table height. Preparation: moderate; reuse towers and grids.

- **K–1:** arrange heights 1, 2, 3 in a single row. Make one tower visible, then all three, then two. **Hint:** put a small figure at the viewing end. **Further:** try a 2×2 city with heights 1 and 2 appearing once in every row and column; demonstrate this new rule separately.
- **2–3:** keep the 3×3 puzzles, with each height once per row and column and edge visibility clues. **Hint:** start with a clue that strongly limits the tallest tower. **Further:** give clues for a completed city.
- **4–5:** solve a 4×4 puzzle, explaining placements. Then start from a solved city and remove one clue at a time. **Hint:** look for a second arrangement satisfying everything that remains. **Further:** repair an ambiguous puzzle with an additional clue. A difficult 3×3 can be equally suitable.

**Facilitator note:** the clue counts visible towers, not cubes or the first tower's height. A clue of 1 places the tallest tower first; a clue equal to the row length requires increasing heights. A full 2×2, 3×3, or 4×4 city uses 6, 18, or 40 cubes respectively. One working arrangement proves existence, not uniqueness; a child-made puzzle may correctly have several solutions. Source: JRMF [Skyscrapers Teaching Guide][skyscrapers], pp. 1–2, 5–14. Clue removal is our extension.

### 6. Hidden color codes: make an informative guess

**Shared kit and launch:** two distinguishable counter types, a screen, rows of spaces, and feedback counters. “This guess has one correct place. What could we try next?” Repeated colors are allowed. Preparation: low. Feedback gives only the total correct positions, never which ones.

- **K–1:** use two positions. First practice scoring a visible example together; then hide the code. Record feedback with zero, one, or two counters. **Hint:** compare the new guess to the previous one. **Further:** change just one counter and notice whether the score rises or falls. No guess limit at first.
- **2–3:** keep three positions and visible guess history. **Hint:** change one position while leaving the others fixed. **Further:** explain why the feedback rules out a possible secret.
- **4–5:** use four positions and seek a reliable strategy. Start with free play before offering a five-guess challenge. **Hint:** compare guesses that differ in exactly one position. **Further:** explain why a method works regardless of the hidden code, then try more positions.

**Facilitator note:** a guaranteed method for four positions is an all-red baseline, three separate single-position flips compared to that baseline, then a final guess. Each score change identifies one position; the baseline total determines the fourth. This guarantees success within five guesses, without claiming five is optimal. An adult should check feedback until children can score reliably. Source: JRMF [Crack the Code][code], pp. 2–4; the two-position start is our adaptation.

### 7. Take-away games: leave a difficult position

**Shared kit and launch:** counters and separate move cards showing the permitted amounts as dots. “Can you leave your partner a pile they won't like?” Taking the last counter wins; a move cannot exceed the pile. Preparation: low.

- **K–1:** take 1 or 2 from piles of roughly 3–8. Play several quick rounds with both starting roles. **Hint:** explore what happens with exactly three left. **Further:** find a way to leave three, then investigate six. Children can explain by showing the opponent's two possible responses.
- **2–3:** keep the 1, 3, or 4 game, using small piles before larger ones. **Hint:** begin where the whole game can be played out. **Further:** mark useful positions with colored counters.
- **4–5:** use the same 1, 3, or 4 game, build a winning/losing-position chart, and explain its repeating pattern. **Hint:** a good position has a move to a bad one; from a bad position every move is good for the opponent. **Further:** change one permitted move and test which arguments survive.

**Facilitator note:** for 1-or-2, losing pile sizes are multiples of 3. For 1, 3, or 4 they are 0 or 2 beyond a multiple of 7: 0, 2, 7, 9, 14, 16, and so on. To justify the latter pattern, check that no allowed move connects two proposed losing positions and that every other position has a legal move into one. Zero represents the next player having already lost. Keep strategies off the children's launch cards. Source: JRMF [Countdown Teaching Guide][countdown], pp. 1, 4–5, and p. 8, challenge 4, which explicitly presents the 1, 3, or 4 game. The smaller-pile progression here is our adaptation.

### 8. Rook's Move: put strategy on a board

**Shared kit and launch:** small grids, one token per game, a goal star, and direction arrows. “Where would you like the token to be when your turn starts?” Move right or down any positive number of squares; whoever reaches the goal wins. Preparation: low; chess knowledge is unnecessary.

- **K–1:** begin near the goal on a 2×3 or 3×3 board. Spot places from which the goal can be reached immediately, then places that give the other player that opportunity. **Hint:** point out all legal destinations from one square. **Further:** try the same start with first and second player exchanged.
- **2–3:** retain the 4×4 investigation, comparing starts and leaving difficult positions. **Hint:** work outward from the goal. **Further:** explain one winning response to a partner.
- **4–5:** explain the strategy on a larger or rectangular board using remaining horizontal and vertical distances. **Hint:** compare positions with equal distances to the goal. **Further:** change to King's Move—one step right, down, or diagonally down-right—and investigate the new losing squares.

**Facilitator note:** in Rook's Move, equal remaining distances are losing positions: any move leaves that set, and the opponent can restore equality. Off it, shorten the longer distance to reach equality. In King's Move the losing squares have both distances even. Use these to check children's arguments; let children discover them through games. Source: JRMF [Rook's Move Activity Guide][rook], pp. 3–8; the small-board K–1 sequence is our adaptation.

### 9. Billiards: aim for a corner

**Shared kit and launch:** large square-grid rectangles, tokens, straightedges, and diagonal path pieces or erasable pens. “Which corner will the ball reach, and can we make it reach a different one?” Use a pretend ball launched diagonally up-right from the bottom-left corner; stop at the first corner reached. Preparation: moderate for K–1 path pieces, otherwise low.

- **K–1:** build or follow a short path on a prepared table. At a wall, choose between two proposed continuations and test the bounce rule with a demonstration. **Hint:** change the horizontal direction at a vertical wall and the vertical direction at a horizontal wall. **Further:** match short table examples to their ending corners. Independent ruler work is unnecessary.
- **2–3:** retain the target-table design challenge: choose or draw rectangles that hit a specified corner, then trade and test designs. **Hint:** compare a tall table with its wide version. **Further:** seek another table with the same endpoint.
- **4–5:** design several tables for the same target, then explain what happens when both dimensions are doubled or exchanged. **Hint:** compare tables that are scaled copies before comparing unrelated sizes. **Further:** seek an endpoint rule from the side lengths; use reflected table copies if a child wants an explanation of that rule.

**Facilitator note:** use whole-number widths and heights and exact 45-degree paths. Width 3/height 6 ends top-left; width 6/height 3 ends bottom-right; width 3/height 9 ends top-right. For a deeper check, reduce width and height by their greatest common divisor: odd/odd ends top-right, even/odd bottom-right, odd/even top-left. A straight line through reflected table copies explains this. The formula is a facilitator reference, not an expected K–5 outcome. Sources: [JRMF Billiards Geometry][billiards], PDF p. 2, and Lowell Handouts 10–11. The youngest scaffolds and leveled design sequence are adaptations. Unfolding remains outside the 2–3 fall core.

### 10. Puzzle workshop: make something worth solving

**Shared kit and launch:** two or three favorite kits from the quarter, blank cards, and a way to record solutions. “Can you make a challenge someone else can solve and check?” Preparation: low; this can also give an unfinished investigation more time.

- **K–1:** choose a concrete starter: remove a few pieces from a picture, write an action-code message, or set up a short game. Demonstrate the rules to a partner. **Hint:** keep a completed example or photograph. **Further:** change one feature and test the new puzzle.
- **2–3:** retain the shape, code, or game workshop. Exchange puzzles, test the directions, and revise. **Hint:** ask the solver to show where the instructions became unclear. **Further:** provide a hint that helps without giving the answer.
- **4–5:** design a challenge with a reason behind it: a forced game move, a code with sufficient clues, or a shape puzzle with two different solutions. **Hint:** start from a solution and work backward. **Further:** justify a claim of uniqueness, impossibility, or a guaranteed strategy; revise that claim if testing finds a counterexample.

**Facilitator note:** a successful contribution may be a clearer rule or a discovered second solution. Keep a solution or explanation with every archived challenge; distinguish conjectures from checked claims. Source inspiration: [Rozhkovskaya][elementary], M.4, “Make your own problem.” These workshop choices are our proposal.

## Preparation and future reuse

One shared kit per theme should do most of the work. Vary the outlines, number of code positions, clue sets, starting piles, and board sizes through small task cards. Use visible icons and dot counts where possible. For concurrent groups, label the take-away move cards clearly so 1-or-2 and 1/3/4 games do not get mixed up.

Keep a record of the actual challenges each cohort encounters, not only the theme. Returning children should meet new diagrams, messages, clue sets, or questions when they move to another level. An older version need not repeat the youngest starter if a child already knows it. The saved Lowell materials contain only part of last year's history, so check additional prior use before choosing the final instances.

The common-theme approach is supported by [Math Circle by the Bay][bay], preface pp. viii–x (PDF pp. 9–11), which describes using themes across age groups with different pace and depth, manipulatives, and repeated practice explaining ideas. The specific level assignments here are our proposed adaptations, to be adjusted after observing the children.
