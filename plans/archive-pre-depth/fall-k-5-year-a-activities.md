# Fall Year A: activities for all three groups

Prepared September 18, 2026. Ten meetings, with thirty group activity sequences, for the roster **K, K, 1 / 3, 3, 3 / 5** and two adults. Companion to the [high-level fall plan][fall] and [lesson-format source notes][format]. Grade bands indicate entry points, not placement requirements.

This guide supplies the actual starting puzzles, rules, hints, and answers. The hand-drawn boards and cards described below are sufficient to run it; no apps, purchases, or elaborate worksheets are required. These are proposed activities, not reports of sessions already taught. Exact examples are new constructions unless identified otherwise; familiar ideas deliberately return with new questions. The saved Lowell handouts do not establish everything children encountered last year.

## How to use the guide

Each week has **one main investigation per group**, followed by a change of question or constraint. Do the entry task and allow sustained play; the extensions are a reserve, not a checklist. A child who spends the whole investigation finding and explaining two constructions has had a successful session.

Use the existing hour: **0–10** handle materials and explore; **10–15** shared launch; **15–35** group entry and main task; **35–40** movement/reset; **40–55** continue or extend; **55–60** show one discovery and tidy up. The timing suggestions in each group card refer to those two investigation blocks. Introduce only the first task at first.

The supporting parent stays primarily with KK1. The organizer starts 333, then launches the fifth grader's task, and returns to each about every five minutes. Keep the fifth grader beside 333 and offer genuine conversation and puzzle exchanges. In weeks 6–8, reserve two short adult play periods within the investigation blocks; the fifth grader also has a supplied investigation to pursue between visits. Independent tasks support that relationship rather than replacing it.

Give each child construction materials. For exchanges in a triplet, rotate maker, solver, and checker after a short puzzle. For competitive games, use **two opposing sides**: two players and a rotating referee who chooses the next start, or one player against a team of two with alternating movers. Rotate regardless of who won. Do not turn the games into three-player games.

**Readiness:** all instructions may be spoken. K–1 needs matching and small counts; 2–3 adds coordination of constraints; 4–5 adds explanations about all possibilities. Week 3–4 letter tasks need alphabet familiarity, but the picture versions support the same reasoning without independent reading. No multiplication tables, fractions, or formal notation are prerequisites. Keep facilitator solutions out of view until children have tried.

### Reusable supplies

Paper, index cards, pencils, markers, sleeves or scrap paper for repeat attempts; pattern blocks; two distinguishable counter types; snap cubes; craft sticks; a few tokens; graph paper and rulers. Symbols or stickers can distinguish counter types as well as color. Use folders as code screens. No child needs a personal notebook or to copy a table before starting.

Allow roughly **20–30 minutes of preparation for weeks 1–5**, mostly making reusable mats/cards, and **10–15 minutes for weeks 6–10** once the kit exists. These are planning estimates, not measured times. First-time wheel making may take longer; sliding alphabet strips are an equivalent low-prep substitute. Each week lists the minimum setup.

### K–1 — Fair trades and a tiny flip · F01-K-v2

**Prerequisites:** match boundaries and turn pieces; no independent reading, writing, or arithmetic. Counting up to four is optional. Reasoning: preserve a boundary while changing its interior.

**First 20 minutes:** fill the purple outline, then trade its piece for two blues and for four greens. Rebuild one version alongside another or place the original purple over the proposed replacement to check. Let children run an exchange shop and explain what makes a trade fair.

**Next 15 minutes:** cover the yellow-hexagon outline using exactly three blues. Trace the joins, lift the blues, and look for another filling. Keep the page in the same orientation. Once both fillings are found, change from one to the other and back: this three-rhombus replacement is one **flip**. A child may continue the exchange shop if the hexagon is not yet useful.

**Ask:** “What stayed the same?” “Where did the joins move?” “Can we undo your move?” **Hint:** start the second filling by changing which two little triangle spaces are paired near the marked edge. **Extension:** can someone who sees only the outside tell which filling is underneath?

**Facilitator check:** purple = two blues = four greens. The unit hexagon has exactly two three-blue fillings; rotating the page is not a new recorded construction. To see why there are only two, choose a corner cell: either of its two neighboring cells can be its partner, and the remaining pairs are then forced. The two possibilities are exchanged by the reversible three-rhombus flip used in the upper investigation.

### 2–3 — Can it fit? How few gaps? · F01-M-v2

**Prerequisites:** count through nine with support and distinguish triangle orientations; all prompts may be spoken. Reasoning: test a conjecture, find an invariant, and combine an example with a reason it is optimal. No multiplication or area formulas are required.

**First 20 minutes:** try to fill the four-cell parallelogram and T2 using **blue rhombi only**. Piece edges must follow printed triangle edges; no overlap, overhang, or stacking. Both regions have room for two blues by area. Does that guarantee both work? Let children experiment before inviting them to mark up-pointing and down-pointing triangle spaces differently.

**Next 15 minutes:** on T3, cover as much as possible with blues and count the **uncovered unit-triangle spaces**. Green triangles may mark those gaps. Try to leave fewer gaps, then explain why the best construction cannot be improved. If there is time, allow purple chevrons and ask whether this can reduce the number of gaps. Keep the objective as gap count or green fillers; total block count is a different question.

**Ask:** “What two kinds of spaces does each blue cover?” “Can every up-pointing space find a down-pointing partner?” “What would change if we split every purple into two blues?” **Hint:** first put two greens over one blue and turn the pair; then compare the orientations on the mat. **Further:** draw a side-four triangle and predict its minimum gaps before building. Save the balanced six-cell bottleneck from the undergraduate notes for a later session or a child who wants a new challenge; it is not part of the required hour.

**Facilitator check:** the parallelogram tiles with two blues. T2 has three up cells and one down cell, while each blue covers one of each; it cannot be tiled by blues. T3 has six up and three down cells, so at most three blues fit and at least **three gaps** remain. This is achievable: in a side-three triangle with axial vertices `(0,0),(3,0),(0,3)`, pair `U(i,j)` with `D(i,j)` for `(i,j)=(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)`, leaving `U(2,0),U(1,1),U(0,2)`. Here `U(i,j)` has vertices `(i,j),(i+1,j),(i,j+1)` and `D(i,j)` has `(i+1,j+1),(i+1,j),(i,j+1)`; the basis directions are one green edge right and one green edge up-right. The printed answer diagram gives the same construction without coordinates.

Each purple is two blues, so any blue/purple tiling can be converted to all blue: purple cannot rescue a blue-impossible region or reduce T3 below three green fillers. For a side-`n` triangle, each row has one extra up cell. Thus at least `n` fillers are necessary; pair `U(i,j)` with `D(i,j)` whenever `i+j<=n-2` to attain exactly `n`.

**Different objective:** on T3, purple reduces the minimum total block count from six to **five**, while the minimum greens remains three. Use purple on `U(1,0),D(1,0),U(1,1),D(0,1)`, blue on `U(0,0),D(0,0)`, and greens on `U(0,1),U(0,2),U(2,0)`. The facilitator guide illustrates it. Four total blocks cannot suffice: at least three would be greens, and one additional piece covers at most four more triangle units, totaling at most seven of the required nine.

**Optional change of assumptions:** now allow the pink right triangles. **Two pinks fill T2**, meeting along its altitude. Pink has side lengths `1, sqrt(3), 2` in green-edge units. The long side of each pink follows one length-two outer side; their long legs share the altitude, and their short legs divide the base. The original up/down proof required every permitted piece to be a union of whole paired triangular cells. Pink cuts across those cells, so that proof no longer applies. This is a useful changed-rule experiment, not a contradiction. The designer's *Five Fun Things*, “Compare Side Lengths,” supplies the short-side/hypotenuse relationship; the packet's kit notes document the geometry.

### 4–5 — Six tilings and the shortest route · F01-U-v2

**Prerequisites:** match a tiling diagram, count small sets, and reason about odd/even numbers. No independent technical reading, algebra, or probability is needed. Reasoning: explore a state space, distinguish an observed list from an exhaustive one, prove a shortest route, and find a parity obstruction.

**First 20 minutes:** give the child eight blues and H122. Let them find a filling before showing tiling A. Demonstrate a flip using three blues in a separate unit hexagon. Then ask them to change A into F using only these flips and record the intermediate tilings. Keep the marked side fixed: tilings that occupy different internal positions count separately; the blue pieces themselves are indistinguishable.

**Next 15 minutes:** collect tilings on cards, link two cards when one legal flip connects them, and improve the A-to-F route. Ask whether every possible tiling has been found and how they could check. Offer the checked six-state gallery when needed so the child can investigate distances even if exhaustive enumeration is unfinished. Do not equate finding six examples with proving there are only six.

**Ask:** “Which three pieces could move together?” “Could two different first choices reach the same later tiling?” “How do you know three moves cannot reach F?” “Can you return in three flips?” **Hint:** label the states and draw a line for each verified one-flip change. **Extension:** find two different shortest A-to-F routes, or find the distance from C to D. Probability and larger-board enumeration are reserves for later discussion.

**Facilitator check:** H122 has sixteen unit-triangle cells and exactly six tilings, A–F. Their complete flip graph has edges **A–B, B–C, B–D, C–E, D–E, E–F**. Exhaustive matching enumeration and all legal flips are checked in the research notes and verification script. The shortest A-to-F routes are **A–B–C–E–F** and **A–B–D–E–F**, both four flips. Assign levels `0,1,2,2,3,4` to A–F; every edge changes level by one, so four is a lower bound as well as an attained route. Every flip switches level parity, so every return has even length. C-to-D takes two flips, via B or E. The level argument is justified by the complete verified graph, not merely the subset a child happened to draw.

**Adult continuation:** these levels encode cube counts in a two-by-two-by-one box; the flips add or remove a cube. Randomly choosing among currently legal flips produces long-run state weights proportional to degree, `(1,3,2,2,3,1)/12`, not uniform weights. Mean first positive returns to A–F are `12,4,6,6,4,12` flips. This direct connection to the cup-swapping example is optional adult material; the core hour needs neither probability nor the general theorem. The research notes specify the random rule and explain the different uniform-sampling rule.

**Optional scale investigation:** scaling both a board and every permitted piece by two preserves its tilings and flip graph. Use three 2× blues on the outline of a 2× yellow hexagon: there are still two fillings. Keep that larger hexagon but switch to 1× blues and the question changes: it takes twelve pieces and has twenty tilings. This is a later extension about what similarity preserves and what changing relative scale changes, not an extra required task.

**Adult route / closing:** the parent stays with KK1 while the organizer starts 333 and then returns regularly to the fifth grader's route and graph. At closing, connect one fair trade, one reason a tiling fails, and one shortest-route argument. Ask for one discovery, not completion of every reserve question.

### 4–5 — Can you beat five pieces? · F01-U

**Prerequisites:** the color rule, addition of small counts, and willingness to compare a construction with a proposed lower bound. No area formula required.

## Week 2: Missing strokes

**Idea:** combining line pictures records which segments are present; shared segments can make the missing picture ambiguous and make subtraction lose information.

**Prepare:** draw several identical six-dot mats per child, sized for seven equal craft sticks. Label one adult reference mat as follows; children's mats can have dots without letters.

```text
A-----B-----C
|     |     |
D-----E-----F
```

Call the complete two-room window **W**. Its allowed sticks are AB, BC, AD, BE, CF, DE, EF. The left square **P** is AB, AD, BE, DE; the right square **Q** is BC, BE, CF, EF. Prepare small reference drawings of W, P, and Q, plus blank mats. Keep pictures aligned to these fixed dots; moving or rotating a picture changes it.

**Rule:** combining pictures puts a stick wherever either picture has one, **only once**. Subtracting takes away the specified sticks. Shared endpoints are fine; a shared whole segment is counted once. Do not stack two sticks at BE: lifting one transparent sheet would model a different operation.

**Shared launch:** build W, hide/remove one stick, and invite someone to restore the window using a completed reference. “What part is missing? How did you know?”

### K–1 — Window repair crew · F02-K

**Prerequisites:** compare two pictures; no reading, counting, or letter labels. Reasoning: identify a missing part while keeping the rest fixed.

**First 20 minutes:** each child builds W from its reference. The adult removes AD, then in a fresh round BE, then both BC and EF. Restore the picture each time. Next children take turns making a missing-piece puzzle for a neighbor, with the whole reference still visible.

**Next 15 minutes:** reveal a whole picture, cover it briefly, remove one piece, and uncover the incomplete version. Keep the reference available whenever memory obscures the mathematical comparison. A child who enjoys inventing can create a new picture with these seven positions.

**Ask:** “Which parts already match?” “Can you point to a gap?” **Hint:** lay the reference beside the mat with matching corners aligned. **Extension:** offer three loose candidate pieces/positions and explain which repair works, or remove two pieces.

**Facilitator check:** the three adult rounds require AD, BE, and {BC, EF}, respectively. This is spatial comparison, not a test of remembering a hidden drawing.

### 2–3 — Add a room, erase a room · F02-M

**Prerequisites:** copy a line picture and follow a one-layer combining rule; no numerical arithmetic. Reasoning: coordinate an operation with the visible result.

**First 20 minutes:** build P and Q on separate mats. Combine them on a third mat. Then start again with W and subtract Q. Compare what remains with P. Also try a nonoverlapping example: combine {AB, AD, DE} with Q, then subtract Q.

**Next 15 minutes:** give **P + mystery picture = W**. Find one mystery picture, then look for another. Children trade their mystery drawings and check them by physically combining. They need not find every possibility.

**Ask:** “What happened to the middle wall?” “Does your mystery really need that stick?” **Hint:** first place every stick missing from P; only afterward consider sticks already in P. **Extension:** make a puzzle with two verified answers and let the partner discover the second.

**Facilitator check:** P combined with Q is W. W minus Q is {AB, AD, DE}, not P, because BE was removed. The nonoverlapping example restores its starting picture. Both {BC, CF, EF} and Q solve P + mystery = W. Extra sticks from P are optional, not wrong.

### 4–5 — The undo machine fails · F02-U

**Prerequisites:** distinguish coincident segments from merely touching ones. Reasoning: give a counterexample, characterize possible solutions, and explain a condition for restoration.

**First 20 minutes:** test the claim “Combine any picture with another, then subtract the second, and you get the first back.” Start with P and Q. Find one pair for which the claim works and one for which it fails; explain the difference with sticks.

**Next 15 minutes:** investigate P + mystery = W. Sort the sticks into “must be in the mystery,” “may be in it,” and “cannot be in it.” Then design an impossible version by putting a stroke in the known picture that does not occur in the target. For example, add diagonal AE to P while keeping target W; use a drawn diagonal if the sticks are too short.

**Ask:** “Can adding remove an unwanted line?” “What information did the combined drawing forget?” **Hint:** focus on one segment belonging to both inputs. **Extension:** find all mystery pictures by choosing independently which optional sticks to include; counting them is optional.

**Facilitator check:** restoration works exactly when the original two pictures have no segment in common. For P + mystery = W, BC, CF, EF are required; AB, AD, BE, DE are optional; anything outside W is forbidden. Thus there are 16 solutions, although two well-explained examples suffice. The added diagonal makes the equation impossible because combining retains it. These statements use the fixed finite segment convention above, not arbitrary freehand curves.

**Adult route / closing:** leave the fifth grader sorting sticks while visiting 333, then return to challenge their restoration rule. Close with a pair of different mystery pictures, or a demonstration of the lost middle wall.

**Sources and returners:** the organizer's [shape-operation source][shapes] and Lowell Handout 11, problem 11.3. The window, fixed-segment convention, and ambiguity tasks are adaptations. They change the drawings and questions from the saved red-square sheets.

## Week 3: Secret alphabets

**Idea:** an agreed one-to-one correspondence makes messages recoverable; repeated symbols preserve repeated-letter patterns, and clues eliminate possibilities.

**Prepare:** small picture/action cards, distinct symbol cards, blank key strips, and the word bank below. An adult may read every candidate word aloud. A usable alphabet needs only the letters in the chosen message; children do not need to design 26 symbols before sending anything.

**Shared launch:** show a key with circle = clap and triangle = hands up. Display circle–triangle–circle and let everyone interpret it. “How could someone else read our message?”

### K–1 — Silent action messages · F03-K

**Prerequisites:** match four distinct symbols to pictures; no reading or counting. Actions can all be done seated.

**First 20 minutes:** use **circle = clap, triangle = hands up, square = touch head, star = hands on knees**, with drawings on the key. Decode circle–square–circle, then triangle–star–square. Each child makes a three-card message for a neighbor to perform. Rotate sender, performer, and checker; the key stays visible.

**Next 15 minutes:** send a four-card message containing a repeated action. The receiver reconstructs the action sequence with picture cards before performing it. Let children change the key, but have sender and receiver agree on the new version first.

**Ask:** “Does the same symbol mean the same thing both times?” “Where did the two messages become different?” **Hint:** decode one symbol at a time with a finger under the matching key entry. **Extension:** deliberately give two actions the same symbol and ask what information a receiver loses.

**Facilitator check:** the first prepared message is clap, touch head, clap; the second is hands up, hands on knees, touch head. With a fixed one-to-one key the sequence is unambiguous. A key that changes halfway through without notice is a communication error, not a failure by the reader.

### 2–3 — A tiny alphabet that really works · F03-M

**Prerequisites:** identify the letters in a short message, with a reading partner if needed; no arithmetic. Reasoning: use a mapping consistently in both directions.

**First 20 minutes:** give this small starter key: **A = triangle, E = circle, M = square, R = star, T = plus**. Decode:

```text
square circle circle plus / square circle / triangle plus / plus star circle circle
```

Then each child chooses a short message and makes an original key for its letters. Keep spaces between words. Exchange message and key; the checker compares the decoded message with the sender's original.

**Next 15 minutes:** repair a message with one deliberately incorrect symbol, or exchange a message with one key entry omitted. Permit several answers if the missing information allows them. Offer “MEET ME,” “AT A TREE,” or a child's name as short starters.

**Ask:** “Which symbols must repeat?” “Could two different letters share one symbol?” **Hint:** write the intended message first and match each occurrence of a letter to the same key entry. **Extension:** withhold a key entry but add a picture or candidate-word list that makes it recoverable.

**Facilitator check:** the prepared message is **MEET ME AT TREE**. This intentionally limited alphabet is sufficient for that message. In a one-to-one alphabet, different letters must have different symbols as well as matching letters having the same symbol. A few decoded letters do not determine unused parts of the alphabet.

### 4–5 — Ask for the clue that matters · F03-U

**Prerequisites:** compare word lengths and repeated positions; an adult may read the words. Reasoning: eliminate possibilities without guessing the author's intention.

**First 20 minutes:** show cipher **triangle circle square square star** and candidate words **BERRY, MERRY, FERRY, HAPPY, ROBOT, LEVEL**. The key is a one-to-one substitution, with no alphabet-shift rule assumed. Sort candidates into possible/impossible and explain. Then reveal **circle = E**. With the adult, discuss which *single symbol's letter* would be the most useful next clue. Reveal triangle = B only after the child asks or explains a useful request.

**Next 15 minutes:** use the recovered letters to read **triangle circle circle**. Then create a small candidate-word puzzle for the adult or a third grader, including at least two initially plausible candidates and one clue that separates them. If writing a word bank is a barrier, reuse the six words with a new symbol key.

**Ask:** “Which positions repeat?” “Would learning square = R help now?” **Hint:** mark equal positions with matching counters, then compare words position by position. **Extension:** deliberately make an ambiguous puzzle and show two complete decodings.

**Facilitator check:** initially BERRY, MERRY, FERRY, HAPPY fit the pattern; ROBOT and LEVEL do not. Circle = E eliminates HAPPY. The three remaining possibilities differ only at the first letter, so asking for triangle's value resolves them. Square = R or star = Y would not. Triangle = B selects **BERRY**; the new word is **BEE**. The message's used symbols are now known, not the whole alphabet.

**Adult route / closing:** give the fifth grader the complete candidate bank at launch and keep the clue cards face down; the discussion about which clue to request is the planned adult exchange. Close with a message read by its recipient, or a clue that changed what was possible.

**Sources and returners:** Rozhkovskaya, *Math Circles for Elementary School Students*, Lesson 2, problems 2.2 and 2.8 and “At the lesson,” item 2 (`OEBPS/part0012.xhtml`). Our symbols, messages, word bank, and action tasks are new. The book's examples involve shifts; the unrestricted substitution in this week is a deliberate adaptation. Save shifts for week 4 so the two weeks ask different questions.

## Week 4: Turn the key

**Idea:** a cyclic shift is a rule determined by one setting; composing and undoing rotations has a predictable effect.

**Prepare:** one movable alphabet wheel or pair of alphabet strips per working child, plus a four-picture version for KK1. For a wheel, both rings run in the same clockwise order; label the outer ring **message**, the inner ring **code**. For strips, repeat the alphabet so wrapping from Z to A remains visible. The adult defines **+k** to mean move k places forward in A–Z when encoding; decoding moves backward. Use that convention throughout.

**Shared launch:** align A with B, encode one letter, then turn the inner ring. “The symbols are still here, but the setting changed. What clue would help us recover it?” Show the four-picture analogue at the youngest table.

### K–1 — Repair the picture wheel · F04-K

**Prerequisites:** match pictures; follow a fixed order. No reading, letter knowledge, or arithmetic.

**First 20 minutes:** the message ring, clockwise, is **sun, fish, tree, moon**. The code ring, clockwise, is **triangle, circle, square, star**. Set the wheel secretly, then give the clue **fish matches star**. Children align that pair and decode **square–star–triangle–circle**, laying out picture cards as they go.

**Next 15 minutes:** make a new setting and give one known picture/code match to a partner. Keep the order of both rings fixed; change only their relative rotation. After several exchanges, try decoding with no clue and demonstrate two possible readings.

**Ask:** “Once fish matches star, can tree match anything we like?” **Hint:** hold the known pair in place and inspect its neighbor. **Extension:** offer a second match to check the setting; invent a second match that contradicts the first.

**Facilitator check:** fish–star forces **sun–square, fish–star, tree–triangle, moon–circle**. The prepared message reads **sun, fish, tree, moon**. One known match determines all four pairings because the order is fixed. Without any clue, four settings are possible.

### 2–3 — Open the two envelopes · F04-M

**Prerequisites:** recognize A–Z or use a partner; move forward/back along a displayed alphabet. Reasoning: infer and apply a common rule.

**First 20 minutes:** offer envelope C1: **JR WR WUHH**, with the clue **message A becomes code D**. Have a child decode and another check by encoding the answer again. Then offer C2: **JMRH XLI VIH FSB**, with the clue **message Z becomes code D**. Use the wheel to handle the wraparound.

**Next 15 minutes:** children choose a new setting, write a short message, and include one correct letter match. Exchange and verify. Keep the earlier action/picture task available if reading is taking over the session.

**Ask:** “Does this setting work for every letter?” “How do we cross Z?” **Hint:** align the known pair and leave the wheel fixed while reading the whole message. **Extension:** remove the letter clue from C1 but say that its first word is GO; use that to find the setting, then check the rest.

**Facilitator check:** C1 is **GO TO TREE**, shift +3. C2 is **FIND THE RED BOX**, shift +4; Z +4 wraps to D. Messages are decoding puzzles, not instructions requiring an actual red box or outdoor movement.

### 4–5 — Two locks, one turn? · F04-U

**Prerequisites:** use the alphabet wheel; addition/subtraction up to 26 with support. Reasoning: compose rules and explain why one example represents every starting letter.

**First 20 minutes:** decode **IYPUN AOL ISBL ISVJR**, told only that the second word is THE. Recover the shift and check all three letters of the clue, then read the rest. Next encode a short word with +7 and encode that result again with +5. Compare with one turn from the original word.

**Next 15 minutes:** find a single turn undoing the two encryptions. Then seek a nonzero turn that undoes itself when applied twice. Test with a word containing letters near Z as well as A. Explain using the wheel, not just successful sample words.

**Ask:** “What happens to any starting point after seven steps and then five?” “Does wrapping change the total turn?” **Hint:** follow one counter around the ring, then place a second counter elsewhere and repeat the same moves. **Extension:** compare reversing the order of the two shifts; distinguish the identity setting from a nontrivial self-undoing setting.

**Facilitator check:** the message is **BRING THE BLUE BLOCK**, shift +7. Then +5 gives a single +12; +14 reverses +12 because the total is 26. The only nonzero self-undoing shift among 1–25 is **13**: two equal shifts must make exactly one full 26-step revolution. Zero also undoes itself. All shifts commute, although arbitrary substitution rules need not; there is no need to introduce that later topic now.

**Adult route / closing:** the fifth grader has a physical wheel to investigate between visits; return to hear the explanation for every starting letter. Close with a demonstration of finding one wheel setting from one match.

**Sources:** Rozhkovskaya, Lesson 2, problems 2.3–2.7 (`part0012.xhtml`), adapted with new messages. Picture wheels and composition tasks are our extensions. We include the zero/identity setting when discussing all settings; the book counts only nonidentity codes in problem 2.5.

## Week 5: Tower cities

**Idea:** a local viewpoint gives information about a hidden arrangement; several constraints can force a solution, while removing one may allow alternatives.

**Prepare:** snap-cube towers and grids with cells wider than a tower. Each youngest child needs heights 1, 2, 3 for row tasks, or two each of heights 1 and 2 for a 2×2 city: six cubes either way. Each third grader needs three each of heights 1, 2, 3: 18 cubes. The fifth grader needs four each of heights 1, 2, 3, 4: 40 cubes. **112 cubes** support all seven simultaneous constructions; allow a few more for demonstrating. Tower-height numeral cards can replace cubes for a second recorded solution, but begin physically.

**Shared launch:** let children build first, then show heights 2, 1, 3 in a row. Put a little figure at the left end, lower your eyes, and ask which towers it can see. For visibility, imagine each tower completely hides shorter ones directly behind it. Clues count visible **towers**, not cubes.

For city puzzles, each row and each column must contain each height exactly once. Demonstrate this separately from the visibility rule. All puzzle data below are **original instances** using JRMF's rules. A dash means no clue, not zero. Top/bottom lists run left to right; left/right lists run top to bottom.

### K–1 — Hide-and-seek skyline · F05-K

**Prerequisites:** compare heights, count to three with support; no reading. Reasoning: distinguish a viewpoint from the whole arrangement.

**First 20 minutes:** freely arrange heights 1, 2, 3 in a row, then make a skyline showing all three from the left; next show just one; then exactly two. Check by pointing to each visible tower in order. Switch ends without moving the towers and notice the change.

**Next 15 minutes:** try S0. First make any legal 2×2 city with heights 1 and 2 once in each row and column; then add the left clue of two on its top row. If the two city rules are too much, keep making row challenges for each other.

**Ask:** “What can the little person see after this tall tower?” **Hint:** touch the tallest tower seen so far as you move along the row. **Extension:** make a three-tower row showing two towers from each end.

**Facilitator check:** 123 shows three from the left; 312 or 321 shows one; 213 shows two. **132** shows two from each end. S0 is top row 12, bottom row 21. Do not imply that a visibility clue usually determines an entire row: several arrangements can give the same count.

### 2–3 — Build a city from two clues · F05-M

**Prerequisites:** compare heights and count 1–3; no independent reading. Reasoning: coordinate row, column, and visibility restrictions.

**First 20 minutes:** build any legal blank 3×3 city. Check every row and column together, then solve S1 on a fresh grid. Let each child make an attempt; compare reasoning rather than dividing a single puzzle into turns.

**Next 15 minutes:** solve S2, or give one another visibility clues for a completed city. A maker keeps the completed city on a small answer sketch while the solver starts from a blank board. The solver may find another valid city; check it against every clue.

**Ask:** “What must a clue of three look like?” “Which heights are still available here?” **Hint:** S1's first column must increase from 1 to 3. For S2, begin with the left clue of one on the bottom row. **Extension:** hide a clue and try to make a second city that still works.

**Facilitator check:** S1 has rows **123 / 231 / 312**. After the first column is fixed, there are only two legal 3×3 cities; the right clue on row 2 chooses between them. S2 has rows **231 / 123 / 312**. Its bottom row starts with 3 and has right visibility 2, forcing 312; row 2 ends in 3, and column restrictions finish the grid. Both puzzles have exactly one solution.

### 4–5 — How many clues are enough? · F05-U

**Prerequisites:** track four heights and multiple constraints; no arithmetic beyond counts to four. Reasoning: distinguish finding a solution from proving it is the only one.

**First 20 minutes:** warm up on a row of heights 1–4, then solve S3. Offer S1 or S2 first if the combined rules are new. Record a solution before taking the city apart.

**Next 15 minutes:** cover the top clue 4 and the left clue 4 on row 1, making S4. Try to construct a different city. Then cover the left clue 2 on row 3, making S5. Now a second solution exists: search for it, and give a new clue that distinguishes the two. If time runs short, the adult builds the alternate solution below and the child checks it.

**Ask:** “Have we found a second solution, or only failed to find one?” “Which clue sees a difference between these cities?” **Hint:** once looking at S5, try exchanging the two middle rows of S3's solution. **Extension:** remove a different clue from S4 and investigate; keep “no second solution found yet” separate from “proved unique.”

**Facilitator check:** S3 and S4 both have the unique solution **1234 / 2341 / 3412 / 4123**. S5 has exactly two solutions: that city and **1234 / 3412 / 2341 / 4123**. In the alternate, row 3 has left visibility 3 instead of 2; restoring that clue separates them. Every remaining S4 clue is necessary for uniqueness within that particular clue set, but this does not establish a globally smallest possible 4×4 puzzle. Exhaustive checks of all 12 order-three and all 576 order-four Latin squares verify the stated counts; the runnable check is linked at the end.

**Adult route / closing:** reserve conversation with the fifth grader for checking the two candidate cities, rather than leaving the whole uniqueness question as silent work. Close with a youngest child's viewpoint demonstration and, if wanted, two cities that obey the same clues.

**Sources:** JRMF *Skyscrapers Teaching Guide*, PDF pp. 1–4 for teaching and checking; pp. 5–12 for the source puzzle progression; pp. 13–14 for blank city grids. We use new clue sets rather than assigning the source puzzle pages. Clue removal and the particular ambiguity investigation are our extensions.

## Week 6: Hidden colors

**Idea:** a carefully controlled change reveals information; a strategy can identify every possible secret, not just a lucky one.

**Prepare:** two counter types, a screen, and rows of 2, 3, or 4 spaces. Call them red **R** and blue **B**, also marked with different symbols if useful. Repeated colors are allowed. Keep guesses and scores visible by drawing their colors/symbols, so old guesses do not consume the whole supply. Use dots or numerals for scores. Prepare the short scored histories below as work available without an opponent.

**Shared launch:** make secret RBR visible and score guesses RRR (2), BRB (0), and RBR (3). Children check one place at a time. Then hide it. **Feedback is only the number of correct positions; do not say which ones. There are no extra “right color, wrong place” clues.** Check children can score accurately before assigning a child checker.

### K–1 — Two hidden places · F06-K

**Prerequisites:** match types, preserve left-to-right order, count 0–2. Reasoning: use earlier evidence instead of starting fresh each turn.

**First 20 minutes:** practice with the secret visible; then hide two counters. A child guesses and the parent gives feedback. Rotate who makes the secret. Start with unlimited guesses and leave every guess on the table. If “zero” is unclear, use an empty feedback box beside one-dot and two-dot boxes.

**Next 15 minutes:** offer history **RR → 1; BR → 0**. Use physical candidate rows RR, RB, BR, BB; remove candidates that disagree with a clue. Play again, inviting the child to change just one place.

**Ask:** “Could the secret still be this one?” “What changed between these two guesses?” **Hint:** test each of the four candidate rows against both clues. **Extension:** invent a two-clue history with one remaining secret, then let a partner solve it.

**Facilitator check:** the prepared history forces **RB**. BR scoring zero says its first color is not B and its second is not R. This is already sufficient; RR → 1 provides a useful consistency check. Success means using a clue, not meeting a guess limit.

### 2–3 — Change one place · F06-M

**Prerequisites:** counts 0–3; no reading beyond optional R/B labels. Reasoning: compare two attempts differing in exactly one feature.

**First 20 minutes:** play with three positions. Rotate setter, guesser, checker after each secret; the setter and checker agree on feedback before giving it. Make the first rounds untimed and unlimited. Try an all-red guess and discuss what its score reveals.

**Next 15 minutes:** solve history **RRR → 1; BRR → 0; RBR → 2**. Then challenge partners to use a single-place change to learn something certain. Offer a four-guess target only after the rules and comparisons are secure.

**Ask:** “Did that change make the score go up or down?” “Which guess are you comparing it with?” **Hint:** compare both BRR and RBR to the same RRR baseline. **Extension:** predict what another guess's score must be before asking the setter.

**Facilitator check:** the prepared secret is **RBB**. Changing place 1 from the baseline reduced the score, so place 1 is R; changing place 2 increased it, so place 2 is B. The baseline had just one R, already found, so place 3 is B. A baseline, two separate flips, and the solved code require at most four guesses.

### 4–5 — Build a reliable code breaker · F06-U

**Prerequisites:** counts 0–4 and compare a score with a baseline. Reasoning: explain a procedure covering every secret.

**First 20 minutes:** play one or two four-position games against the organizer, then investigate history **RRRR → 2; BRRR → 3; RBRR → 1; RRBR → 1** while the adult visits 333. Build and check all remaining candidate secrets, or reason place by place.

**Next 15 minutes:** try to devise a method guaranteed to finish within five guesses, including the final correct submission. Play another adult-held secret to test it. If the child has a different promising strategy, explore that before offering the baseline hint.

**Ask:** “What could happen if we change only one color?” “Can a two-color flip leave the score unchanged?” **Hint:** take an all-red baseline, then test the first three positions **one at a time, returning all other positions to red**. **Extension:** adapt the method to five positions; or design a history with contradictory feedback and explain how to spot it.

**Facilitator check:** the prepared secret is **BRRB**. A single flip increases the score by one if that place is B and decreases it by one if it is R; it cannot leave it unchanged. Baseline + three separate flips determine the first three positions; the baseline's total number of Rs determines the fourth. Submit the answer as guess 5, unless it was already guessed. For n binary positions this method needs at most n+1 guesses. This is a guarantee, not a claim that the bound is optimal.

**Adult route / closing:** the parent handles KK1 feedback. The organizer checks a 333 scoring example, plays a short game with the fifth grader, leaves the prepared history, returns to 333, then comes back for the strategy test. Close by showing a single changed position and asking what its score change tells us.

**Sources:** JRMF *Crack the Code*, PDF pp. 2–4: three positions/four guesses and four positions/five guesses. Physical materials, two-place entry, unlimited practice, histories, and the worked baseline method are our scaffolds. More colors are deliberately reserved for a later visit to this theme.

## Week 7: Take the last

**Idea:** solve a small endgame, then use it to understand larger positions; a winning position has a move that leaves the opponent a losing one.

**Prepare:** 25 counters per group, separate large move cards **1 or 2** and **1, 3, or 4**, and ready-numbered strips 0–21 with space for marks. Use the correct move card at each table. Taking more counters than remain is illegal. Taking the last counter wins; taking none is not allowed. Zero means the game has ended and the player who would move next has lost.

**Shared launch:** play a three-counter 1-or-2 game without explaining the strategy. Ask children to check each move. Tell the older tables that their move card will change. Avoid a universal “pair to a constant” explanation at the launch; it does not directly describe the 1–3–4 game.

### K–1 — Leave a little trap · F07-K

**Prerequisites:** count and remove 1 or 2; small piles to eight. Reasoning: examine both possible replies.

**First 20 minutes:** play from 4, 5, and 3 counters, changing who starts each time. Ask children to choose whether to go first or second from three and demonstrate why. Do not require them to label positions formally.

**Next 15 minutes:** play from 6, 7, and 8. Try arranging counters in groups of three if a child notices the pattern. Let each child challenge the adult to escape from a chosen small pile.

**Ask:** “If I take one from three, what can you do? If I take two?” **Hint:** keep a spare pile of three to play through the two replies. **Extension:** from seven or eight, leave six and explain the response to either opposing move.

**Facilitator check:** multiples of three are losing for the player to move if the opponent plays correctly. From 4 take 1; from 5 take 2. From 3, go second and take whatever remains. From 6, respond to 1 by taking 2, or to 2 by taking 1, leaving 3. Children can prove this with two demonstrations rather than a formula.

### 2–3 — The missing move changes the game · F07-M

**Prerequisites:** count small piles and follow the unusual allowed set; subtraction can be physical. Reasoning: use established smaller positions to judge a new one.

**First 20 minutes:** take **1, 3, or 4** from piles 5, 6, and 7, playing each with both starting roles. Then investigate 1, 2, 3, 4 to explain what happened. Mark a strip with positions “good to receive” and “bad to receive,” always from the viewpoint of the next player.

**Next 15 minutes:** explore 8, 9, and 10, then up to 14 if interest holds. For a disputed number, list its legal next piles and use the smaller games already checked. The strip records reasoning, not simply who happened to win a game.

**Ask:** “Can you leave two?” “If every move gives the other player a good position, what does that say?” **Hint:** from two, only one can be taken. **Extension:** present a move from 8 that wins and a move that gives away the advantage; ask a partner to distinguish them.

**Facilitator check:** losing piles through 14 are **0, 2, 7, 9, 14**. From 5 take 3 to leave 2; from 6 take 4; from 7, every move leaves a winning pile. From 8 take 1 to leave 7; taking 3 to leave 5 gives the opponent a winning move. From 10, both taking 1 and taking 3 work, leaving 9 and 7 respectively. Not every winning position has only one winning move.

### 4–5 — Explain the repeat; change one rule · F07-U

**Prerequisites:** use a small position chart; addition/subtraction to about 21. Reasoning: distinguish an observed pattern from an argument that continues.

**First 20 minutes:** play 1–3–4 against the organizer from 10, 14, and 16, then classify positions through 21. For each winning mark, draw an arrow to one losing position; for a losing mark, check every legal destination. Use this chart between adult visits.

**Next 15 minutes:** explain the repeating pattern if it has emerged. Then change the permitted moves to **1, 3, or 5** and play from 6 and 7. Predict whether the old chart survives and build a new explanation. Alternatively, continue the first game's proof if that is engaging.

**Ask:** “Can a legal move connect two of your supposed losing piles?” “Does every other pile have a way into that set?” **Hint:** arrange the chart in rows of seven for 1–3–4; arrange physical pairs for 1–3–5. **Extension:** give the adult a larger starting pile and defend the choice of first or second without playing the entire game.

**Facilitator check:** for 1–3–4, losing sizes are **7k and 7k+2**, for k ≥ 0. No move of 1, 3, or 4 connects these sizes. The other remainders 1, 3, 4, 5, 6 can respectively subtract **1, 1, 4, 3, 4**, reaching remainder 0 or 2; these moves are legal even in the first block. This proves the pattern by reduction to smaller piles. For 1–3–5, every move changes parity; every positive odd pile can leave an even pile by taking 1. Thus even piles are losing, odd piles winning. The old 1-through-5 game from Lowell is a different rule set.

**Adult route / closing:** 333 can play and argue over the small chart while the organizer plays two-minute games with the fifth grader. Leave a specific disputed position between visits. Close with someone showing both replies from a small trap; no need for the oldest child's general formula at the whole-group close.

**Sources and returners:** JRMF *Countdown Teaching Guide*, PDF pp. 1–2 (play and questions), pp. 4–5 (position reasoning), **p. 8, challenge 4 (1–3–4)**. The source explicitly includes this move set; the previous plans' attribution of it as our invention was mistaken. The 1–3–5 comparison is our choice. Lowell Handouts 2–3 used 1-through-3 and 1-through-5. Handout 3.1 also contains a contradictory winning/losing label; use the checked definitions here.

## Week 8: Race to the star

**Idea:** a strategy can maintain a spatial relationship after every pair of moves; changing legal moves changes that relationship.

**Prepare:** boards with **3 rows × 3 columns**, **4 rows × 4 columns**, and **5 rows × 7 columns**, one token per game and spare paper for recording positions. Put the goal star in the bottom-right **cell**, not at a grid intersection. Row numbers increase downward, column numbers rightward; labels are for adults and may be omitted from children's boards. Add right and down arrows beside each board.

**Shared launch:** players share one token. On each turn move it right **or** down any positive number of cells, never both directions in a single rook move. No backward or diagonal movement. The player reaching the star wins. Demonstrate a legal long move as well as a short one. No chess knowledge is needed.

### K–1 — One move from the star · F08-K

**Prerequisites:** recognize right/down with arrows; count up to two spaces if needed. Reasoning: inspect the opponent's immediate options.

**First 20 minutes:** use the 3×3 board. Start in the top-right cell, then bottom-left: can the next player win immediately? Next start in the center (row 2, column 2). Play it both ways and show what happens after either legal move.

**Next 15 minutes:** start at row 2, column 1; seek a move that leaves the other player the center trap. Try the top-left start once the rules are secure. Keep rounds short, changing which child starts.

**Ask:** “Show every place you could move now.” “If you stop there, what can your partner do?” **Hint:** point along the row and column, excluding the starting cell. **Extension:** mark two starting cells the child would choose to receive and explain the choice.

**Facilitator check:** top-right and bottom-left allow immediate wins. Center is losing: either move lets the opponent reach the star. From row 2, column 1, move one right to the center. Top-left is also losing with correct opposition, but a child may enjoy investigating without reaching that general conclusion.

### 2–3 — Make a map of traps · F08-M

**Prerequisites:** follow the rook rule; compare small distances. Reasoning: work backward from the goal and give a response to every opposing move.

**First 20 minutes:** play on 4×4 from row 1, column 2, then from top-left, swapping first player. Begin marking cells good or bad to receive. Use “?” for unresolved cells. Start near the star and work outward; winning one game alone is not evidence that a cell guarantees a win.

**Next 15 minutes:** children choose a start for an opponent and predict which player can force a win. Ask a partner to try to break the prediction. Each child explains one trap by listing all moves from it and a winning response to each.

**Ask:** “Where can you leave equal distances in the two directions?” only after initial exploration. Earlier ask, “What do the difficult squares have in common?” **Hint:** compare the center-like cell row 3, column 3 with row 2, column 2. **Extension:** try a 4×5 rectangle and locate its traps; do not assume the top-left is always losing.

**Facilitator check:** on 4×4 the losing cells are (1,1), (2,2), (3,3), plus the already-finished goal (4,4). From (1,2), move down one to (2,2). From a trap, if the opponent moves some number right, move the same number down; if they move down, match right. Equal remaining distances make the reply legal. On 4×5, the trap diagonal ends at the goal: (1,2), (2,3), (3,4), and (4,5).

### 4–5 — A rectangle, then a different piece · F08-U

**Prerequisites:** count remaining horizontal and vertical moves; odd/even for the optional king task. Reasoning: justify a strategy from arbitrary starts and test which parts survive a rule change.

**First 20 minutes:** challenge the adult on the 5×7 board from top-left, then from a chosen interior cell. Find a strategy expressed in remaining distances to the goal. Between adult games, choose a supposed trap and mark the reply to each legal opposing move.

**Next 15 minutes:** replace the rook rule with **one cell right, one cell down, or one cell diagonally down-right**. These are the only king moves. Play from row 4, column 6, then from top-left. Map a few losing cells before proposing a pattern.

**Ask:** “Which distance can you shorten to make them match?” “Does matching still work if I can change both distances at once?” **Hint:** for the king, first classify all cells within two steps of the goal, then look at the parity of both remaining distances. **Extension:** explain how to move into the proposed losing set from any cell outside it.

**Facilitator check:** rook losing positions have equal remaining distances. From top-left of 5×7, remaining distances are 6 right and 4 down; move right two to column 3, leaving 4 and 4. For the king, losing positions have **both remaining distances even**. A legal move from such a cell makes at least one odd. From any other cell, change exactly the odd distance(s) by one, reaching two even distances. At (4,6), remaining distances are (1,1): rook-losing but king-winning by moving diagonally to the goal. Top-left's (6,4) is king-losing. The game always ends because every move decreases total distance.

**Adult route / closing:** leave a response map to work on between two adult games with the fifth grader. The third graders can challenge one another's marked starts without the organizer. Close with an actual move and response; save the full king explanation for the older table.

**Sources:** JRMF *Rook's Move Activity Guide*, PDF pp. 3–8, particularly pp. 5–6 for strategies. Smaller boards, selected starts, and the staged comparisons are our adaptation. Queen's Move is intentionally reserved for a later year rather than added as another rule today.

## Week 9: Corner hunters

**Idea:** a simple reflection rule creates a path whose endpoint depends on the table's shape; inverse design and scaling reveal structure.

**Prepare:** large square-grid rectangles, counters to follow paths, rulers, and a few paper diagonal arrows. Label every table explicitly **width × height**, with width horizontal. Launch from the bottom-left corner, moving diagonally up-right at 45°. At a vertical wall reverse only the horizontal direction; at a horizontal wall reverse only the vertical direction. **Stop at the first corner reached.** Crossing an earlier part of the path does not cause a bounce. A token traces the ideal path; rolling a real ball is not needed.

Pre-draw 3×3, 3×6, 6×3, and 3×9. Older children can draw more on grid paper. For KK1, mark potential wall-contact points and let the adult draw a chosen continuation; fine ruler control is not the challenge.

**Shared launch:** show the path to the first wall on a 3×6 table. Offer two arrows, up-left and down-left. “Which one is a bounce off this side?” Demonstrate with a token, emphasizing that upward motion continues at a vertical wall.

### K–1 — Choose the bounce · F09-K

**Prerequisites:** follow a diagonal, distinguish a corner from the middle of a wall; no reading or arithmetic. Reasoning: preserve one direction while reversing the other.

**First 20 minutes:** use a 3×3 square to reach a corner directly. Then follow 3×6 with one bounce, choosing its continuation arrow. Each child moves their own token and puts a marker on the ending corner. Try 6×3, where the first wall is horizontal instead.

**Next 15 minutes:** sort those tables by ending corner; try 3×9 if the bounce rule is comfortable. Let children place the arrow at each wall and have the adult follow their instructions. Remain with one-bounce paths if needed.

**Ask:** “Which part of the direction changes at this wall?” “Is this a wall meeting or a corner?” **Hint:** use one finger for left/right and another for up/down; change just the blocked direction. **Extension:** predict a table's endpoint before moving the token and explain one bounce.

**Facilitator check:** 3×3 ends top-right. 3×6 goes **(0,0) → (3,3) → (0,6)**, top-left. 6×3 goes **(0,0) → (3,3) → (6,0)**, bottom-right. Coordinates here are adult drawing instructions, not a child prerequisite. 3×9 ends top-right after two side-wall bounces.

### 2–3 — Design a table for my corner · F09-M

**Prerequisites:** follow reflections on a grid; count small side lengths. Reasoning: work backward from a target and test a design.

**First 20 minutes:** verify 3×6 and 6×3. Give each child a target corner—top-left, bottom-right, or top-right—and invite a rectangle that reaches it. Offer 3×9 as a trial card, with its endpoint undisclosed. Partners trace one another's designs to test them.

**Next 15 minutes:** design a second table for the same corner; make a prediction about doubling both dimensions before drawing. Offer 6×9 or 9×6 as a more intricate path for a child who wants one. There is no required catalogue of all sizes.

**Ask:** “What changed when width and height swapped?” “What stayed the same when both doubled?” **Hint:** rotate your attention between the tall and wide sample tables, or draw a larger copy with the same proportions. **Extension:** design a table reaching top-right after at least one bounce, so a square is no longer enough.

**Facilitator check:** target examples are 3×6 (top-left), 6×3 (bottom-right), and 3×9 (top-right, two bounces). Doubling both dimensions preserves the path shape and endpoint. A 6×9 path has vertices **(0,0), (6,6), (3,9), (0,6), (6,0)** and ends bottom-right; 9×6 ends top-left. Exchanging width and height exchanges top-left/bottom-right and preserves top-right.

### 4–5 — Explain a family of tables · F09-U

**Prerequisites:** grid paths and small whole-number side lengths. Reducing ratios or finding common multiples is optional support, not assumed prior knowledge. Reasoning: distinguish an invariant under scaling from a coincidence.

**First 20 minutes:** choose a target corner and build a family of three tables that reach it. Include a non-square table for top-right. Challenge the adult to predict a new family member, then justify why the pattern should continue.

**Next 15 minutes:** investigate 6×9, 9×6, and **6×10**. Compare the last with a 3×5 sketch if helpful; returning children may already recognize that shape. Ask whether raw odd/even side lengths suffice, and whether the path can ever end back at the launch corner. If the child wants a general explanation, place reflected copies of a small table side by side so one bounce becomes a straight continuation. Full unfolding remains an optional preview of the summer activity.

**Ask:** “Could scaling alter which corner it reaches?” “What smaller table has this same shape?” **Hint:** reduce both side lengths by the same factor; to explain a bounce, reflect the table across that wall. **Extension:** derive an endpoint rule, or give a convincing explanation that the first corner reached cannot be bottom-left.

**Facilitator check:** possible families are 3×6, 6×12, 9×18 (top-left); 6×3, 12×6, 18×9 (bottom-right); 3×9, 6×18, 9×27 (top-right). Large members need only a scaled sketch, not huge grids. Reduce width and height by their greatest common divisor to coprime numbers a, b:

| Reduced width a | Reduced height b | Endpoint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Odd | Odd | Top-right |
| Even | Odd | Bottom-right |
| Odd | Even | Top-left |

Thus 6×10 reduces to 3×5 and ends top-right despite both original lengths being even. For an adult explanation, in reflected copies the straight path first reaches a simultaneous table corner at distance L in both coordinate directions, where L is the least common multiple of width and height. It has crossed b table widths and a table heights. An odd number of widths ends on the right, even on the left; an odd number of heights ends at the top, even at the bottom. Coprime a and b cannot both be even, so the first corner cannot be bottom-left. This is facilitator background, not a required child result.

**Adult route / closing:** leave the fifth grader a family to test, then return to discuss why scaling works. Share a youngest child's bounce and an older child's target design. Do not require all three groups to hear the endpoint rule.

**Sources and returners:** JRMF *Billiards Geometry*, PDF p. 2; Lowell Handouts 10–11. Width/height language here avoids orientation ambiguity. Saved handouts include 7×5, 4×3, 8×10, several 1×n and 2×odd families, consecutive side lengths, and the height-five family. The main task here is inverse design with different initial tables; scaling is a deliberate revisit, not a new idea claimed as unseen. Check spring/summer history before promising novelty for any particular table.

## Week 10: Puzzle makers

**Idea:** posing a problem means choosing rules, checking existence, and communicating enough information for another person to investigate it. Testing may reveal an unintended solution worth keeping.

**Prepare:** select **at most three kits**, preferably window sticks (week 2), symbol codes (week 3), and take-away counters (week 7). Lay out one familiar example from each. Use folded paper with the puzzle outside and its solution inside. Children may dictate or demonstrate; writing a polished worksheet is not the work. A board-game kit can replace counters if that was more popular.

**Shared launch:** show a deliberately underspecified instruction such as “Take some counters and try to win.” Let children identify a missing rule, then repair it with legal moves and a win condition. “Make a puzzle another person can understand, try, and check.”

**Today's timing:** 0–10 revisit kits; 10–15 launch; 15–30 make and solve; 30–35 explain directions to a first tester; 35–40 movement/reset; 40–50 exchange and revise; 50–55 retest one change; 55–60 share and archive. Each child may make a tiny puzzle; no public event or visitors are assumed.

### K–1 — The repair shop opens · F10-K

**Prerequisites:** copy a small construction or match an action key; no reading or independent recording. Reasoning: create a difference someone can recover and check.

**Make:** build a picture on the window mat and have the parent trace it. Remove one or two sticks; give the incomplete picture and reference to a tester. Alternatively, make a three-symbol action message with its key. Keep the completed construction or adult drawing as the answer.

**Test and revise:** the maker watches the tester without immediately fixing things. Change one confusing feature and try again. Each child gets a turn being both maker and solver; the parent helps the exchange happen.

**Ask:** “How will your friend know when it is fixed?” **Hint:** start with a puzzle the child can solve and change one thing. **Extension:** make two different incomplete versions of the same target and compare which needs more explanation.

**Facilitator check:** verify the supplied reference agrees with the intended repair, or that every action symbol has a matching key entry. A clear one-piece puzzle is enough; avoid converting the task into a drawing or memory contest.

### 2–3 — A puzzle, a hint, and a test · F10-M

**Prerequisites:** know the selected kit's rules; adult scribing available. Reasoning: design from a solution and learn from a solver's attempt.

**Make:** choose one starter: (a) divide a window picture into two parts and hide one; (b) write a short symbol message with a key or sufficient clues; (c) choose a 1–3–4 pile with a useful first move. The maker records a checked answer and one hint without writing the solution on the front.

**Test and revise:** rotate maker, solver, and checker. The solver says where directions were unclear or information was missing. Revise a rule, clue, or starting position and retest. For a game, the tester should try an opposing reply, not merely read the proposed first move.

**Ask:** “Does your hint give someone a next thing to try?” “Does their different answer still obey your rules?” **Hint:** make the solution first, then remove information. **Extension:** create two difficulty versions differing by one clue, and explain the difference.

**Facilitator check:** a worked example is enough to establish a shape/code puzzle is solvable. A claim of a winning move needs responses to opposing play. A reliable game starter is eight counters with moves 1, 3, 4: take one to leave seven, then use the checked small-position strategy from week 7. The hint “Try a smaller game first” preserves more discovery than naming the move.

### 4–5 — Control what the solver can discover · F10-U

**Prerequisites:** the chosen activity's rules. Reasoning: justify a claim about number of solutions or a forced strategy.

**Make:** choose one concrete brief: **exactly two missing-picture solutions**, **a code with two initial candidate words separated by one clue**, or **a game position with exactly one winning first move**. Solve the puzzle and write or dictate the justification. The adult is the first mathematical tester; invite a third grader to test an accessible version later.

**Test and revise:** let the tester search for a counterexample to the claim. If a third solution or extra winning move appears, revise either the puzzle or the claim. Supply a hint and an answer explanation for archiving.

**Ask:** “Why are there no other answers?” “Which condition is doing the work?” **Hint:** reuse a familiar construction and restrict just one freedom. **Extension:** deliberately remove that condition and show why the number of solutions changes.

**Facilitator check / available starter:** use **P + mystery = W** from week 2, but now specify that the mystery may use **only BC, BE, CF, EF**, the right-square positions. BC, CF, EF are required, BE is optional, so exactly two pictures work. Without that restriction there are 16, not two. For the game brief, eight counters under 1–3–4 has just one winning first move (take one); ten has two, so it is a useful counterexample. For a two-word code, BERRY/MERRY with the week 3 pattern is separated by revealing the first symbol's letter.

**Adult route / closing:** the supporting parent scribes and runs KK1 exchanges. The organizer checks 333 puzzles for workable rules, then gives the fifth grader a substantive test. Choose one example from each group to show, with the child's consent; an improved instruction or discovered extra solution is a mathematical contribution. If an earlier week needs more time, this workshop can use that kit throughout while retaining the make/test/revise cycle.

**Sources:** Rozhkovskaya, M.4, “Make your own problem,” especially M.4.1 (`OEBPS/part0031.xhtml`), supplies the invitation to invent a problem under constraints. Its animal/figure/number prompt is different from this kit-based workshop; the specific briefs, exchanges, and timetable here are our proposal.

## What informed the format

These are the source-supported lessons used in the design, separate from our staffing proposal:

- *Math Circle by the Bay*, preface printed pp. viii–x (PDF pp. 9–11), describes weekly elementary circles, available manipulatives, varied participation, extra challenges, practicing explanations, and adjusting depth across age groups. It does not establish our exact seven-child/two-adult schedule.
- Rozhkovskaya, Lesson 3 “At the lesson,” item 1 (`part0013.xhtml`), lets children attempt and explain before introducing a table; additional challenges are available without becoming compulsory whole-class material. That informs our attempt-first sequence and local extensions.
- Lesson 7 “At the lesson,” items 1–2 (`part0017.xhtml`), reports apparently missed legal moves during a game and strong engagement with open K'nex construction. That informs checking rules through a complete example and allowing substantial time with materials.
- Lesson 8 “At the lesson,” item 2 (`part0018.xhtml`), reports copying/coloring taking different amounts of time. That informs ready-numbered strips, movable clues, and adult scribing rather than extra copying for quick finishers.

## Prior use, recording, and future years

Do not label all of a week's reserve tasks as “used” simply because the theme was taught. Record the actual card, outline, code message, clue set, rule, and start a child encountered. In particular, a child who tried F01-K should not automatically receive that exact P1 question again on entering the middle group in a later year. Use the stronger constraints, a different outline, or a reserved problem then.

The saved Lowell collection already contains hexagon decompositions and other pattern-block work; shape operations; take-away games with 1-through-3 and 1-through-5; and multiple billiards families. The organizer also reported that ciphers were popular, but their exact previous instances are not established by the saved fall handouts. This guide changes keys and messages without asserting that the underlying cipher types are new.

Use the separate [session use log][use-log] after each meeting. It starts with all activities marked **planned**, not taught. Note who encountered which level: an accessible entry task and a deep extension may both belong in one child's history. Preserve one child-made puzzle with its solution when possible.

For future visits, leave substantial branches unused: more-color codes, other subtraction sets, Queen's Move, new silhouettes, different city clue structures, and physical unfolding of billiards. Ten fall meetings are one part of the twenty-session year and six-year path, not a complete rotation by themselves.
