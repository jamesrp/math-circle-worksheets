## How to use the guide

Most weeks have **one main investigation per group**, followed by a change of question or constraint. Week 2 instead has one shared collection with several routes; choose by interest and demonstrated readiness. Do the entry task and allow sustained play; the extensions are a reserve, not a checklist. A child who spends the whole investigation finding and explaining two constructions has had a successful session.

Use the existing hour: **0–10** handle materials and explore; **10–15** shared launch; **15–35** group entry and main task; **35–40** movement/reset; **40–55** continue or extend; **55–60** show one discovery and tidy up. The timing suggestions in each group card refer to those two investigation blocks. Introduce only the first task at first.

This is the default rhythm in the individual plans and facilitator packets. Week 2 uses its own 0–5 handling, 5–10 launch, and 30–35 movement break, as shown in the shared guide. Each level has a satisfying stopping point and an optional explanation or proof continuation. Keep the reset even when a table or proof is unfinished; return to the same question afterward if it still holds attention. In Weeks 4 and 6, omit redundant table entries once the child can explain the structure. Use the [shared divisibility scaffold](coprime-divisibility-scaffold.md) with adult support for the general rules in Weeks 4 and 9.

Record *tried*, *conjectured*, *verified these cases*, *proved*, or *given as a theorem* for each actual claim in the [use log](../LOCAL-RESOURCES.md#private-records). A destination in the facilitator notes is not evidence that a child established it. The timings and new scaffolds are our adaptations; the [book notes][format] document the actual source lessons about trying first, manipulatives, explanation, and flexible pace.

The organizer demonstrates the common first action with everyone. Then keep one adult anchored to each group: **parent with KK1, other mathematician with 3333, organizer with 445**. The parent reads one prompt at a time, checks the visible rule, and invites children to show a move; the unified Week 2 adult guide supplies a short script, route choices, and answers. If mathematical help is needed, the parent keeps KK1 on a familiar task and signals the organizer. Before visiting, the organizer explicitly hands coverage of 445 to the nearby mathematician, who keeps both older groups on known tasks; no group is left unattended. See the [current coverage plan](fall-k-5-year-a.md#adult-coverage-and-timing). Older two-adult logistics in individual core guides are superseded by this plan. This is the September 28 planning configuration, not a claim about Week 1 attendance.

Give each child a work surface and materials for an individual attempt. In KK1 and 445, rotate maker, solver, and checker after a short puzzle, or let the adult partner the third child while two children work together. In 3333, use two pairs and compare their attempts. For competitive games, preserve **two opposing sides**; rotate players or partners regardless of who won. Do not turn the games into three-player games.

**Readiness:** all instructions may be spoken. K–1 needs matching and small counts; 2–3 adds coordination of constraints; 4–5 adds explanations about all possibilities. The new Week 3 letters function as labels and can be replaced with pictures. Weekly notes state when multiples, remainders, binary place value, or extra-page algebra need support; these ideas grow from concrete models. Keep facilitator solutions out of view until children have tried.

**Preparation:** print US Letter, single-sided, 100% / Actual Size. Use small 1× blocks, nominal one-inch edges; compare a real block against a printed cell before the meeting. The calibration line is on the adult guide and optional cutout sheet. Larger 2×/3× blocks change the puzzles. Per K–1 child: 6 blues, 12 greens, 3 purples, and a few reds/yellows. Per middle child: 10 blues, 5 greens, 3 purples. Per upper child: 8 blues, pencil, and optional string. For a future use with the current 3/4/3 roster this is **82 blues, 56 greens, 21 purples**, reused between boards. Use paper rhombi if needed. No pink pieces are needed for the core tasks.

**Shared hour:** 0–10 minutes handle materials and explore; 10–15 bring everyone together for a concrete warmup; 15–35 level-appropriate investigations; 35–40 movement/reset; 40–55 continue; 55–60 share and tidy. Before handing out separate level tasks, use middle Problem 1. Invite children to place blues on Board B and then try Board A. Demonstrate covering two whole triangle spaces with each blue, with no overlap or overhang. Show what a completed filling looks like and what being stuck looks like, without explaining away the puzzle. Let children place pieces during the demonstration. Then give each child a suitable first page. Keep later pages in reserve until the child has a next action.

**K–1 / F01-K-v4:** match edges and rotate pieces, with no reading or arithmetic prerequisite. Four pages retain the eight easy outlines: free fillings, two ways, chevron-only arrow/star, blue-only long hexagon, green-only mountain. Ask which piece fits a corner, or offer one starting piece. Solutions: sailboat 11 greens; cat 7; hexagon 1 yellow/3 blues/6 greens; diamond 4 blues/8 greens; arrow 2 purples; star 3; long hexagon 5 blues; mountain 9 greens. A completed filling is a satisfying stop. [Exact K–1 geometry](week-01-k1-shape-mats.md) is unchanged.

**Grades 2–3 / F01-M-v3:** count spaces and compare up/down orientations, with counting through 25 supported by an adult. Problem 1 gives four boards: 9-cell triangle, 12-cell parallelogram, 11-cell board (6 up/5 down), 10-cell board (5/5). Their maximum blue counts are 3, 6, 5, 5. First develop the even-area requirement; the side-2 and side-4 triangles in Problem 2 then show even area is insufficient. On the side-2/3/4/5 triangles, best blue counts are 1/3/6/10 and gaps are 2/3/4/5. Each blue consumes one up and one down; each triangle has one extra up space per row. Pairing each down space with the up space just below-left attains the bound.

Problem 3 compares blue-only and purple-only packings on six boards, using a blue-only trial followed by a separate purple-only trial. Do not mix the pieces in one trial. Compare actual results before asking what replacing one purple with blues tells us. Every chevron splits into two blues, so a purple packing converts to a blue packing with the same gaps; the converse can fail. The six cases include exact tilings by both, exact tilings by blues only, equal nonzero gap minima, and a strict improvement in gaps with blues. The PDF key gives witnesses and optimality reasons. Pink-triangle work is removed. Give enough time to explore a few shapes deeply; all pages are available activities, not an hour-long completion requirement.

**Grades 4–5 / F01-U-v3:** place eight blues, record a filling by drawing a line across each paired set of triangle spaces, and find more fillings before introducing flips. The first sheet combines the full-size mat with eight smaller recording boards. Next demonstrate a three-rhombus hexagon flip and A↔B, then draw connections directly between the six cards. Find a shortest A–F route and investigate odd returns. Build ribbon reasoning in separate tasks: trace from the sole bottom edge, record L/R, reconstruct from a path, compare adjacent letter changes, and enumerate the orders of two L's and two R's. Independent reading can be supported; counting, small sums and parity suffice, with no combinations notation prerequisite.

**Upper key:** graph edges AB, BC, BD, CE, DE, EF; shortest routes A–B–C–E–F and A–B–D–E–F, each four flips. Codes A–F are RRLL, RLRL, LRRL, RLLR, LRLR, LLRR. Every tiling has one bottom-to-top ribbon; the remaining orientation is forced. All six orders occur. Each adjacent RL or LR is a flip site, and every flip has this form. Add the positions of the two R's: a flip changes the sum by exactly one, so its parity switches. Returning to the same tiling needs even flips. Save “How do you know for sure?” for adult conversation when useful.

Hints follow attempts; avoid supplying the up/down or purple replacement idea immediately. ### K–1 — The block exchange shop · F01-K

**Prerequisites:** match boundaries and turn pieces; no reading or arithmetic. Counting up to six is optional.

**First 20 minutes:** fill the blue outline, then trade its piece for green triangles. Try the red outline, first freely and then without a red piece. Rebuild the same outline beside the first version so the child can compare them. Invite children to operate an exchange shop: a partner brings a piece and receives smaller pieces covering exactly the same space.

**Next 15 minutes:** fill P1, then replace one of its pieces without changing the boundary. Each child chooses a trade to demonstrate. If this is too much, continue with the blue and red cards.

**Ask:** “How can we check the trade is fair?” “Can you turn that piece?” “Did the outside change?” **Hint:** put the original piece on top of the proposed replacement, or trace around it. **Extension:** make two different fillings of P1; colors may occur in both.

**Facilitator check:** blue = two greens; red = one blue plus one green, or three greens. P1 can be yellow + green, or two reds + green. Cover exactly, with no gaps, stacking, or overhang. A different orientation of an unchanged single piece is not a new decomposition.

### 2–3 — Two teams, no shared colors · F01-M

**Prerequisites:** match outlines and track which block types occur; small counts only. Reasoning: combine several local trades under a global rule.

**First 20 minutes:** make P1 in two ways. Then add the rule: **a color used anywhere in one copy cannot occur anywhere in the other**. Children build their own pairs, then show a neighbor how they checked the color rule. If stuck, allow overlapping colors initially and fix one shared color at a time.

**Next 15 minutes:** try P2 with the same rule. Then ban yellow from both copies. Children can exchange the task “Here is my first copy; can you build the other?” A first copy using every color may make the partner's task impossible; noticing this is useful.

**Ask:** “If you use green here, what must the other copy avoid?” “Could changing two pieces together help?” **Hint:** for P1, begin with yellow + green on one side; the other side may use only red and blue. **Extension:** invent a small outline, demonstrate a legal pair, then give only the outline to someone else.

**Facilitator check:** P1 has the disjoint pair **yellow + green / red + two blues**. To construct the latter, imagine the hexagon as six triangles around its center: join the added outside triangle to the inside triangle sharing its edge to make a blue rhombus. Split the remaining five sectors into two consecutive sectors for the other blue and three consecutive sectors for the red. P2 has **two yellows / four reds**, or with yellow banned, **four reds / six blues**. Each original hexagon can be filled separately to verify the latter constructions.

### 4–5 — Can you beat five pieces? · F01-U

**Prerequisites:** the color rule, addition of small counts, and willingness to compare a construction with a proposed lower bound. No area formula required.

**First 20 minutes:** solve P1's disjoint-color challenge. Then minimize the **total number of pieces across both copies**. Make a candidate, let the adult try to improve it, and explain why an even smaller pair would fail.

**Next 15 minutes:** try the same question for P2; then prohibit yellow in both copies. If the fifth grader has already found the limits, invite them to make a new outline and challenge the adult to beat their construction. Record a best-so-far result as a conjecture unless they have an argument.

**Ask:** “How much space can one allowed piece cover?” “What would two pieces have to be?” **Hint:** replace each block by green triangles to measure its area. **Extension:** separate two claims: “These piece areas add up correctly” and “These pieces actually fit.” Ask the child to draw or build the fit as well.

**Facilitator check:** areas in green-triangle units are 1, 2, 3, 6. P1 has area 7. Each copy needs at least two pieces; a two-piece filling must use yellow + green. Two such fillings violate the color rule, so a pair needs at least **five pieces**, achieved above. P2 has area 12. If one copy uses yellow, it needs at least two pieces and the other, with pieces of area at most 3, needs at least four: **six total**, achieved. If neither uses yellow, at most one copy can use red. That copy needs at least four pieces; the other needs at least six, giving **ten total**, achieved by red-only and blue-only fillings. If neither uses red either, the lower bound is still larger.

**Adult route / closing:** the fifth grader can work on the lower bound between visits; reserve a visit for the adult's attempted improvement. At closing, let KK1 demonstrate a fair trade and an older child show how the color restriction changes the problem.

## What informed the format

These are the source-supported lessons used in the design, separate from our staffing proposal:

- *Math Circle by the Bay*, preface printed pp. viii–x (PDF pp. 9–11), describes weekly elementary circles, available manipulatives, varied participation, extra challenges, practicing explanations, and adjusting depth across age groups. It does not establish our proposed ten-child/three-adult schedule.
- Rozhkovskaya, Lesson 3 “At the lesson,” item 1 (`part0013.xhtml`), lets children attempt and explain before introducing a table; additional challenges are available without becoming compulsory whole-class material. That informs our attempt-first sequence and local extensions.
- Lesson 7 “At the lesson,” items 1–2 (`part0017.xhtml`), reports apparently missed legal moves during a game and strong engagement with open K'nex construction. That informs checking rules through a complete example and allowing substantial time with materials.
- Lesson 8 “At the lesson,” item 2 (`part0018.xhtml`), reports copying/coloring taking different amounts of time. That informs ready-numbered strips, movable clues, and adult scribing rather than extra copying for quick finishers.

The selected tasks and timing remain proposals to test with these children. The [source notes][format] contain the fuller account and its limits. The initial guide drew on the local collection. ## Prior use, recording, and future years

Do not label all of a week's reserve tasks as “used” simply because the theme was taught. Record the actual card, outline, code message, clue set, rule, and start a child encountered. In particular, a child who tried F01-K should not automatically receive that exact P1 question again on entering the middle group in a later year. Use the stronger constraints, a different outline, or a reserved problem then.

The saved Lowell collection already contains hexagon decompositions and other pattern-block work; shape operations; take-away games with 1-through-3 and 1-through-5; and multiple billiards families. The organizer also reported that ciphers were popular, but their exact previous instances are not established by the saved fall handouts. The revised coding weeks investigate cycle structure and modular orbits. These are new questions and representations without asserting that all underlying ideas are unfamiliar.

Use the separate [session use log][use-log] after each meeting. It starts with all activities marked **planned**, not taught. Note who encountered which level: an accessible entry task and a deep extension may both belong in one child's history. Preserve one child-made puzzle with its solution when possible.

For future visits, retain unused extra pages, more-color codes, other subtraction sets, new graph shapes, different city clue structures, and more general billiard directions. Unfolding is now part of the Week 9 core; record its actual use rather than continuing to list it as wholly reserved. Ten fall meetings are one part of the twenty-session year and six-year path, not a complete rotation by themselves.
