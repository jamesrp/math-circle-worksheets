## The mathematics

Noncrossing pairings of 2n fixed points, paths of n up and n down steps that never dip below the start, and ordered rooted trees with n edges correspond one to one. Each inverse is forced (close the most recent open string; at U add a rightmost child, at D go to the parent), so all three are counted by 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, through the first-return split. With objects, children also reach that a fixed chord can be completed exactly when it leaves an even number of dots on each side, that odd counts cannot pair, and that every noncrossing pairing joins two neighbours. A mathematician would enjoy it. K–1 P1–P6, 2–3 P2–P6 and 4–5 P2–P6 carry it.

## Fix

1. **must**, guide p. 4, K–1 P4: "Hint: compare only the partners at a single numbered dot to check for duplicates." False: at any dot the 14 pairings split 5, 2, 2, 5 by partner; 12|34|56|78 and 12|38|45|67 agree at dot 1. The parent volunteer would call a new pairing a repeat. Fix: "Two drawings are the same only if every dot has the same partner. Sort by 1's partner, then compare dot by dot within each group."
2. **should**, guide p. 10: "Grades 2-3 can use their pairings to rebuild codes (P3-5)". P3 and P5 rebuild pairings from codes. Write "rebuild pairings from codes and decide which codes are legal (P3-5)".
3. **should**, K–1 p. 3, P3: the frames carry 14, 14, 16, 16, 13, 15, telling the child each possible start has two endings (guide p. 4: duplicates are "for the two completions"). Print every start twice.
4. **should**, K–1 p. 4, P4: six frames for 14 pairings and "Draw extra circles on blank paper if you need them." A kindergartner cannot draw a numbered eight-dot circle; 2–3 gets 16 rows for the same 14. Add a page with "Problem 4 (continued)" and ten frames, and drop the sentence.
5. **should**, guide p. 2: "50 flexible 40 cm strings" on mats 64–86 mm across leave 30 cm or more of tail per string, over labels 12.6 mm outside each dot. The longest arc used, 2–3 P1's 1–6, is about 19 cm. Prediction, unrehearsed. Cut 20 cm strings and rehearse one eight-dot mat.
6. **should**, guide p. 3: "Choose depth: K-1 P3 or P6 … P4/P6 in K-1 … are surplus work." P6 is both; P5 is in no route here or on p. 10. The K–1 adult is not a mathematician; give one order: "P1–P2, then P3 and P5; P4 and P6 are surplus."
7. **could**, 4–5 p. 2: cut "Numbers show the walk order."; the labels "1 U" to "4 D" say it.
8. **could**, guide p. 7: add partner lists for the 14, as p. 3 has for the five, so the K–1 adult checks circles by number.
9. **could**, K–1 pp. 2–5: lower the record rows a few millimetres; each mat's bottom label sits just above the middle frame's "1".
10. **could**, K–1 P3: "Keep each drawn pair when you try it on the large circle" is hard to hear once; "Put a string on the drawn pair and leave it there."

## App fit

Fit B, confirmed. A child taps two dots to lay a string; the app refuses crossings, which paper cannot. Solves: finish a pairing from fixed chords or declare it impossible (certificate: the trapped odd region lights up); collect every six- or eight-dot pairing, with the child declaring "done" and the app checking, never a visible "3 of 5"; build the one pairing or tree for a given path, or tap out the path of a given pairing or tree; try to avoid joining neighbours (certificate for "can't": the innermost string). The return visit's P4 (swap DU to UD to reach the mountain in fewest moves) has a "best" certificate: the D-before-U count, which each swap changes by one. Avoid pure "is this code legal?" prediction (have the child build it or show where the walk drops below the line), found-count displays, and treating rotated pairings as equal. Draw on K–1 P1, P3, P4, P6; 2–3 P3, P5; 4–5 P3, P4; RV P4.
