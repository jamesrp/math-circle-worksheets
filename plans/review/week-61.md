## The mathematics

A taut string on a ball follows a great circle, and opposite dots have endless shortest routes (P1). A triangle can have three right corners (P2). With N fixed and A, B on the equator, the triangle is half a lune and covers gap/720 of the ball (P3–P4). The lune pairs at the three corners cover the triangle and its opposite three times and every other cell once, whatever the cells' sizes (P5–P6), so the pair areas sum to 1 + 4f and f = (sum − 180)/720 (P7). This is Girard's theorem by its classical proof; a mathematician would enjoy it.

## Fix

1. **should**, K–1, guide p. 3. The youngest get route and corner play for the hour, though P1's opposite dots and P2's question suit non-readers. Not a must: the outline promises no K–1 packet, and the parent is not stuck. Add: with dots exactly opposite, "Pull the string tight. Can you find a second tight route just as short?"; then "Make a three-string triangle whose corners all fit the paper corner", and the same with three straws on the table, where it can't be done. That K–1 sustains this is a prediction.
2. **should**, p. 1 rules: "For two distinct points that are not opposite, its shorter arc is the shortest surface route." Read aloud, it answers P1 (math.md item 4). Write "A shortest surface route between two points runs along a great circle through them", or cut it.
3. **should**, p. 2 rules: "Use the smaller convex region: each inside corner is less than 180°, and the whole triangle fits strictly inside an open half of the ball." A third grader can't act on it, triangles with corners on the equator (P2, P3) seem to break it, and guide p. 3 gives this check to the adult. Write "Our triangles enclose some area, and no two corners are opposite. Use the smaller region inside the three sides." That implies the rest. Cut "A flat picture of the ball is a sketch, not an angle meter."
4. **should**, P7: "Explain why the covering counts persist for every triangle under our rules, and why opposite triangles have equal area." This is the proof's plan, and it answers P6's open question. Write "Find a rule for a triangle's fraction of the ball from the sum of its corners. Use it for these three triangles. Explain why it works for every triangle." Move the two steps to guide p. 8 as a hint.
5. **should**, p. 5 example, which guide pp. 3 and 5 call "non-task". The panels are P6's triangle in P6's views, and "Pair at A" shades regions 1, 4, 5 and 8, P6's whole A column (math.md item 1). Redraw on an unused triangle, such as three 70° corners; if kept, say so in both guide sentences.
6. **should**, guide pp. 2–3. Page 2's print list has "First visit: P1–3" and puts P4 on a "Return menu", but the p. 3 timeline offers P4 at minutes 45–54. The adult has no P4 to hand out, and a quick upper trio stops at P3 (prediction). Print P4 for every older child and P5–7 for the trio on the first visit.
7. **should**, guide p. 2 kit: "removable meridian references at 45, 72, 120 degrees" from "a manufactured calibrated globe or center-plane/angular reference". Nothing supplies them, and 72° falls between a globe's 15° lines. P4's fractions follow from the printed gaps: say rough marks suffice and name a ball to buy, or change 72° to 60°.
8. **should**, guide p. 8: "Counterexamples to overextension." The octant's complement (630/720 = 7/8) and the long-side triangle obey the rule; only the latitude case fails (math.md item 2), so an adult could call a correct extension wrong. Relabel it "Outside our rules", say the rule still gives 7/8 there, and on p. 1 write "This proof does not cover".
9. **could**, p. 5: A's label box hides the corner "Corner A" names, as do A and A* on the next two panels; set labels beside the dots.
10. **could**, guide p. 6 hint: "compare the sizes of cells 1 and 2" judges area by projection, which guide p. 5 forbids; say cells 1 and 2 make the 80° lune at C, 2/9 (math.md item 3).
11. **could**, p. 3: cut "Its corner with the equator is a right angle."; checking it is part of P3.

## App fit

C, confirmed; size L. A port worth making: a globe the child turns, corners snapped to a 15° grid, and the app draws shortest arcs, true surface corners and the region. Solves: three right corners (P2); pole–equator triangles hitting a named fraction such as 1/12 (P3–P4); a lune-pair playground that counts coverings (P5–P6). The "can't" certificate is the rule: a corner sum of 180° or less leaves no area. Pitfalls: corners read from the flat picture, a fuzzy right-angle tolerance, typing a fraction after watching. P7 stays on paper.
