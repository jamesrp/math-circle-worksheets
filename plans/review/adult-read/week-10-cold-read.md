Reader: a fresh agent briefed as a parent volunteer, handed the three print band PDFs (K–1 12 pages, 2–3 13, 4–5 12) ten minutes before the session, with no guide. November 7, 2026. Week 10 was rated strong at every age in the kid-fit sweep, so it is the control. Report as written by the reader, lightly trimmed.

---

**1. What I think the activity is**
Each drawing is a "town": white circles are islands, grey bands are bridges. Children put a counter on every bridge and move a token from island to island; crossing a bridge means picking up its counter, so no bridge can be used twice. The question each time is whether they can collect every counter, and from which islands they can start. K–1 adds tracing pictures without lifting the pencil; 4–5 ends with Königsberg and proofs. Confidence: high (I already know the Königsberg puzzle; the rules paragraph on K–1 p1 got me there in about 30 seconds).

**2. Preparation**
Worked out: counters about 1/2 inch (bands are about 0.6 in wide), about 20 per child; one token per child; coloured pencils; pencils; "hexagons and craft sticks" for K–1 P7 and 2–3 P8.
Could not work out: what the hexagons are and how many; whether craft sticks are bridges between hexagons and how to show two bridges between one pair; alone or in pairs; whether counters go back on between tries; timing (K–1 has 12 pages); no answers anywhere.

**3. Problem 1 launch**
- K–1: read the opening rules aloud; demonstrate on the triangle town; children do all four towns and colour the starting island. Works. Unclear: reset counters for each town?
- 2–3: same, plus writing the walk "like A B C A". Clear enough.
- 4–5: same; the page states the goal outright. Clear.

**4. The point**
A walk using every bridge exactly once exists only if 0 or 2 islands have an odd number of bridges; with 2 odd islands it starts at one and ends at the other. Königsberg has four, so it's impossible. Confidence: high, but only because I already knew Euler. A non-math parent couldn't check children's "how can you tell?" answers.
