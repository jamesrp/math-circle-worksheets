The shared rules block on page 1 is the same in all three bands and does its job. The rule sentence plus the two worked examples (one allowed, one not, each with a shapes row and a fills row marked "all different" or "two the same") told me within a minute what an allowed three is. The examples show all three shapes (circle, triangle, square) and all three fills (empty, black, striped), so I could work out that the nine tiles are the 3 x 3 combinations. I still had to work that out: the full set of nine is not pictured until K-1 page 5 and page 2 in the other bands.

## K-1

**What the activity is and what children are trying to find (confidence: high).** Children have nine tiles, one of each shape (circle, triangle, square) and fill (empty, striped, black). An "allowed three" is three tiles whose shapes are all the same or all different and whose fills are also all the same or all different. In Problem 1 they finish a printed pair with a third tile. In Problem 2 they sort all nine tiles into three allowed threes in different ways. In Problem 3 they find every allowed three that contains one chosen tile. In Problem 4 they cover as many printed tiles as they can without the covered tiles containing an allowed three.

**Launch of Problem 1 and what children then do.** I would hand out the tiles and let the children handle them. Then I would hold up the page 1 "allowed three": all different shapes, all different fills. Next I'd hold up the "not allowed" one and point at the two empty fills. Then, on page 2, I would put a tile under the first pair (empty circle, striped circle). That pair needs a black circle: same shape, so a circle, and all different fills, so black. Children then fill the other three boxes, and a partner checks each one.

**Point or surprise (confidence: medium-high).** Any two tiles have exactly one tile that finishes them, so each pair has one answer. The nine tiles can be split into three allowed threes in several ways: by shape (rows of page 5), by fill, and in mixed ways. Each tile belongs to the same small number of threes (I count four), which the middle-square layout in Problem 3 seems built to show. In Problem 4 you can't cover many tiles: I find four is the most. I am less sure that K-1 children are meant to reach the "four" in Problem 3 or Problem 4 rather than just play with them.

**Where I got stuck.**

1. **Page 2, Problem 1, "Put a tile in each big box."** *Slowing.* With one set of nine tiles, the four boxes cannot all be filled at once. The first pair (empty circle, striped circle) and the fourth pair (striped triangle, empty square) both need the black circle. The second pair (empty circle, empty triangle) and the third pair (striped circle, black triangle) both need the empty square. A child who fills the boxes in order will find their answer tile already used, and I would lose time deciding whether that means they are wrong. The page says "Use the nine tiles", which reads as one set per child.
2. **Page 2, Problem 1, "Then give your partner new pairs."** *Slowing.* There is nowhere on the page for a new pair. I guessed that the child lays two tiles on the table, but those two tiles then aren't available to answer with, which makes the duplicate-tile problem worse.
3. **Page 3, Problem 2, "Find different ways."** *Slowing.* The page doesn't say whether the same three threes in other rows, or a three with its tiles in another order, count as "different." The 2-3 page says they don't ("the same three groups in another order are not a new split"), but the K-1 adult never sees that. There is also one mat and no record, so each way found is destroyed when the child builds the next. A child, or I, will lose track of which ways we have already made.
4. **Page 3, Problem 2, "one three in each row of the mat."** *Minor.* "Mat" is first used here. It obviously names the 3 x 3 grid on this page. Rows mattering here sits a little oddly beside page 1's "Tile positions do not matter," but I could manage.
5. **Page 4, Problem 3, "Choose one tile and put it in the middle square. Find all allowed threes using it."** *Blocking (for using the printed mat).* The page doesn't say what goes in the eight squares around the middle. Is a three the middle tile plus two squares in a line through it (across, down, or corner to corner)? Or are the eight squares just a tray? Page 1 says "Tile positions do not matter," which suggests the squares carry no meaning. That makes me doubt the line-through-the-middle reading the layout hints at. I could run a table version, where children make threes with the middle tile one at a time on the table. I could not tell children how to use this mat, or how they would know they have "all" of the threes.
6. **Page 4, Problem 3, "then try other middle tiles."** *Minor.* Clear enough, but with no record each new middle tile wipes out the last arrangement. That's fine if nothing is to be compared.
7. **Page 5, Problem 4, "How many tiles can you keep?"** *Slowing.* "Keep" isn't defined. The problem says "put a counter on each tile you choose," so I took "keep" to mean "cover with a counter," but a five-year-old may hear it as taking tiles away. It's also unclear whether the partner's check ends the turn or the child may move counters and try again. That's a minor point, since a re-try seems natural.
8. **Page 5, Problem 4, physical tiles.** *Minor.* This problem uses the printed tiles plus counters, not the nine physical tiles. That's fine, but it's the first mention of counters.

## Grades 2-3

**What the activity is and what children are trying to find (confidence: high).** These are the same tiles and rule as K-1. Problem 1 finishes printed pairs and asks whether a pair can ever have two different answers. Problem 2 colours every way to split the nine into three allowed threes, with order not counting. Problem 3 covers as many tiles as possible with no allowed three among them and asks why no more can be covered. Problem 4 picks one tile and finds every allowed three that uses it, with an explanation of why the list is complete. Problem 5 is a two-player game: take turns adding tiles to one shared collection without making an allowed three, and the player who can't move loses. Do choices change how long the game lasts?

**Launch of Problem 1 and what children then do.** Same demonstration of the page 1 examples with the physical tiles. Then I'd work the first pair aloud: "both circles, so the third is a circle; empty and striped, so the third is black." Children complete the other three pairs and make pairs for a partner. The question "can any pair have two different answers?" is something I can actually ask and check.

**Point or surprise (confidence: medium-high).** Every pair has exactly one completing tile. The nine tiles can be split into threes in exactly four ways: rows, columns and two "diagonal" kinds on the printed board, by my count. Each tile is in four threes. At most four tiles can be chosen with no allowed three. The game always ends after exactly four tiles, whatever anyone does, so the length is fixed and the player who moves first always loses. The last two facts are my own working out; the pages only ask the questions.

**Where I got stuck.**

1. **Page 1, Problem 1, the empty box after each arrow.** *Minor.* It doesn't say whether children draw the third tile or place a physical one. Drawing seems intended, since physical tiles would run into the same duplicate problem as K-1 (two pairs need the black circle, two need the empty square). Either way works.
2. **Page 1, Problem 1, "Choose other pairs to give your partner."** *Minor.* There's no space for these. Children will use tiles on the table or draw on the back.
3. **Page 3, Problem 3, "How do you know you cannot keep more?"** *Minor.* "Keep" here means the tiles with counters on them. That's clear enough after "Choose as many as you can," but it's a new word for the same thing.
4. **Page 4, Problem 4, two small printed boards.** *Slowing.* Several threes go through the chosen tile, and the page doesn't say how to show them on two boards. One colour per three on one board? One three per board? With only two boards, one three per board won't fit, and with colours the threes overlap at the chosen tile. I would lose a few minutes agreeing on a recording method with each child. Problem 2's "one colour for each three" suggests colouring, but that problem's threes don't overlap.
5. **Page 5, Problem 5, "Mark each game's tiles on a board."** *Minor.* It isn't clear whether the mark is a dot, an X, or a number for the move order, or whether it should show which player chose which tile. It also isn't clear who checks that a move doesn't make an allowed three: the other player, presumably. The 4-5 page has a third player check, which the 2-3 adult won't see.

## Grades 4-5

**What the activity is and what children are trying to find (confidence: high).** These are the same tiles and rule, with reasons asked for each time. Problem 1 completes pairs and asks whether a pair can have two completing tiles, or none. Problem 2 finds the largest collection with no allowed three and why a larger one is impossible. Problem 3 finds all threes through one tile, why the list is complete, and how many tiles two different threes can share. Problem 4 finds every split of the nine into three threes and why the list is complete. Problem 5 is the take-turns game with a third player checking, and asks whether it can stop at different numbers of tiles and why one can or cannot "get stuck early."

**Launch of Problem 1 and what children then do.** Same page 1 demonstration and one worked pair, after which children complete the rest and argue the "two or none" question. They can then mostly run themselves through Problems 2-4.

**Point or surprise (confidence: medium-high).** Each pair has exactly one completing tile, never two or none, because each feature independently has one forced value. So two different threes share at most one tile. The threes through any tile pair off the other eight tiles, giving four. There are exactly four splits. The largest no-three collection has four tiles, and the game always lasts exactly four tiles. The pages pose these clearly enough that I can see them as the destination even without a guide.

**Where I got stuck.**

1. **Page 1, Problem 1, "Complete the pairs."** *Minor.* Unlike 2-3, it doesn't say "to make an allowed three." I inferred it from the rule block, and "completing tiles" in the next sentence confirms it.
2. **Page 3, Problem 3, two small printed boards.** *Slowing.* Same as 2-3 Problem 4: it doesn't show how several overlapping threes through one tile are to be marked on two boards.
3. **Page 3, Problem 3, "threes through one chosen tile."** *Minor.* "Through" is line language. "That contain" or "that use" (as 2-3 has it) would be plainer, but it is readable.
4. **Page 5, Problem 5, "a third player checks each move."** *Minor.* This needs groups of three. Fine, but worth knowing when seating a table.
5. **Page 5, Problem 5, "get stuck early."** *Minor.* "Early" has no stated baseline: early compared with what? Read with the previous sentence, it means "stop at fewer tiles than other games do," which is workable.
6. **Page 5, Problem 5, the blank mat.** *Minor.* "Put the chosen tiles on the mat" is clear. Children may wonder whether position on the mat matters. Page 1 says it doesn't, so the mat is only a holding area, but its 3 x 3 cells invite position-based thinking.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K-1 page 4, Problem 3 (blocking).** Say what the eight outer squares are for, in the problem's own words. If threes are meant to sit in lines through the middle: "Put a tile in the middle square. Fill the squares around it so that every line through the middle (across, down, corner to corner) is an allowed three." This also makes "all" checkable, because the mat is full when they are done. If the mat is just a tray, remove the grid and leave open space. Also qualify the shared rule: "Tile positions do not matter" should read "The order of tiles in a three does not matter," so the rule stops contradicting mats whose rows or lines carry meaning (K-1 Problems 2 and 3).
2. **K-1 page 2, Problem 1 (slowing).** Change one pair in each duplicate set so that the four completing tiles are four different tiles. For example, replace pair 4 (striped triangle, empty square) with a pair whose answer is not the black circle or empty square, and pair 3 likewise. Alternatively, if duplicates are intended, say "Move tiles when you need them again." For the adult card, if no change is made: one set of nine per child cannot fill all four boxes at once, so give each pair of children two sets.
3. **K-1 page 3, Problem 2 (slowing).** Add the 2-3 sentence: "The same three groups in another row are not a new way." To keep found ways, add a strip of small printed 3 x 3 boards to colour, as in 2-3 Problem 2. Or, for the adult card, the adult takes a phone photo of each finished mat.
4. **2-3 page 4, Problem 4 and 4-5 page 3, Problem 3 (slowing).** Give one board per three, at four boards, or state the record: "Colour each three a different colour on a board." Four boards, one per three, keeps the overlap at the chosen tile readable.
5. **K-1 page 5, Problem 4 (slowing).** Replace "keep" with the object it names: "How many tiles can you cover?" Make the same change in 2-3 Problem 3: "How do you know you cannot cover more?"
6. **K-1 page 2, Problem 1, "give your partner new pairs" (slowing).** Either add one or two empty pair-plus-box rows labelled as extra workspace, or cut the sentence and leave partner pairs to the adult card.
7. **All bands, page 1 (minor).** Show the nine tiles once in the shared rules, as a small 3 x 3 strip under the rule sentence, so the set exists before Problem 1 uses it. K-1 currently first pictures it on the last page.
8. **4-5 page 1, Problem 1 (minor).** Write "Complete the pairs to make allowed threes," matching 2-3.
9. **2-3 page 5, Problem 5 (minor).** Say who checks a move ("Your partner checks each move") and how to mark: "Number the tiles in the order they were chosen." Numbering also lets children compare game lengths, which is the question asked.
10. **4-5 page 5, Problem 5 (minor).** "Can the game ever stop with fewer tiles chosen than in another game?" would make "get stuck early" concrete. For the adult card: this game needs groups of three.
