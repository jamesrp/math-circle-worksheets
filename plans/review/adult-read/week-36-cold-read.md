# Week 36, Making threes: cold adult read

Reader: a fresh agent briefed as a parent volunteer who has never played Set, handed the three print band PDFs (K–1, 2–3, 4–5; 15 pages) ten minutes before the session, with no guide. November 7, 2026. Report as written by the reader.

---

The pages never mention Set and never assume you know it. The rules are fully stated on page 1 of each band.

**1. What I think the activity is**
There are 9 tiles: 3 shapes (circle, triangle, square) × 3 fills (empty, striped, solid). An "allowed three" is three tiles whose shapes are all the same or all different, and whose fills are also all the same or all different. Children complete pairs into allowed threes, split all 9 tiles into three allowed threes, find every three through one tile, pick the most tiles with no allowed three, and play a take-turns game built on that last idea. Confidence: **high** on the rules, medium on the logistics.

**2. Preparation**
- One set of 9 tiles, about 2-inch squares (the work-area cells look about that size).
- **I couldn't work out:**
  - Where the tiles come from. "Use the nine tiles" assumes they exist. Am I meant to cut up K–1 page 5 or 2–3 page 3, which show all nine at roughly tile size? Those pages are also problem pages.
  - How many sets I need: one per child, per pair, or per table?
  - How children mark a split on the pre-printed 3×3 tile grids (2–3 Problem 2, 4–5 Problems 3–4): circle it, colour it, use three colours? Nothing says.
  - Whether children need pencils or crayons, and whether they draw in the big empty boxes or put real tiles on them.

**3. Problem 1 by band**
- **K–1:** I'd read the rules aloud; page 1 is too much reading for K–1. I'd walk through the two examples using real tiles. Then each child picks a third tile for each of the four printed pairs, probably placing a real tile in the big box under the arrow (or drawing it?). The partner checks, then lays out new pairs with tiles. Runnable, apart from "draw or place?" and "with whose tiles?"
- **2–3:** Same task, with smaller answer boxes, plus "can any pair have two different answers?" Clear.
- **4–5:** "Complete the pairs. Can two different tiles have two completing tiles, or none?" I read "two different tiles" as "a pair" only on the second read.

**4. The point (my guess)**
Any two tiles have exactly one completing tile; each tile is in 4 allowed threes; two threes share at most one tile; a few (I'd guess 4) ways to split all nine; at most 4 tiles without an allowed three, and the game always lasts 4 moves so the first player always loses. Confidence: **medium**. Nothing tells me the target answers, so I couldn't confirm a child's "I found them all."
