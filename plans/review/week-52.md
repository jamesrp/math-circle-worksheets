## The mathematics

Hinged bars make a rigid triangle and a shearing square; one diagonal fixes the square. In a complete grid of square cells, a brace in row i, column j links dot Ri to dot Cj, and the grid holds exactly when the links connect every dot (Bolker–Crapo). So m + n − 1 braces is the minimum; in a frame that holds, a brace can go exactly when its link lies on a loop; a brace in every row and column is not enough (P4B, P8, P14), though six such braces in different cells of a 3-by-3 are (P13). A mathematician would enjoy it: rigidity becomes connectivity, because bar directions propagate along links. P4 and P6–P11 carry it; P12–P14 ask for the arguments.

## App fit

Fit B, size M, confirmed. The child taps a cell to brace or unbrace it and drags a corner to push. When the link groups are separate the frame really moves, each group's strip directions turning by its own angle (guide p. 9), so the wobble certifies failure. Solves: hold with the fewest braces, starred against Plume (P5, P6, P12); find every removable brace, declaring "done" with no visible count (P9); add one brace that makes it hold, or show none can (P11); cover every row and column and still wobble with as many braces as possible (P8, P14; the 4-by-4 maximum is 10). For Hard, block some cells; "can't" then has a tappable certificate: rows and columns whose crossings with the rest are all blocked. Pitfalls: a live link view or group count gives the theorem away, so offer links only after hand bracing; a "will it hold?" button is prediction; never remove bars, where the criterion fails. The K–1 triangle and square suit a playground; P13's proof stays on paper.
