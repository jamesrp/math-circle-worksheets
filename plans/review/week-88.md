## The mathematics

Disks touch a middle disk without overlapping. Each neighbouring pair needs a least wedge of the turn: 60° for equal disks, so at most six, and six force every neighbour to touch (P1, P6). The same wedge gives 9 small around a double-size disk and 4 large around a half-size one (P3, P4); pair wedges cap mixed rings at 8 and 5 (P5, P10); only the ratio matters (P11). In a 160 mm tray a row of four is locked, so 4-3-4 rows need 40 + 40√3 ≈ 109.3 mm (P7–P9). A mathematician would enjoy the rings: a sharp bound and its equality case, by sharing out 360°. The trays are a good exercise, not a theorem.

## Fix

1. **should**, guide p. 2, materials: no source or tolerance for 90 rigid disks, and "the practical 112 mm tray gives more tolerance" holds only for height, since 160 mm is exactly four diameters. A row of four needs disks of at most 40.00 mm; below 39.63 mm 4-3-4 rows fit 108 and P8's "No" fails in hand (common 1.5 in craft circles fit them in 102.6 mm). Fix: name metric 40 and 80 mm circles; pp. 1–5 work with any 2:1 pair, pp. 6–7 need 39.7–40.0 mm.
2. **should**, p. 8, P10: "can you explain why a ring with more outside disks is impossible?" Guide p. 3 accepts non-maximal rings, so a child with eight in P3 must prove something false (math.md 2). Fix: "For each of Problems 3–5, what is the largest possible ring? Explain why no ring can have more outside disks."
3. **should**, guide p. 3, P5: "Changing the central disk can change which size consumes the most room." False: a large disk takes more turn around either middle (math.md 1). Delete it.
4. **should**, p. 8, P11: "what rule links the two disk sizes…?" Two sizes give four pairings, and 80 around 80 needs seven large disks against a pair's five. Fix: add a 120 mm card middle per table and begin P11 "Make the biggest ring of 40 mm disks around a 120 mm disk." Twelve fit; 4, 6, 9, 12 against middle-to-outside ratios ½, 1, 2, 3 invite the rule.
5. **should**, guide p. 6, P10: the route is arcsin and the cosine rule; the kit has no protractor. Add: slide each outside disk round the middle until it touches the next. Nine small disks chained round the 80 mm middle leave about 9 mm; a ten-ring would slide into that chain with its tenth disk in the gap.
6. **should**, p. 6, P7: "Fit eleven 40 mm disks in this exact tray … Use the center distances to decide whether the disks really fit." It presumes the fit and gives the method. Fix: "Can eleven 40 mm disks fit in this tray in three straight horizontal rows: 4, then 3, then 4? Explain how you know."
7. **should**, guide p. 2: every table gets pp. 1–2 and "25–35 minutes on Problem 1 or 2"; centers wait. Prediction: a fourth grader finds six in minutes. Print pp. 1–5 for the 4–5 trio, with P6 as the target.
8. **could**, pp. 5–8 say "Grades K–5" though guide p. 1 maps centers to 4–5; relabel and rebuild the band index.
9. **could**, p. 3, P4: add "Sketches, not actual size"; its "80 mm" circle is 40 mm across (math.md 3).
10. **could**, p. 5, P6: the ring reaches 60 mm, the text is 55.7 mm away; enlarge the box to ±62 mm (math.md 4).
11. **could**, pp. 2, 6, 7: cut "Practical tray: 160 mm × 112 mm interior", which repeats the board's labels.
12. **could**, guide pp. 2, 5–6: "KK11 / 3333 / 445", "in[20,140]", "For(20, 40)", "radiia, b".
13. **could**, guide p. 4: six green pattern-block triangles make the 60° wedges; a seventh will not fit.

A board that keeps disks touching the middle makes the equal ring one push, and around the packet's middles, with outside sizes up to 4:1, I found no set whose fit turns on order by even half a degree. Free-drag trays have no "can't" certificate (11 in 160 × 112 is proved only to at most 12). The wedge argument belongs on paper.

If a road wants touching disks, use Rhombus gardens' triangle grid, the six-ring's lattice: tap a coin, then an empty spot touching two; turn a line into a ring, or flip a 10-coin triangle in 3 moves. The best overlap with the flipped copy (7 of 10, 10 of 15, 14 of 21) certifies 3, 5 and 7. Pitfall: the six-ring has no room, and floor(π/asin(1/2)) returns 5 in floating point; validate tangency exactly.
