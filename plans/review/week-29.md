## The mathematics

With unlimited rods of lengths a and b, n can be built exactly when n = ax + by with x, y ≥ 0. If a consecutive lengths can be built, so can every later one (add an a-rod), so a finite run settles infinitely many targets. For coprime a, b > 1 the last gap is ab − a − b; a shared factor leaves gaps forever. This is the Frobenius coin problem with a certificate a child can hold, and a mathematician would enjoy it. K–1 P1–P4, 2–3 P1–P6 and 4–5 P1–P8 carry it; 4–5 P6 adds the exchange of four 3-rods for three 4-rods.

## App fit

B, confirmed; size S, as a group in Water jugs, as the app board plans. Jugs pour water away, so 3 and 4 jugs measure 1, 2 and 5; rods only add, and those are exactly the gaps. That contrast justifies the shared home and is worth a puzzle. The child drags rods onto a number track that snaps them end to end; a target lights when a train ends on it.

- Find every: build each buildable target in a range and declare the rest impossible; the app checks the claim, with no counter.
- Forever: lay a run of a consecutive trains; the app slides an a-rod onto each, again and again. The run is the certificate for "every later target".
- Can't: the app lays zero, one, two… of the longer rod and shows no leftover fills with the shorter.
- Design: pick two rods from a tray with last gap 5 (2/7 or 3/4), 11 or 17, or with gaps forever.
- Best: fewest rods with 3, 5 and 8 (bonus P6).

Pitfalls: a typed last gap invites guessing ab − a − b; a slot per way gives away "every way" (K–1 P2); no predict-then-test (4–5 P7). Draw on K–1 P1, P3, P5, P6; 2–3 P1, P3, P5, P6; 4–5 P1, P2, P5, P6; the guide's extension; bonus P3–P6.
