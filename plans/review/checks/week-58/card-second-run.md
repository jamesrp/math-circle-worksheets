## The mathematics

People share cards or a paper strip, each valuing items by their own card. Different tastes let both get more than half (P1–P3). Exact cut-and-choose always gives both half or more and no envy; an equal-length cut need not (P3–P4). Whole items can make envy-freeness impossible, and one split removes the block (P5). Envy-free implies proportional by adding one observer's comparisons; the two coincide for two people, while three people can each hold a third and all envy (P2, P6, P7). These are fair division's first theorems, with honest hypotheses; a mathematician would enjoy them, though each proof is one line.

## App fit

B, confirmed; size M. A live table of both people's values turns dragging cards into trays into trial and error, so put the solve in decisions the app does not make:
- **Divide against the app.** It takes its better tray, and on a tie the one worse for the child (P1's all-red tie). Goal: keep at least half, or claim that no division is safe; a wrong claim is answered with a safe one.
- **Every envy-free allocation** (P2), ended by "That's all" with no visible count.
- **Cut a strip** of coloured panels with value dots; the app chooses. Snap to 10 mm so exact cuts exist.
- **Fewest cuts** to make a whole-card set envy-free (P5, fix 5). **Three people:** repair (P6), or build a proportional table with envy.

Pitfalls: no "is this fair?" predictions; 16 allocations invite brute force; never compare one person's numbers with another's. Draw on P1–P6; P4 and P7's proofs stay in grown-up notes.
