## The mathematics

Four labelled points are split into two groups; each group's region is its convex hull, a point, a closed segment or a filled triangle. In the plane some split of any four points has meeting hulls (Radon); three noncollinear points have none, so four is sharp. In general position exactly one of the seven splits works, crossing diagonals or an inside point against its triangle; collinear and coincident placements give two, four, up to seven, and no split of four shares area. A mathematician would enjoy this: a real theorem with a three-case proof and a sharp threshold, reached by placing points and making complete lists. K–1 P1–P6, 2–3 P2 and P4–P6, and 4–5 P2–P7 carry it.

## App fit

Fit B, size M, a short set: confirmed. Colouring dots while the app draws hulls is a seven-way tap-through, so put the solve elsewhere, on a lattice where collinear and coincident placements are exact. Find every working split, shelving each and declaring "That's all" with no counter: K–1 P2's positions (two each), the line boards (four), 4–5 P4 (four). Place D against a fixed triangle so a named split is the only one, or exactly two work (on a line through two dots), or four (only on a vertex; `card_app_check.py` finds only 1, 2 or 4). Tap every place C can go (K–1 and 2–3 P5); off line AB the app shows all three splits failing, the certificate for "can't". Build two boards with no common split (2–3 P4) or different unique splits (4–5 P5). The games make a playground. Pitfalls: "which split works?" predictions, free continuous placement, thick strokes faking contact, untappable stacked labels. Draw on K–1 P1–P3 and P5, 2–3 P1, P2, P4 and P5, 4–5 P2, P4, P5 and P7.
