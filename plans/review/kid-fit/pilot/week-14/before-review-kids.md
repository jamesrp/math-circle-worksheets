Kid-fit review of the draft in `draft/`, rendered and read page by page. I have not watched children use this packet; every verdict below is a filter, not a pilot result. Sizes are measured from 70-dpi renders and are approximate: large working boards are about 3–3.8 in across, recording copies about 0.95–1.15 in.

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** The core move (draw a straight line from one printed corner to another until the shape is all triangles, then count them) is something a five-year-old can do within a minute of the launch, and "Can you fill this shape with 3 triangles, or with 4 triangles?" (Problem 3) gives a real invariant children can feel by drawing it again and again. What holds it below strong: the rule that lines "may not cross" is enforced by nothing but an adult, and Problem 2's "Find every way to fill this five-corner shape" has five answers that are all the same picture turned round. Whether two fillings differ lives only in the corner numbers, which young children don't hold onto.

**Page: weak.** The opening rules ("Keep the corner numbers fixed. Use tracing paper to change printed lines.") mean nothing to a child who hasn't done it. After the first big board, every second way, every "every way" and every change goes onto copies about one inch across (Problem 1 right column, Problems 2–6 bottom rows) or onto tracing paper the child must place, trace and label. Problem 6 ("visit each of the other fillings once and then return") needs a five-step memory that a five-year-old can't keep.

**K–1 bar: not met.** Children can make the core move themselves, but they can't tell for themselves whether a drawing is legal (crossing lines), whether a filling is new (labels only), or whether a change was one change (tracing-paper overlay). The parent volunteer has to referee all three.

Patterns found:
- **2. Precision children don't have.** Problems 1–6 (pages 1–6): every filling after the first is drawn with a straightedge on a pentagon or hexagon about 1 in across (Problem 1's copies about 1.1 in, Problem 2's about 0.95 in), or on a traced copy. A kindergartner's hand-held ruler on a 1-inch hexagon will miss corners and run lines into each other, so a legal filling and an illegal one look alike. The tracing workflow (lay the sheet, trace the outline, copy six numbers, then draw) is fine-motor work that comes before any mathematics.
- **5. The adult runs it.** Page 1 rules: four rule sentences read at once (join corners, no crossing, fill with triangles, keep numbers fixed, use tracing paper). The volunteer will end up checking crossings, comparing labels to decide whether two pictures differ, and handling the tracing paper while children watch. Problem 3's "If one cannot be done, explain why" has an answer only the adult can give at this age; children can say "it always came out 4."
- **3. A procedure instead of choices.** Problem 6 (page 6): a chain of five one-line changes on blank 0.95-in copies, where the interesting thing (you get back to the start, and you've seen all five) happens only at the end, after redrawing each filling from scratch.
- **1. Record-keeping.** Problem 2 and Problem 6 (pages 2 and 6): eight blank copies to fill with redrawn fillings. Problem 5 avoids this well: its copies print the start in gray, so a child crosses out one line and draws one.
- **4. Told, not felt.** Problem 2 (page 2): that the five fillings are different is a fact about labels, so an adult announces it. Children may feel it through one handle: each pentagon filling has a single corner where both lines meet, and there are five corners.

**Delight moment:** Problem 3, the discovery that however you fill the six-corner shape you always get 4 triangles, and 3 never works. It is modest but children can see it for themselves.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Third graders can draw, compare by letters, and pool a collection, and a mathematician is at the table. Problem 2 ("Find every filling of this five-corner polygon. How do you know none are missing?") and Problem 7's hexagon collection are substantial goals children care about. Problem 4 ("Join two drawings when one flip changes one into the other") turns the five fillings into a ring. The flip rule is mostly self-enforcing: once one line is erased, the only way to restore "all triangles" with one line is the other diagonal of the four-sided hole. Still unpiloted: whether all four children keep fixed labels when comparing rotated pictures.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1, 2, 4 and 7 are clear and roomy. Problems 3, 5 and 6 make children redraw whole fillings of three diagonals on 1.1-in blank hexagons and pentagons to record each step. Problem 5 ("return to it after an odd number of flips") is answered by Problem 4's picture once the joins are drawn, so it becomes copying.

Patterns found:
- **1. Record-keeping.** Problem 3 (page 3): to show each "result of one such change," a child redraws all three inside lines on a blank 1.1-in hexagon copy, six results in all, though only one line differs from the printed start. Problem 5 (page 5): after Problem 4 the five-flip loop is already visible, and the page asks for "every filling along your route" on eight blank copies, which is transcription that confirms. Problem 6 (page 6): each intermediate filling redrawn on a 1.1-in copy.
- **2. Precision.** Problems 3, 5 and 6: three lines on a 1.1-in hexagon meet at one corner (the A-fan, the B-fan), and a slightly mis-aimed line reads as going to the wrong corner. The tracing-paper workflow helps on the large boards if the sheets don't slide; only a rehearsal can settle that.
- **4. Told, not felt.** Problem 5: "an odd number of flips" is surprising only if a child expects back-and-forth trips to be even. Third graders haven't formed that expectation, so the adult has to supply why "odd" matters. The loop itself is felt; its oddness is told.
- **3 and 5:** not significant. Each flip is a choice, and the rules are short enough for eight-year-olds to read.

**Delight moment:** Problem 4 (page 4). The five fillings are already placed in a ring, and once the joins are drawn they close up into a big five-sided loop. "It's a pentagon of pentagons" is a likely gasp.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** Odd loops, the shortest route to a fan, and "Can any two fillings of the same polygon be joined by flips?" (Problem 7) are real questions with a research mathematician at the table. Problem 5 ("Find the fewest flips from this filling to a fan at A, and to a fan at E. Explain why each route is shortest") gives a concrete lower-bound argument children can discover: count the lines already at A.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1 and 2 repeat the 2–3 tasks briskly, and Problem 3 combines the join picture with the odd-return question well. The octagon problems ask for routes of up to five flips, each recorded by redrawing five diagonals on 1.15-in octagons (Problem 5) or 0.95-in hexagons (Problem 4). Problem 6 offers one octagon for a rule about "every number of corners".

Patterns found:
- **1. Record-keeping.** Problem 5 (page 5): the route to a fan at E takes five flips, each recorded as a whole octagon with five diagonals on a 1.15-in copy. That is about 25 lines drawn to record what is really five "erase this, draw that" moves. Problem 4 (page 4) has the same problem on 0.95-in hexagons.
- **2. Precision.** Problem 5: on the small octagon copies, four lines converge on D (and in the target, on A or E); at that scale a line to D and a line to E are hard to tell apart, so a mistaken record can hide the count pattern the fan rule depends on.
- **5. The adult runs it (mild).** Problems 6 and 7 (pages 6–7): "when all corners point outward" and "any polygon with no inward-pointing corners" are rule-of-thumb phrases the organizer will have to unpack. That is acceptable this late in the packet.
- **3 and 4:** not significant. Each flip is a choice, and the odd loop and fan distances can be seen at the octagon's scale.

**Delight moment:** Problem 3: you can come back to where you started in five flips. A close second is Problem 5, when the fan count turns out to be "how many lines aren't at A yet."

## What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle

- Whether quarter tracing sheets stay put and cover the largest boards. Whether K–1 children can hold a short straightedge on a 1-in copy at all.
- Whether gray start lines can be crossed out legibly.
- How long the pentagon "every way" takes at K–1, and the 14-filling hexagon search (2–3 Problem 7).
- Whether third graders keep labels fixed when comparing rotated pictures.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1, all pages: drop tracing paper and the tiny copies from the K–1 path.** Replace the 1-in recording copies on Problems 1, 2, 4 and 6 with fewer, larger boards (about 2.5 in, four to a page), and add extra full-size pages of the pentagon and hexagon. Delete "Use tracing paper to change printed lines" from the K–1 rules. This page change addresses pattern 2 and most of pattern 5 and keeps the activity.
2. **K–1, page 1 rules: make the legality check physical.** Replace "Lines inside a shape may meet at corners but may not cross" with a check a partner can do by pointing: every triangle's three corners must be printed dots. A crossing makes a corner that isn't a dot. Cut "Keep the corner numbers fixed" for K–1; the numbers stay on the boards.
3. **K–1 Problem 6 (page 6):** print the start in gray on each copy, as Problem 5 already does, or move this problem to the end as an adult-led ring on the table, with each filling on its own large board laid out in order. A page change rescues it. Without one, it is an adult procedure.
4. **2–3 Problem 3 (page 3) and 4–5 Problem 2 (page 2):** print each start filling in light gray on its four copies, as K–1 Problem 5 does, so a child crosses out one line and draws one.
5. **2–3 Problem 5 (page 5):** either move it before Problem 4, so the odd return is found by flipping before the ring is drawn, or cut its eight blank copies to one route strip. As written, it copies the answer of Problem 4.
6. **4–5 Problems 4 and 5 (pages 4–5) and 2–3 Problem 6 (page 6):** enlarge the recording copies, or give a large working board plus room to record a route as a list of flips (for example "erase HD, draw AE"), so children don't redraw five-line octagons. Keep the method choice with the child: offer the space, not the instruction.
7. **4–5 Problem 6 (page 6):** add a blank hexagon and seven-corner board beside the octagon, so "every number of corners" has concrete cases to test.
8. **K–1 Problem 3 (page 3):** keep the question, and leave "explain why" as adult conversation. Children's evidence will be "it always came out 4". This is minor and only needs to be noted for the facilitator.

None of the bands needs "outline: for the organizer". K–1's shortfall is in the page and the materials path, not in the outline's mathematics.
