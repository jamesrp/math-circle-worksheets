All verdicts come from reading the rendered pages against the room described in PROMPT.md. Nobody has piloted this packet. Several questions can only be settled by a rehearsal or a pilot:

- whether a kindergartner can hold a short straightedge steady enough to join two dots on a 2.5–3.5 in board;
- whether quarter sheets of tracing paper stay put while a child traces a hexagon or octagon with its labels;
- how long one flip takes when done by tracing, compared with drawing on a pre-printed board;
- whether the K–1 table reaches Problem 6 at all.

---

## K–1 (two kindergartners and two first graders, parent volunteer)

**Activity: workable.** Drawing dot-to-dot lines until "the whole shape is made of triangles" is something a five-year-old can start right away, and Problem 3 ("Can you fill this shape with 3 triangles? Can you fill it with 4 triangles?") gives a surprise the children can feel at their own scale: every hexagon comes out with 4. Problem 6 is a real five-step loop that comes back home. Two things hold the activity at workable. Nothing physical enforces the rules. And the "every way" and "one change" judgments rest on conventions that a non-mathematician parent has to police.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1–4 and 6 have large, well-labeled boards and one-sentence tasks that make sense after one hearing. Problem 5's boards are about 1.3 in across, which is too small for this age. They also carry grey printed lines that the task says to "erase", and printed lines can't be erased.

**K–1 bar: not met.** Most children can make the first move (draw a line between two dots) within a minute. What stops the materials or a partner from enforcing the rules is this:

- The shared rule, "Every corner of every triangle must be a printed dot", is an adult's way of forbidding crossings. A kindergartner's most natural drawing is the five-pointed star, which is full of triangles, and only the adult's reading of that sentence rules it out.
- "One change" (Problems 5–6) is judged by eye on the page. The overlay referee check from the launch needs tracing paper, which the page boards don't use.
- Whether two pentagon fillings are "different ways" depends on fixed labels, and the K–1 page never says so.

### Patterns found (K–1)

1. **Hands-on start turning into record-keeping.** Mostly absent. One mild case: in Problem 2 ("Find every way to fill this five-corner shape", six boards for five answers), all five fillings look like the same fan turned around. A child is soon drawing pictures that look alike and asking whether they count. That makes the sixth board a duplicate trap rather than a goal.
2. **Precision.** Mild. The triangle count in Problem 3 survives wobbly lines. Problem 5 is the exception: small boards with grey lines meeting at corner 1 (and a grey triangle 1-3-5 on the second set). Crossing out one grey line and ruling a new one at that size is beyond kindergarten hands.
3. **Procedure instead of choices.** Problem 6 is a chain of five dependent drawings. After the first choice, each later step is forced (only one change avoids going back), so the payoff, landing on the top filling again, comes only at step 5. Children who make an illegal change at step 2 won't find out until the end.
4. **Told, not felt.** Problem 2: the count of 5 is "felt" only if the child accepts that "lines from dot 1" and "lines from dot 2" are different ways. For many five-year-olds, someone will have to announce that convention.
5. **The adult runs it.** The rules exist only on paper, the same failure mode as the lamp session. The parent decides whether a drawing is legal (no crossings), whether two drawings differ, and whether a change is a single change. They can't decide "every way" in Problems 2, 4 and 5 without an answer key. Reading load itself is light.

**Most likely delight.** Problem 3: "It's always 4!", after several tries at 3. Problem 6 could beat it ("we came back!"), but it sits on page 6, and a K–1 table will probably not reach it by minute 30.

**Picture at the table.** At minute 5, a first grader is ruling lines across the first pentagon, and a kindergartner draws a star and is told it doesn't count. At minute 15 they are on Problem 2, drawing fans from different dots and asking "is this the same one?" At minute 30, the first graders are counting triangles in Problem 3 hexagons or doing Problem 4. The kindergartners have drifted to coloring triangles.

## Grades 2–3 (four third graders, mathematician)

**Activity: strong.** Third graders can draw diagonals freely. The sequence moves from the triangle-count question in Problem 1 ("Can the two fillings of one polygon have different numbers of triangles?") to a complete pentagon collection, then one-line changes, then the "map" in Problem 5 ("Join two drawings when one flip changes one into the other"). That progression lets a felt fact turn into a picture of all the fillings. The printed five fillings in Problem 5 are laid out so that the correct joins form a neat pentagon with no crossings. "The map of the pentagon's fillings is a pentagon" is a real, visible surprise.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1–2 and 5 are clear and well sized. Three things pull the page down:

- Problem 3's grey-line boards have no stated way to show which line was removed.
- Problem 6's answer area is ruled lines under one blank hexagon. Children must write moves like "erase BF, draw AE", a notation that has only appeared in a caption on page 5.
- Problem 7 (all 14 hexagon fillings, 17 boards over two pages) is a long cataloguing job.

### Patterns found (2–3)

1. **Record-keeping.** Problem 7, pages 7–8: fourteen hexagon fillings drawn on roughly 1 in boards, then checked for repeats. Third graders will enjoy the first eight. After that the work is mostly comparing letter pairs to confirm duplicates. It is acceptable as the last, optional problem, but it is bookkeeping.
2. **Precision.** Low. Fillings are robust to wobble. The tracing-paper flip (trace six dots, six letters and the kept lines, then draw the new one) is the fiddliest step. Rehearse it.
3. **Procedure instead of choices.** Low. Problem 6 asks for choices (which flip next) with a clear goal.
4. **Told, not felt.** Problem 4 ("get back to this filling after an odd number of changes"). Third graders have no reason to expect odd to be hard, so the significance of a 5-step return has to be announced by the adult. Finding a loop home is still fun in itself.
5. **The adult runs it.** Low. Problem 5 has four sentences plus a caption. A mathematician will probably read it aloud and demonstrate one join, which is appropriate.

**Most likely delight.** Problem 5, when the joins close up into a five-sided loop. A runner-up comes in Problem 1: the moment a child realizes every hexagon filling has 4 triangles, however different it looks.

## Grades 4–5 (two fourth graders and one fifth grader, organizer)

**Activity: strong.** Problem 4 is the heart: three hexagon starts that look about equally far from the fan at A are in fact 3, 1 and 2 flips away, with "Explain why no shorter routes can work." That gives a ten-year-old a concrete lower-bound argument: each flip can add at most one line at A. Problems 5–7 push the same idea to an octagon, then a general rule, then connectivity. Each step uses the one before, so the deep questions grow out of drawing.

**Page: strong.** Boards are large, tasks are two to four sentences, and the move notation is demonstrated in Problem 3. The ruled answer areas in Problems 4–5 support the move-word record ("erase AD, draw CE ...") that keeps the reasoning recoverable while the board is erased and redrawn. Minor gaps:

- Problem 7 has no recording area for a route of five or more flips.
- Problems 1–2 repeat the middle band's warm-up. That is fine for this group, but quick children will move through them fast.

### Patterns found (4–5)

1. **Record-keeping.** Low. Problem 1's collection serves Problem 3's map.
2. **Precision.** Problems 5 and 7 (octagons with five diagonals crowded near D or E): lines nearly coincide near the shared corner on the 2.5 in drawings of Problem 7. Children may misread which corner a line ends at. Working on the large blank octagons avoids this.
3. **Procedure.** Problem 5 ("to a fan at E", five flips) and Problem 7 are long chains of redrawing. Every step involves a choice, and the interest lies in the count and the argument, so this is mild.
4. **Told, not felt.** Problem 6's claim "for every number of corners" can only be argued, not seen. Children can test hexagon, heptagon and octagon, which is enough to feel the rule. The general proof will be a conversation led by the organizer, as intended.
5. **The adult runs it.** Low for a table of three with the organizer.

**Most likely delight.** Problem 4's middle start, the triangle ACE that looks far from the fan but is one flip away. Close behind is Problem 6's realization that "you can tell the answer before flipping: just count the lines at A."

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1 page 1, shared rule.** Replace "Every corner of every triangle must be a printed dot" with a rule a child can check by eye: "Draw straight lines from dot to dot. Lines may not cross." This makes crossings visibly wrong, so a partner can catch the star instead of the parent.
2. **K–1 Problem 6, move it earlier.** Place the five-step loop right after Problem 2. The children will have just drawn the five pentagon fillings, and the loop is the K–1 table's best moment, so it shouldn't sit where they won't reach it. Hexagon Problems 3–5 follow for those who continue.
3. **K–1 Problem 5, enlarge and clarify the boards.** Print the result boards at Problem 4's size (three per row) and say in the task how to show the change: "Cross out one grey line and draw a new one." Keep both starting drawings. If this doesn't fit on one page, use one starting drawing per page.
4. **2–3 Problem 3 and 4–5 Problem 2, state the recording convention.** Use the same "cross out one grey line and draw a new one" wording, or print the result boards empty. As written, a child can't tell how to show a removed printed line.
5. **K–1 Problem 2, make "different" checkable.** Add one heard sentence to the shared rule: "Ways are different when their lines join different numbers." The board count can stay at six.
6. **K–1, give the parent an answer key.** The answers are 5 (Problem 2), 2 and 4 (Problem 4), and 3 results from each drawing (Problem 5). This belongs in the facilitator guide, not on the page. Without it the parent can't end an "every way" search.
7. **2–3 Problem 6, give a drawing record.** Replace the ruled lines with three small blank hexagons per route. Or keep the lines, but move the "erase AC, draw BD" example from Problem 5's caption to the shared rules on page 1, so the notation is known before it's needed.
8. **2–3 Problem 4, make the goal natural.** Change to "Get back to this filling without making any filling twice." The odd count is then something the child finds. The adult can still point out that 5 is odd.
9. **4–5 Problem 7, add a recording area.** Add ruled lines under the blank octagon, as in Problems 4–5, for the move word.
10. **2–3 Problem 7.** No page change needed. It is last, and the route through this band is otherwise strong. In the guide, suggest the pair split the 14 cases (for example, by whether line AD is used) and pool them, so the catalog doesn't become a solo transcription job.

None of the K–1 weaknesses requires a different activity. Fixes 1–3 and 5 are page changes. Fix 6 is a guide change. Together they could bring K–1 close to the bar. Whether kindergartners can rule lines steadily enough needs a rehearsal with real five-year-olds.
