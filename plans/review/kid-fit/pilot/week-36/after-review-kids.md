I looked at all fifteen pages, rendered from `draft/*.pdf` into `renders/`. These are judgments made before any pilot. Some questions only a rehearsal or pilot can answer: whether kindergartners can hold both attribute tests at once; whether a partner catches a bad three; whether striped and solid stay easy to tell apart on the printed card stock; how long Problems 2–4 take at each table; and whether children notice that the game always lasts four moves.

The core action is good for children. Each child has nine large card tiles (60 mm) and moves them into threes, and each try gets a yes or no right away. The tiles are big enough for small hands, and nobody has to cut anything during the session. The weak point is the same in every band: the materials do not enforce the rule. A three is allowed or not only because someone checks both shape and fill. For the older bands that check is the mathematics, and a partner or third player can do it. At K–1 it is the whole difficulty.

---

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** The moves are concrete. In Problem 1, "Pick a third tile for each pair to make an allowed three," the child puts a real tile in a big box. In Problem 2, "Put all nine tiles into three allowed threes," a sort-by-shape attempt is an allowed split right away. First graders with an adult can probably get absorbed. The trouble is the judging. Two of the four printed pairs, striped circle + solid triangle and striped triangle + open square, need a "missing value" on both attributes at once. For a five-year-old, fills that are "all different" is a second simultaneous test, and the near miss with "two the same" fills is the error they will keep making.

**Page: workable.** Every working page has a large 60 mm mat or answer box that a real tile fits on, and each prompt is one or two sentences an adult can read once. The page as written causes three problems:

- Page 1 is a rules paragraph and a labelled example, with no task on it. The words "all different" and "two the same" in the example are unreadable to this table.
- Problem 3 says "keep it in the middle of the table," but its own 3×3 mat on page 4 has a middle square.
- On page 5, Problem 4's board lays the tiles out in rows by shape and columns by fill. Children will treat it as tic-tac-toe.

**K–1 bar: not met.** The core move takes no reading, and a child can place a tile within a minute. What keeps the bar out of reach:

- Neither the tiles nor the partner reliably enforce the rule. The partner who "checks each three" needs exactly the two-attribute fluency that is in doubt.
- In practice the parent volunteer becomes the referee for every three.
- The facilitator material's own readiness gate ("a child must reliably apply both attribute tests independently") is marked as not yet performed.

### Patterns found (K–1)

- **5, the adult runs it (p. 2, Problem 1; p. 5, Problem 4).** At minute 5 the hands are busy: tiles go into the boxes under each pair. The decision ("is this one right?") goes to the adult, because the partner cannot be trusted to check fills and shapes separately. In Problem 4 the partner "looks for an allowed three" among four or five chosen tiles. That means up to ten triples, each checked on two attributes. A five- or six-year-old will glance and say "no." The adult ends up doing the search.
- **2, noise hides the pattern (p. 5, Problem 4).** This is a checking error, not a measuring error. The striking fact is that you cannot keep more than four, and it shows only if every allowed three among five tiles gets caught. When a child misses a three, the table proudly reports five or six, and the fact disappears.
- **4, told, not felt (p. 5, Problem 4; also p. 1).** The printed board's rows and columns are all allowed threes. Children will avoid straight lines and miss the four bent ones, for example open circle, solid triangle, striped square. Page 1 staggers the allowed example to show that "positions do not matter," but on page 5 the adult will still have to announce that a bent three counts.
- **1 and 3: not found.** Nothing gets written down; the tiles are the record. No step-by-step procedure.

**Child at the table.**

- **Minute 5:** filling the four Problem 1 boxes. The first two pairs are easy: open circle + striped circle, and open circle + open triangle each differ in only one attribute. The last two take real thought.
- **Minute 15:** in Problem 2, sorting all nine tiles by shape works at once ("all the circles!"). Sorting by fill works too. A third way stalls.
- **Minute 30:** in Problem 4, choosing tiles is fun, but the "is there a three?" check is the adult's.
- **Would they ask to do it again?** Trading pairs in Problem 1 ("Then give your partner new pairs") is the part they would ask to repeat.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 2. Sorting the nine tiles into three rows by shape turns out to be allowed. Then the same tiles, sorted by fill, are allowed too. A partner seeing both splits side by side, made from two sets, is a small surprise children can see for themselves.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Nine tiles with two attributes are well within reach of third graders. The questions are real hunts:

- Problem 1 asks "can any pair have two different answers?"
- Problem 3, "Choose as many tiles as you can with no allowed three," turns into a hunting game when "Your partner looks for an allowed three in your tiles."
- Problem 5's game is rigged: every legal game lasts exactly four moves, and the second player always wins. Children can find that out by playing, which is the best kind of discovery for this age.

The second mathematician can referee the checks without taking over.

**Page: workable.** The prompts are short and open. Three things on the page get in the way:

- Problem 1's answer boxes on page 1 are about 22 mm, so a 60 mm tile does not fit and children have to draw the answer.
- Problem 2 (p. 2, six printed 3×3 boards) and Problem 4 (p. 4, two boards) ask children to record threes on printed grids, with no demonstrated way to mark a bent three.
- Problem 5 (p. 5) prints four record boards for a game whose only result is a move count.

### Patterns found (2–3)

- **1, hands-on turning into record-keeping (p. 2, Problem 2; p. 4, Problem 4; p. 5, Problem 5).** In Problem 2 the child builds a split with tiles, then has to transfer it onto a small printed grid: circle cells, colour them, or letter them. A split with bent threes becomes a tangle of loops. With six boards printed, children may also believe there are six splits; there are four. In Problem 5 the four record grids record a game that needs no record, since "how many moves?" is the whole question. At minute 15 a child copying a split onto paper is doing clerical work, not mathematics.
- **2, noise hides the pattern (p. 3, Problem 3).** The maximum of four is felt only if the partner catches every three among five tiles. Third graders are better at this than K–1, but a missed bent three still gives a false "five." The partner's hunt makes the checking a game, which helps.
- **4, told, not felt (p. 3, Problem 3; p. 4, Problem 4).** The rows-and-columns layout invites tic-tac-toe thinking. In Problem 4, "Find all the allowed threes that use it": on the printed board, the centre tile looks as if it lies on four lines, a corner on three and an edge tile on two. In fact every tile lies on four allowed threes. A child working from the picture gets the wrong count unless an adult steps in.
- **3 and 5: not found.** The game moves are free choices, and the reading load suits third graders.

**Child at the table.**

- **Minute 5:** completing pairs and inventing stumpers for a partner.
- **Minute 15:** building splits with tiles; this is fun until the recording.
- **Minute 30:** either the five-tile hunt ("I bet you can't find one" / "found it!") or several quick games of Problem 5.
- **Would they ask to do it again?** Yes, especially the game.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 5. After three or four games the table notices the second player always wins and every game stops at four tiles. "It's rigged" is a gasp children make themselves.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** Every question is an explanation ten-year-olds can actually reach with tiles in hand:

- Problem 1: "Can two different tiles have two completing tiles, or none? Explain."
- Problem 2: why five is impossible. Pigeonhole on shapes is a natural first step: three tiles of one shape always form a three.
- Problem 3: the four threes through a tile.
- Problem 4: the four splits.
- Problem 5: why the game cannot stop early.

The three-child table matches Problem 5's "a third player checks each move," which puts the referee role into the rules. The organizer is at this table for the proofs.

**Page: workable.** The wording is clean and leaves the method to the child. The page's weaknesses:

- **Order.** The hardest explanation, Problem 2 on page 2 ("Explain why a larger collection is impossible"), comes before the facts that make it approachable: every tile in four threes (Problem 3) and every legal three-tile collection having fourth tiles (Problem 5).
- **Recording.** Problem 3 (p. 3) and Problem 4 (p. 4) repeat the 2–3 recording-grid issue: six boards for four splits, and no way shown to mark a bent three.
- **Two questions in one.** Problem 3 joins two separate questions. The second, "How many tiles can they share?", is a one-word answer.

### Patterns found (4–5)

- **1, hands-on turning into record-keeping (p. 4, Problem 4; p. 3, Problem 3).** "Find every split, and explain why your list is complete" needs a record. The six printed grids make the record a transcription job, with tangles for bent threes. A list written in words, or two tile sets kept side by side, would serve the completeness argument better than shading grids.
- **2, noise hides the pattern (p. 2, Problem 2).** This is milder here, because these children can check all pairs systematically. A missed three still gives a false five, and the proof then argues against a counterexample the child thinks they have.
- **4, told, not felt (p. 3, Problem 3).** As at 2–3, the grid layout suggests that tiles lie on different numbers of lines. For a 4–5 child this can be productive: "the picture lies, the tiles don't." But someone has to raise it, so it is listed here.
- **3 and 5: not found.** Reading is a paragraph at most, and the game rules sit in one problem.

**Child at the table.**

- **Minute 5:** completing pairs and arguing that the answer is forced, one attribute at a time.
- **Minute 15:** hunting for a five-tile collection and failing. A child who reaches the pigeonhole step is beginning a real proof.
- **Minute 30:** either stuck on the full impossibility proof (risk of stalling) or playing Problem 5 with a referee and noticing the fixed length.
- **Would they ask to do it again?** Yes, the game and the "find five" challenge.

**Most likely delight:** in Problem 2, trying all five remaining tiles against a four-tile 2×2 rectangle and watching every one make a three. Close behind is Problem 5's discovery that the game is rigged.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1, p. 1 rules and all problems: start with shapes only.** Put the shapes-only version first: same pages, with fills ignored, so the child judges one attribute and a partner can really check. Bring in fills only after one problem has worked. The rule is stated once on page 1, so this is one rule change plus one added problem line. Example: Problem 2 repeated as "Now the fills must work too." It keeps the outline's mathematics, since the facilitator material already gives shapes-only as the fallback. This is the change most likely to let a K–1 partner, rather than the parent, enforce the rule. Whether even that meets the K–1 bar for kindergartners is something only a pilot can settle. If the organizer wants both attributes from the start for K–1, that is **outline: for the organizer**.
2. **K–1, p. 4, Problem 3: use the mat's middle square.** Change "keep it in the middle of the table" to "Put one tile in the middle square." The 3×3 mat then holds the answer: the other eight tiles fit around it in four opposite pairs, and "all" means every square is filled. The mat, not the adult, shows when they are done.
3. **2–3 p. 2, Problem 2 and 4–5 p. 4, Problem 4: cut the transcription.** Print fewer record boards: two at 2–3, four at 4–5 or none. Let a found split be kept in tiles by using the pair's second set, or recorded in the guide's way. At least, do not print six boards for a four-answer question; at 2–3 that reads as "there are six."
4. **2–3 p. 5, Problem 5: drop the record grids.** Remove the four record grids, or replace them with one 60 mm play mat as at 4–5. The game needs tiles and a move count, not a record.
5. **2–3 p. 1 and 4–5 p. 1, Problem 1: make the answer boxes tile-sized.** Enlarge the answer boxes to 60 mm, as at K–1, so the child places a real tile instead of drawing one. This may push Problem 1 to page 2; that is acceptable.
6. **4–5, Problem 2: move it later.** Move the "Explain why a larger collection is impossible" problem after Problem 3 (threes through a tile), so the hardest proof comes after the counting facts it can use. Stopping anywhere is still fine.
7. **K–1 p. 5, 2–3 p. 3, 4–5 p. 2: flag bent threes in the launch.** The rows-and-columns board invites tic-tac-toe thinking. The smallest change is in the guide, not the page. At the launch, show a bent three from the board itself, using the page 1 example (open circle, solid triangle, striped square) placed on page 5's layout, so children feel it before the cap-set search. A page alternative is to scatter the nine tiles on the selection board instead of a grid. That changes the board, so it should be checked against the cut-tile page, which is the same 60 mm grid.
8. **4–5 p. 3, Problem 3: split the questions.** Either cut "How many tiles can they share?" or move it into the guide as an adult follow-up. It is a separate small question tacked onto a substantial one.
