Basis: all 13 rendered pages (K–1 4 pp., 2–3 4 pp., 4–5 5 pp.) and PROMPT.md. Not piloted. The verdicts are a filter: they assume 60 mm card-stock tiles cut by an adult, one set per child, and the launch described in the outline.

The whole week runs on one physical action: hold loose shape-and-fill tiles and pick, sort or add tiles so that each group of three is "all the same or all different" in shape and in fill. That action is good for children. The tiles are big, discrete and sortable, and mistakes in cutting or placing them do not matter. The weak point is the one the first sessions warned about. The rule lives only in the child's head. Nothing in the tiles stops a three with "two the same", which is exactly the error the near miss shows. The question in every band is who catches that error: the child, a partner, or the adult.

---

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** Completing a pair ("Pick a third tile for each pair to make an allowed three", p. 1) and sorting all nine tiles into threes ("Put all nine tiles into three allowed threes. Find different ways.", p. 2) are things a five-year-old can do with their hands. A child who sees "two circles, so a circle; open and striped, so the other one" can be right within a minute. The difficulty is judging two features at once and spotting the "two the same" near miss. The outline's own readiness gate for this is unperformed, and its fallback is shapes only. With a parent volunteer who is not a mathematician, a child's wrong three will often go uncaught.

**Page: workable.** The pages are sparse, and the adult reads one sentence per problem. The mats on pp. 2 and 4 have 60 mm cells that hold the real tiles. Two things work against a non-reader. The rule panel on p. 1 draws the three fills as three small squares ("fills □ ■ ▨ all different"). A child who has just learned the shape rule sees "square, square, square". The Problem 1 answer boxes are about 23 mm across, too small for a 60 mm tile, so a child who solved the pair with a tile then has to draw a striped triangle instead of placing it.

**K–1 bar: not met.** A child can make the core move within a minute, but the materials do not enforce the rule, and the page does not make the partner the checker. The parent volunteer therefore becomes the referee for every three. The two-feature judgement is also not yet known to be within reach for kindergartners; only a readiness check at the table can settle that.

Minute by minute, for a kindergartner:
- **Minute 5:** placing tiles beside the four printed pairs. The first pair (open circle, striped circle) is easy. Striped circle with solid triangle needs both "missing" values at once, and the child will likely offer a solid or striped square and look at the adult.
- **Minute 15:** sorting tiles on the mat. Sorting by shape comes almost at once, and sorting by fill comes soon after. The two splits in which every three is all different are unlikely without an adult prompt. Each new way wipes out the last one, which is fine at this age.
- **Minute 30:** Problem 3 or 4. Both ask the child to check every three in a pile for an allowed three. A five-year-old cannot do that alone, so the child waits while the adult checks.

Patterns:
1. *Record-keeping:* none of note. Nothing is tallied or tabulated.
2. *Precision:* none. The tiles are discrete. Whether the striped and solid prints stay distinguishable on the card-stock printer is a check for the person preparing the tiles.
3. *Procedure:* mild, in Problem 4 (p. 4), "then try other middle tiles". Repeating the eight completions for each middle tile is the same move over and over. The payoff, that every middle tile gets four, comes only after two or three full rounds.
4. *Told, not felt:* Problem 3 (p. 3), "How many can you keep?" A child can feel that four is easy and five keeps failing. But a child cannot tell that a five-tile pile really contains a three, especially a bent one, so whether 5 is impossible is settled by the adult's word.
5. *Adult runs it:* the main risk, on pp. 1, 3 and 4. Every claimed three, and every "no three in my pile", needs a check of both features. The page names no checker, so the parent volunteer does it.

**Most likely delight:** in Problem 2, finding that the tiles also split into threes that are "all different and all different", groups that look wrong but are allowed. A second possibility is in Problem 4: around any middle tile, the other eight pair off into exactly four threes.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Four third graders with a mathematician, SET-like tiles and a real hunt make a good table. The hunt is "Choose as many tiles as you can with no allowed three among them. How do you know you cannot keep more?" (p. 3). The table also has a game whose surprise children find by playing: "Can your choices change how many moves the game lasts?" (p. 4). Every game ends after four tiles, and the second player always wins. Third graders will notice that before anyone tells them and will argue about going second. In the game, each player has a reason to catch the other's illegal tile, so the opponent enforces the rule.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1, 3 and 4 are clear goals that leave the method open. Problem 2 asks the children to "Find different splits", but it gives one blank mat and nowhere to keep a split. A child cannot tell whether a new split repeats an earlier one, because the earlier one is gone. Page 4 puts two mini boards under Problem 4 and two under Problem 5 with no stated role. For the game, marking the printed boards is clumsier than playing with tiles, and two boards run out after two games.

Minute by minute, for a third grader:
- **Minute 5:** completing the four pairs, then setting pairs for a partner. This goes quickly. The question "can any pair have two different answers?" gives the trading a purpose.
- **Minute 15:** splits. Splits by shape and by fill come first. Then comes the hunt for "another one", which needs a way to keep the earlier splits.
- **Minute 30:** building a four-tile pile and trying to add a fifth tile, with the partner hunting for a three. Then the game, where the gasp is "it stopped at four again".

Patterns:
1. *Record-keeping:* mild. The two mini boards per problem on p. 4 ask for marking threes on a printed grid. Bent threes cross the rows and columns, and no marking convention is shown.
2. *Precision:* none.
3. *Procedure:* none. Every problem is a search or a game.
4. *Told, not felt:* little. The fixed game length and the four threes through each tile can be felt with tiles. The maximum of four is felt through failed fives. "How do you know you cannot keep more?" is a real explanation task that the mathematician will have to support. That task is appropriate.
5. *Adult runs it:* moderate in Problem 3. Checking whether a claimed five-tile pile really has a three, especially a bent one, will often fall to the adult. Once the rule is secure, the game itself is enforced by the players.

**Most likely delight:** in Problem 5 (p. 4), noticing that the game always lasts four moves and the second player always wins, whatever anyone does.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** With the organizer at the table, three explanations are within reach of ten-year-olds using tiles they can hold:
- Problem 1 (p. 1): "Can two different tiles have two completing tiles, or none? Explain." Each feature has exactly one choice.
- Problem 3 (p. 3): "Find as many allowed threes through one chosen tile as possible, and explain why you have them all." The other eight tiles pair off.
- Problem 4 (p. 4): "Find every split, and explain why your list is complete."

Each later problem builds on the earlier ones. Problem 2's maximum argument is hard, but it rewards a child who likes cases.

**Page: workable.** The prompts are short and substantial. Problem 4 gives six mini boards for recording splits but no convention for marking three groups, and the bent threes make loops tangle across the printed grid. Problem 5 is a solo routine: "Add one unused tile at a time... Can different choices make you stop at different numbers of tiles?" Grades 2–3 got this as a two-player game. This table has a pair plus a referee, and a game would give each child a role.

Minute by minute, for a fourth grader:
- **Minute 5:** the four pairs are done fast. The child is writing a short explanation of why the completing tile is unique.
- **Minute 15:** building four-tile piles, failing at five, and starting to argue why.
- **Minute 30:** around one tile, laying out the four threes and seeing every other tile used exactly once. Then splits, looking for the fourth one.

Patterns:
1. *Record-keeping:* mild. Six mini boards in Problem 4 (p. 4) and two in Problem 3 (p. 3) ask children to mark several threes per board with no convention given.
2. *Precision:* none.
3. *Procedure:* Problem 5 (p. 5) as printed. Adding tiles until stuck, again and again, is a routine whose point appears only after several runs.
4. *Told, not felt:* Problem 2 (p. 2), "Explain why a larger collection is impossible". The ten-pair counting argument is an adult's proof. Children can feel the maximum but will need the organizer to steer any full explanation. That is acceptable for this table.
5. *Adult runs it:* low. Fourth and fifth graders can check both features reliably.

**Most likely delight:** in Problem 3, choosing any tile and finding that the other eight always pair up into exactly four threes through it, even for a corner tile, where the printed grid shows only three.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1 p. 1, Problems 1 and 3 (also 2–3 Problem 3): make the partner the checker.** Add one short clause, for example to Problem 1, "Your partner checks each three", and to K–1 Problem 3, "Your partner looks for an allowed three in your tiles." This moves rule enforcement from the parent volunteer to a player with a reason to catch errors, which is what made the tiling session work. It does not by itself meet the K–1 bar: whether kindergartners can judge both features is still the readiness gate in the outline, and if the gate fails, the outline's shapes-only fallback applies (outline: for the organizer).
2. **All bands, p. 1 rule panel: redraw the fill swatches.** The "fills" row shows three small squares, which reads as "shapes all the same". Draw fills as short, wide fill bars, or as the actual tiles shrunk, so the fill row shows no repeated shape. This matters most at K–1, where the picture is the rule.
3. **K–1 p. 1, Problem 1: make the answer box hold a real tile.** Give Problem 1 its own page with the four pairs at tile size (60 mm cells), so the child places the completing tile instead of drawing it. K–1 has room for a fifth page.
4. **Grades 2–3 p. 2, Problem 2: give a place to keep splits.** Replace or supplement the single mat with four mini boards, as in 4–5 Problem 4, so "Find different splits" has a record to compare against.
5. **Grades 2–3 and 4–5 mini boards (2–3 p. 4; 4–5 pp. 3–4): show the marking convention.** Demonstrate it at the launch: three colours, or the letters A, B, C written in the cells, never loops. Bent threes cross the printed rows and columns. This is a launch and guide item; the problem text can stay.
6. **Grades 4–5 p. 5, Problem 5: make it the two-player game.** Use the 2–3 wording ("Take turns adding... The first player who cannot move loses") and keep "Explain why you can or cannot get stuck early." The third child referees. The mathematics is unchanged, and the routine becomes play.
7. **Grades 2–3 p. 4: separate Problems 4 and 5.** Give the game its own page, played with tiles, with at most a few small boards for saving a final four-tile collection. That leaves Problem 4's two boards unambiguous.
8. **K–1 order: put Problem 4 (the middle tile) before Problem 3 (the most tiles).** Problem 4 repeats the completion move with all eight other tiles, and the tiles almost check themselves. Problem 3 needs every three in a pile checked, which is the hardest check for this age. Stopping anywhere stays fine.
9. **Grades 4–5 p. 3, Problem 3: reorder the two questions.** Ask for the threes through one tile first, then "How many tiles can two different allowed threes share?", so the search comes before the general question.
10. **All bands, p. 1 demo: give the arrow a role or remove it.** The allowed example has an arrow from the triangle to the square, and the near miss has none. If the arrow means "completes the pair", draw it in both examples the same way Problem 1 does.

## Only a rehearsal or pilot can settle

- Whether kindergartners and first graders can judge shape and fill together, including the "two the same" near miss.
- Whether striped and solid stay distinct on the card stock actually printed.
- How long Problem 1 takes at each table.
- Whether children find the two all-different splits without a prompt.
- How many times third graders want to replay a game whose length never changes.
- Whether false five-tile piles are caught by children or only by adults.
- Whether four children each with nine 60 mm tiles and a 180 mm mat fit on the K–1 table.
