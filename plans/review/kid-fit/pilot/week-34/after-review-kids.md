Kid-fit review of the Week 34 "Hidden turns" draft (W34-*-v4). I rendered all three PDFs (17 pages) and read every page. Nobody has watched children use this packet, so these verdicts are a filter and not a pilot result. Counts quoted below come from a quick enumeration I ran myself. Among two-kind patterns, 0 of 8 work on the 3-ring, 0 of 16 on the 4-ring, 0 of 32 on the 5-ring, 12 of 64 on the 6-ring and 96 of 256 on the 8-ring.

## The shared mechanic

Every band uses the same cycle. Build a ring of counters, make a tracing copy with coloured-pencil dots, then the partner lifts the copy and turns or flips it, hunting for a match. The tester's side is a real game: the tester wants to catch the builder, and a match is something you can see. That is the activity's strength.

The cost sits between building and testing. **Every new or changed pattern needs a freshly coloured copy before anyone can test it.** On a 3- or 4-ring that takes a minute. On the 8- and 9-rings it means colouring eight or nine dots in the right colours for each attempt. Declaring "no match" also means checking every turn and every flip: 7 checks on the 4-ring, 11 on the 6-ring and 15 on the 8-ring, each comparing every spot.

Only a rehearsal can settle these points:
- whether coloured-pencil dots on tracing paper can be read over coloured counters and again from the back after a flip;
- whether lifting and turning the sheet over raised counters disturbs them (the guide marks this as an untested prediction);
- how long a five-year-old takes to make one copy;
- whether 20 mm counters sit comfortably in the nine-ring's spots, which almost touch;
- whether young testers actually try every move before they give up.

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** Putting counters on "this ring" is immediate and pleasant. Turning a see-through sheet until "every dot sit[s] on its own color" is a physical, checkable action. Physical counterexamples are the point at this level, and they come naturally: the partner flips the sheet and it matches. Holding it back from strong is that the build-then-test cycle passes through copy-making and an exhaustive check, which five-year-olds will hand to the parent.

**Page: workable.** Each problem is one or two read-aloud sentences over a large ring, which suits this table. But Problem 1 ("Can you make one your partner cannot match?", 3-ring, two kinds) can never be won by the builder. Problems 4–6 also ask essentially the same question on 5-, 6- and 8-rings, and each needs more checks than the last.

**K–1 bar: not met.** A child who reads nothing can place counters within a minute. But they cannot make the core move (a test) until a copy exists. The materials do not enforce three of the rules:
- "keeps the counters still";
- "A whole turn does not count" (a child laying the copy back where it started will shout "match");
- the requirement that every spot match, which near-matches will tempt them to wave through.

Calling "nobody can match it" requires trying every turn and flip. That is beyond a kindergartner's patience, so the parent volunteer ends up as referee for every claim.

**Patterns found**
1. *Record-keeping:* a mild form on all pages. The copy is a tool and not a table, but a child must colour one for every attempt (p. 3, Problem 3: each one-counter change needs a new copy). Problem 3 also asks for "changes that let a turn or flip match and changes that do not", but there are 8 possible changes and nowhere to keep track of which were tried.
2. *Precision:* low risk. Spots are big (about 28 mm) and positions are discrete. Dots placed by eye only need to land near a spot. Whether they show from the back is a rehearsal question.
3. *Procedure instead of choices:* pp. 5–6, Problems 5–6. The builder's choices are quick. The tester then runs 11 or 15 comparisons, and "no match" only shows after the last one. A K child will stop after a few tries and declare victory, or concede too early.
4. *Told, not felt:* p. 1, Problem 1 and p. 4, Problem 4. "Two kinds never work on a small ring" is felt only if the child has truly tried every move on every pattern. Otherwise the adult announces it. A match, when it happens, is felt.
5. *The adult runs it:* the opening rules paragraph carries six rules at once (fill, copy with matching colours, roles, keep still, turn or flip, whole turn does not count). Copy-making and the exhaustive check will also drift to the adult. On p. 3 the icons (black dot, ring, square) have no stated link to the counter kinds, so the parent must decide how to read them.

**Most likely delight:** the tester flips the copy over and every dot drops onto its colour ("Got you!"), especially when the builder was sure it was safe. Problem 3 adds the reverse: one swapped counter, and suddenly nothing matches.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Four third graders with a mathematician can run the builder-against-tester game themselves. "Find how few kinds of counter can make a pattern on this ring that no turn or flip can match" gives the answer 3 on the 3-, 4- and 5-rings. Problem 4 ("using only two kinds", six-ring) then overturns the pattern. The 6-ring success can be found by trying (12 of 64 patterns work), so the surprise is felt, not announced. Problem 5's "How few counters of one kind can it have?" has only four placements of two counters to try on the 8-ring, so children can check it by hand.

**Page: workable.** It is clear and uncluttered, but:
- Problems 1–3 repeat the same sentence on three separate pages. The 3-ring case (Problem 1) is likely a two- or three-minute task for third graders.
- The rules contain the cryptic "Only counter kinds and spots count."
- The two ruled lines for "Explain how you know" are tight.

It is close to strong.

**Patterns found**
1. *Record-keeping:* making a copy for every candidate (p. 3, 5-ring; p. 5, 8-ring) is the main chore. The kept copies do serve as the record of what was tried, which helps with "why fewer kinds would fail".
2. *Precision:* not found.
3. *Procedure:* p. 5, Problem 5. Fifteen checks for each 8-ring candidate is long. Third graders will want to start reasoning ("there's a mirror line through these two") rather than checking, and the mathematician can welcome that.
4. *Told, not felt:* low risk. Every key fact can be found on the rings provided.
5. *The adult runs it:* not expected beyond the launch, apart from that one cryptic rule sentence.

**Most likely delight:** after three rings that each needed three kinds, two kinds suddenly work on the bigger 6-ring ("bigger is easier?").

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** With the organizer at the table, "Can you make a two-kind pattern that no turn or flip can match on any ring with at least six spots? Test your idea on this eight-ring, then explain why it always works" is a genuine construction-and-proof task. Children can test it physically on the 7- and 9-rings. Problem 3 ("Find an explanation that also works for the three-ring and four-ring") gives the small-ring failures a single reason, which suits fourth and fifth graders.

**Page: strong.** Each problem is a short task with large rings and a real answer box. The questions are open, and the sequence carries the development. Two minor issues:
- Problem 1 (3-ring) will take four- and fifth-graders only a couple of minutes.
- On the extra workspace (p. 6), the seven-ring and nine-ring share vertical space and the nine-ring's spots almost touch. A tracing sheet over one ring will lie across part of the other and its counters.

**Patterns found**
1. *Record-keeping:* p. 5 and p. 6. Colouring 8- and 9-dot copies for each attempt is tedious. These children will mostly replace copying with reasoning, which is the intended direction.
2. *Precision:* not found.
3. *Procedure:* p. 5. Checking 15–17 moves is a procedure, but the problem asks for an explanation that replaces it.
4. *Told, not felt:* low risk on the page. "Always works" for every ring size cannot be seen by building and must be argued, but that is the explicit task and the organizer is there.
5. *The adult runs it:* not expected. Three children rotating builder, tester and referee fits the mechanic.

**Most likely delight:** a pattern that beat the 6-ring also survives the 7-, 8- and 9-rings, so the children are holding something that seems to work forever.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **All bands, p. 1 rules (and the launch):** cut the copy cost. Add one sentence of rule: "Make a copy when you think your partner cannot match your pattern." Children can still build and rearrange freely, and only bets get traced. For K–1, if a rehearsal shows copies take more than a couple of minutes, let the parent make the copies and keep the turning and flipping with the children. That keeps the children's hands on the core move. Rehearse dot visibility over counters and from the back before printing.
2. **K–1, p. 1, Problem 1:** give the builder a chance to win early. Change it to "Make different patterns on this ring with two kinds of counter, then with three kinds. Which ones can your partner not match?" The 3-ring two-kind failure stays, and the first builder win moves from about minute 15 to the first few minutes.
3. **K–1, pp. 5–6, Problems 5–6:** the page can partly rescue these. Combine them into one problem with both rings ("Make a pattern with two kinds of counter that no turn or flip can match, on this ring or the big one"), so a child chooses one ring rather than repeating the same long check twice. The exhaustive check itself is beyond most kindergartners: the parent should treat "no match found" claims as "we couldn't catch it". The guide should say so. A page change cannot make 11–15 checks K-sized. Outline: for the organizer, on whether rings past six belong in K–1 at all.
4. **K–1, p. 3, Problem 3:** add a small key beside the ring showing the three marks as "your three kinds of counter", or print the problem text as "Build this pattern with your three kinds". The parent then need not decide how to read the icons.
5. **Grades 2–3 and 4–5, p. 1 rules:** replace "Only counter kinds and spots count." with "Which way a counter faces does not matter." Or delete it, if the launch covers it.
6. **Grades 4–5, p. 6:** put the seven-ring and the nine-ring on separate halves of the page with clear space between them, or on separate pages. A tracing sheet can then cover one ring without lying on the other's counters.
7. **Grades 2–3, pp. 1–2, Problems 1–2:** consider placing the 3-ring and the 4-ring under one Problem 1 ("Find how few kinds … on each ring"), so the first numbered problem is a full five minutes. Renumber the rest.
8. **Grades 2–3, pp. 4–5:** enlarge the explanation space from two lines to a small box like the 4–5 pages. Problem 4 asks children to "Explain how you know", and some will draw mirror lines.
