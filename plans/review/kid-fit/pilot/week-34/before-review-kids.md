## The core move, for all bands

A child puts counters on the spots of a printed ring. Then they make a tracing copy: trace every spot circle and colour each one to match its counter. To test, they lift the copy, turn it or flip it, and look to see whether every coloured circle sits on a counter of the same colour. The tracing paper does a good job of enforcing the motion rules, because it can only move rigidly, much as pattern blocks enforced tiling. The page leaves three things unenforced:

- **Each new pattern costs a new copy.** That means tracing and colouring n circles, which is 3 to 9 circles for every attempt.
- **The goal is to show that something never happens.** "No turn or flip can match" only holds once all 2n − 1 moves have been tried: 5 on the three-ring, 11 on the six-ring, 15 on the eight-ring. A child can't tell when they're done, and when they win, nothing visible happens.
- **The opponent isn't built into the rules.** The partner who tries to find a match is what makes the activity a game. It appears in K–1 Problems 1 and 5 and in 2–3 Problem 2, but it isn't part of the shared rules.

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** Placing counters and turning a see-through copy until it "clicks" into place is a good hands-on move, and on the three- and four-rings the hiding game ("Can you make one your partner cannot match?", Problem 1) has few enough moves for a five-year-old to try them all. From the five-ring on (Problems 4–6), each candidate takes several minutes of tracing and colouring and then 9 to 15 checks. The real result is that every move fails, and nobody sees that happen.

**Page: weak.** Problems 1, 2, 4 and 5 are clear one-hearing tasks on big rings. Three things pull the page down:
- **Problem 3 is vague.** "Change just one counter in different ways" has no ring of its own and no starting pattern. It sits at the foot of the four-ring page, so the adult has to invent the task.
- **Problem 6 asks too much.** "Build two two-kind patterns that no turn or flip can match and that cannot match each other. Keep each on its own tracing copy." That stacks a second comparison across two copies on top of 15-move testing on an eight-ring.
- **The opening rules are abstract when read aloud.** For example: "Only counter kinds and spots count."

**K–1 bar: not met.** Children can place counters within a minute. What stops them doing the core move themselves:
- **A slow copy step.** Five-year-olds trace and colour 3 to 8 circles for every attempt.
- **A match is judged by eye, spot by spot.** The materials don't settle it.
- **Nobody can see when the testing is finished.** The parent volunteer has to keep asking "did you try every flip?", which means the adult is running the check.

**Patterns found**
1. **Record-keeping (all pages).** The tracing copy is the price of each attempt, not a goal in itself. On the six-ring (p4, Problem 5) a child spends most of an attempt tracing and colouring six circles, and Problem 6 asks them to keep and compare two copies. Ten sheets per pair may run out.
2. **Precision (p1 demo and every test).**
   - **Colours have to read through the paper.** A child compares pencil colour on tracing paper with the counter underneath it, and after a flip the pencil is seen from the back of the sheet. A solidly coloured circle hides the counter beneath. Light colours such as yellow may barely show from the back.
   - **A slip gives a false answer.** One mis-coloured circle creates a false "no match" or a false match.
3. **Procedure (p3–p5, Problems 4–6).** On the five-, six- and eight-rings, the child turns one spot at a time, then flips and turns again, for 9, 11 or 15 checks. The interesting outcome, that every check fails, only arrives at the end.
4. **Told, not felt (p3, Problem 4; p2, Problem 2).** "How few kinds" depends on two kinds failing on every pattern. On the three- and four-rings a child can feel this, because "the flip always catches me". On the five-ring a K–1 child won't run out the cases, so the adult ends up saying "two kinds never works here".
5. **Adult runs it (p1 rules, p2 Problem 3, p5 Problem 6).** The opening is five rule sentences. Problem 3 has to be made up by the adult. In Problem 6 the adult would be the one cross-checking two copies.

**Most likely delight.** In the hiding game on the three- or four-ring, the tester flips the copy and the colours suddenly line up: "Got you." A child who then finds a four-ring pattern with three kinds that the partner can't catch would want to play again.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: workable.** Asking "Find how few kinds of counter…" on the three-, four- and five-rings (Problems 1–3), then "Find a six-ring pattern using only two kinds" (Problem 4), sets up a genuine surprise: the bigger ring needs fewer kinds. Third graders can feel it because they have just failed with two kinds three times. Problem 5 ("How few counters of one kind can it have?") is a good optimisation. What keeps the activity at workable is the cost of each attempt: a fresh copy, then 11 to 15 checks on the larger rings. Each candidate in Problem 5 is a slow loop.

**Page: workable.** Clean, large rings with very little reading, and each ring on its own page. Problems 1–3 repeat the same question as contrasting cases, which suits a sequence where the pattern is supposed to emerge. There are two weaknesses:
- **Problem 4 invites a list.** "Show how you know every possible move fails" suggests an 11-item list of moves, and two writing lines can't hold it.
- **The partner role appears once.** It is only in Problem 2 ("Let someone test your best pattern"), so Problems 1 and 3–5 read as solo checking.

**Patterns found**
1. **Record-keeping (p4, Problem 4).** Children write down a move-by-move list that confirms what the partner's testing already showed. Copies for each candidate (p5, Problem 5) are the same overhead as at K–1, but third graders handle it better.
2. **Precision (all pages).** Same colour-through-paper and flip-from-the-back issues as K–1, but milder: third graders trace and colour more neatly.
3. **Procedure (p4–p5, Problems 4–5).** For each candidate, a child turns the copy through 7 positions and flips it through 8 on the eight-ring. Minimising the minority kind means repeating this for several candidates.
4. **Told, not felt (p5, Problem 5).** A child who finds a three-counter pattern can feel the answer only if they also try the few two-counter placements and see that each has a mirror line. Otherwise the mathematician announces that 3 is the minimum. This is minor: the cases are few and buildable.
5. **Adult runs it.** Not found. Reading is light and the mathematician can let children run it.

**Most likely delight.** Problem 4: after three rings that each needed three kinds, a two-kind six-ring finally beats the tester.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** The arc fits ten-year-olds working with the organizer: small rings with "Can you rule out every pattern with fewer kinds?" (Problem 1), one explanation covering three rings (Problem 3), then "Can you make a two-kind pattern that no turn or flip can match on any ring with at least six spots?" (Problem 5), with seven- and nine-rings to test the idea. Children can check their own conjectures with the tracing copies, and the general construction is a real discovery.

**Page: workable.** Reading level and length are fine. There are two issues:
- **Problem 4 invites a written case list.** "Explain why each turn and flip fails" means 11 separate cases written on two lines.
- **Explanations get little room.** Problem 3 asks for an explanation across three rings in the same two lines.

The extra-workspace page (p6) is a good addition.

**Patterns found**
1. **Record-keeping (p4, Problem 4).** The child writes out 11 cases, where one sentence about the pattern's feature would do.
2. **Precision.** Minor. By the eight- and nine-rings, children will likely reason from the pattern rather than physically test 15 to 17 moves, which is fine.
3. **Procedure (p5–p6, Problem 5).** Physically testing a nine-ring means tracing and colouring nine circles and making 17 checks. This is acceptable only if children are allowed to stop testing once they can explain.
4. **Told, not felt.** Problem 5's "always works" is reasoning by design, which is appropriate for this band and adult.
5. **Adult runs it.** Not found.

**Most likely delight.** Problem 5: one trick ("two together, skip one, then one more" on a ring otherwise of the other kind) works on the eight-ring, and then on the seven- and nine-rings too.

## What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle

- **Visibility of colours.** Whether coloured pencil on the chosen tracing paper shows clearly from the back after a flip, and over a counter.
- **Timing and supply.** How long a five-year-old takes to trace and colour a four- or six-ring copy, and whether ten sheets per pair last.
- **Moving the copy.** Whether children can lift and turn a copy over loose counters without disturbing them.
- **Coverage.** How many problems each table reaches in 35 to 40 minutes.
- **Whether the game holds.** Whether K–1 partners keep testing seriously once "no match" becomes the usual answer.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **Make the testing a two-player game (all bands, p1 shared rules).** Add one sentence to the shared rules: "One partner builds; the other tries to find a turn or flip that matches." The partner then enforces the "every move" condition, and winning becomes the tester giving up instead of an unseen absence. Drop the per-problem "Let your partner test it" / "Let someone test" once this is a rule. This is a page fix. It keeps the activity and makes it a game at every table.
2. **Make copies cheaper and easier to read (all bands, p1 rules and demo; launch).** Have children colour a small dot over the centre of each counter instead of tracing and colouring the whole circle. That is faster for K–1, and the counter's colour shows around the dot, so the comparison is visible. This stays within the outline's tracing paper and pencils. Rehearse it.
3. **Give K–1 Problem 3 something concrete to start from (p2).** Give it its own four-ring, drawn with a working three-kind pattern, and say "Change just one counter of this pattern…". At the moment the adult has to invent the starting point.
4. **Cut K–1 Problem 6 back to one pattern (p5).** Drop "and that cannot match each other. Keep each on its own tracing copy." Leave one two-kind eight-ring pattern for the partner to test, or end K–1 at Problem 5. Whether K–1 should reach the eight-ring at all is outline: for the organizer.
5. **Stop asking for a list of every move (2–3 p4 and 4–5 p4, Problem 4).** In 2–3, replace "Show how you know every possible move fails" with "Explain how you know no turn or flip can match it". In 4–5, replace "Explain why each turn and flip fails" with "Explain why no turn or flip can match it". Both still ask for the explanation without implying an 11-case written list.
6. **Shorten the K–1 rules (p1).** Keep "Fill every spot", "Keep the original still. Turn or flip its tracing copy until all spots line up" and "A whole turn does not count". Cut "Only counter kinds and spots count" and "neighbors may be the same kind"; the adult can say these if they come up. The demo picture already carries the moves.
7. **Give 4–5 more room to write (4–5 p2–p5).** Replace the two rules under Problems 2–5 with a larger open box, especially for Problem 3's explanation across three rings and Problem 5's general argument.
8. **K–1 from the five-ring on (p3–p5).** No page change rescues the testing load on rings of five or more spots for five-year-olds. Fixes 1 and 2 reduce it. Whether K–1 should spend its time on the three- and four-rings, with only an optional six-ring, is outline: for the organizer.
