# Week 12 kid-fit review (Catalan bijections, draft)

| Band | Activity | Page | K–1 bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| K–1 (W12-K-v4, 7 pages) | workable | weak | not met |
| Grades 2–3 (W12-23-v3, 6 pages) | strong | workable | |
| Grades 4–5 (W12-45-v4, 6 pages) | workable | workable | |

I looked at every rendered page of all three draft PDFs. I have not watched children use this packet, so these verdicts are a filter, not a pilot result. Only a rehearsal or pilot can settle the following:

- whether 20 cm strings stay put on an 8 cm circle with their tails folded outside while a five-year-old copies the pairing;
- how long a K–1 child takes to copy four chords into a 3 cm circle;
- whether K–1 children treat a rotated pairing as "the same";
- whether 2–3 children reach P4 by minute 25;
- how long drawing fourteen trees (4–5 P5) or fourteen eight-dot pairings (2–3 P6) actually takes.

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** The core move is excellent for this age. Children lay strings between dots on a big printed circle, and a crossing shows because one string lies on top of another. The fixed-chord and odd-count problems produce obstructions children can see: "Pair all the dots in each circle if you can. If you cannot, show what gets in the way" (P5). Two of the six questions, though, are proofs that a non-mathematician parent would have to run:

- P2: "Show how you know that no pairing is missing."
- P6: "Show why your answer is right."

The rule that decides when two pairings are different ("Pairings are different when a dot has a different partner") is also something the adult has to apply for the children.

**Page: weak.** Page 1 starts well. Within a minute a kindergartner can lay three strings on the six-dot circle for "Find every way to pair six dots." But the printed order makes the table stall at P2, which is the second problem. That page is the same six-dot circle again, with six more blank boards and an explanation demand, and it gives the child no new action. The best K–1 problems (P3 and P5) come after it, and P5 is not reached until page 6. Between them, pages 4–5 hold sixteen 3 cm eight-dot circles for pencil copying.

**K–1 bar: not met.** The children can make the core move themselves, and the strings enforce most of the rules. Three things hold it below strong:

- The opening five rule sentences are read aloud before anything happens.
- The parent must decide whether two pairings are the same, because rotations look identical to a child.
- Problems 2 and 6 ask the parent volunteer to lead completeness and impossibility arguments.

**A child at the table (a kindergartner):**

- **Minute 5:** laying three strings on the P1 circle, then copying them in pencil into a small circle.
- **Minute 15:** has four boards, two of which are rotations of the "three bumps" pairing. The child is waiting for the adult to say whether they count.
- **Minute 30:** if the table followed the printed order, the child is on page 2 looking at six empty circles while the parent tries to explain. If the parent jumped ahead, the child is on P3 or P5 and engaged.

**Patterns found**

1. **Record-keeping**
   - P1, page 1: every string pairing is copied by pencil into a 3 cm circle. This serves the goal, because the collection is the point.
   - P2, page 2: six more blank boards invite redrawing the same five pairings. This only confirms work already done.
   - P4, pages 4–5: sixteen boards with four chords each. That is up to 64 pencil lines for "as many as you can," and duplicates are hard for a five-year-old to spot.
2. **Precision**
   - Strings: 20 cm strings on an 8.6 cm circle (P3, P4, P6) and on the 10-dot circle (P5) leave about 12 cm of tail to fold outside. The tails cross labels and shift when the table is bumped.
   - Pencil: chords on the 8-dot small circles join dots about 1 cm apart. Crossings stay visible, so the risk is moderate, but it needs a rehearsal.
3. **Procedure instead of choices:** none. Every problem is a choice.
4. **Told, not felt**
   - P2, page 2: the completeness reason has to come from an adult. (The reason is that dot 1 can only partner 2, 4 or 6, and each choice leaves a forced or small remainder.)
   - P6, page 7: this is partly felt, since every attempt ends with two neighbors joined. The "why" will still be the adult's.
5. **The adult runs it**
   - Page 1: five rule sentences are read aloud before the first move.
   - P1 and P4: the rotation/duplicate judgement falls to the adult.
   - P2 and P6: the parent volunteer leads a proof.

**Most likely delight:** P3, page 3, the 1–3 chord. Once the string is down, dot 2 is trapped alone and cannot reach anyone without crossing ("it's stuck!"). P5's odd circles, where one dot is always left over, give the same moment.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** The activity moves through four stages:

- Strings over a row of six dots.
- A code that turns arcs into a mountain.
- Puzzles that run the code backwards: "Draw a pairing for each path. Could a path come from two different pairings without crossings?" (P3).
- Codes that fail in visible ways: "Which strings of letters can be codes for pairings of eight dots?" (P5). In UDDUUDUD, dot 3 has nothing to close. In UUUUDDUD, two dots are left open.

Third graders with a mathematician should enjoy the "secret code" feel. In P4 they find that the five mountains and the five pairings match one for one, so the bijection is felt there and not announced.

**Page: workable.** Most of it reads cleanly. But P2 ("Draw the path for each pairing… How does the height of each path compare with its starting level?") is a short procedure on three given pairings, and its question has an obvious answer. P6 asks for fourteen eight-dot pairings drawn as arcs over dots about 1 cm apart, a heavy drawing load at the end of the hour. Strings appear only in P1. The U/D cards in the materials list are never used on the page.

**A child at the table:**

- **Minute 5:** placing strings above the P1 row and drawing arcs.
- **Minute 15:** writing U/D in boxes and drawing mountains (P2), then puzzling over which up-step each down-step closes (P3).
- **Minute 30:** sorting mountains in P4 or testing P5's bad codes.

**Patterns found**

1. **Record-keeping**
   - P2, page 2: each pairing is written as letters in boxes, then drawn again as a path. That is two transcriptions, but it produces the picture.
   - P4, page 4: the paths are matched to pairings that are back on page 1.
   - P6, page 6: fourteen arc drawings.
2. **Precision:** mild. P6 needs nested arcs three deep over dots about 1 cm apart, in rows about 2.5 cm apart.
3. **Procedure:** mild. The P2 encoding is a rule applied step by step, but it is only six steps, and the result is visible at once.
4. **Told, not felt:** P2's height question has an obvious answer and no surprise. Otherwise the surprises can be seen and felt.
5. **The adult runs it:** not much. The three-sentence code rule on page 2 is readable for third graders, and the worked example is clear.

**Most likely delight:** P5, page 5. A child tries to draw UDDUUDUD and finds that dot 3 is a "D" with nobody to go back to. A close second is the P4 realization that "there are five mountains, just like the five pairings."

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: workable.** Trees, walk codes and decoding are good material for a fourth or fifth grader with the organizer:

- P3 ("Can a code describe two different trees?") and P4 (legal codes) are real puzzles.
- P5 ("How many different trees have four edges? Organize the count…") is a satisfying hunt.

But the band is pencil-only from the first line. The whole-group string demonstration never connects to it, and the "same objects" surprise of the week (trees = mountains = pairings) is never something these children experience at their table. P6 ("Find the number of trees with five edges, then six edges. Give a counting rule…") asks for a recurrence. Children cannot reach it by drawing 132 trees, so the adult is likely to announce it.

**Page: workable.** Page 1 opens with seven abstract sentences (root, parent, child, "fixed left-to-right order," "Only the branching and its order matter") before any action. P4 stacks three demanding written tasks into one problem: decide, give general rules and explain them, then describe a rebuilding method. Pages 5–6 are mostly blank space, which is appropriate for the quick finisher.

**A child at the table (a fourth grader):**

- **Minute 5:** still reading the rule paragraph, then drawing three-edge trees. The child is unsure whether a mirror image counts as a different tree.
- **Minute 15:** walking trees and filling P2's boxes.
- **Minute 30:** decoding P3 or sorting P4 codes, with the organizer pressing for a general rule.

**Patterns found**

1. **Record-keeping**
   - P2, page 2: fills boxes.
   - P5, page 5: fourteen trees to draw.
   - P6, page 6: drawing toward 42 trees if no rule appears.
2. **Precision:** none.
3. **Procedure:** mild. The P2 walk is an eight-step rule per tree, but the output (a mountain) is immediate and the contrasts between trees are interesting.
4. **Told, not felt**
   - P6, page 6: the counting rule.
   - Whole band: the link to pairings and paths shows up only in page 2's example path, not in the children's own work.
5. **The adult runs it**
   - Page 1: the seven-sentence definition.
   - P4, page 4: three writing tasks in one problem.

**Most likely delight:** P2, page 2. The tall chain tree's code is one big mountain (UUUUDDDD) and the four-way star's code is a sawtooth (UDUDUDUD), so the shape of the tree shows in the code. A secondary delight is at sharing time, if someone points out that P5's 14 is the same number as the 2–3 table's eight-dot pairings.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1 order (pages 2–7).** Reorder the problems so concrete obstructions come before arguments:
   - P1 (find six-dot pairings)
   - current P5 (odd and even circles)
   - current P3 (fixed chord)
   - current P4 (eight-dot pairings)
   - current P2 (completeness check)
   - current P6 (neighbors)

   This keeps the activity and gets every child to the trapped-dot moment before the stall. Page change only.
2. **K–1 P2 (page 2).** Remove the six extra blank boards so the child checks and marks the boards already drawn in P1 instead of recopying them. Keep the large circle for trying a suspected missing pairing with strings. The facilitator guide should give the parent one concrete question to ask, such as "Who can dot 1 hold hands with?", rather than leaving the parent to invent a completeness argument.
3. **K–1 opening rules (page 1).** Cut the read-aloud to three short sentences:
   - Use each dot once.
   - Strings stay inside the circle and do not cross.
   - A dot with a different partner makes a different pairing.

   Show "keep dots in place" and "strings on big circles, pencil on small" at the launch instead of reading them.
4. **4–5 opening (page 1).** Cut the seven-sentence definition to about four sentences, and draw the existing five-edge example beside its mirror image marked as different. That one non-task picture carries "fixed left-to-right order" and "only the branching matters" better than the prose does.
5. **4–5 P4 (page 4).** Drop "Describe how to rebuild a tree from any code that follows your rules." P3 already has children rebuilding trees, and the rebuild description can be a facilitator follow-up. What remains is one decision task plus one rule-and-reason task.
6. **2–3 P2 (page 2).** Replace the thin height question with one open question tied to the next problems, for example "Can a pairing ever make a path that goes below the dark line?" Otherwise remove the question and let P4 carry it.
7. **K–1 P4 (pages 4–5).** Use fewer, larger recording circles (about 5 cm instead of 3 cm, eight to ten boards instead of sixteen). "As many as you can" needs no sixteen slots, and larger circles reduce the pencil precision and duplicate-spotting burden for five-year-olds.
8. **2–3 P6 (page 6).** No page change is required. The facilitator guide should let the pair pool one collection of fourteen, and let children record a path instead of arcs if they prefer. If a page change is wanted, enlarge the rows by printing eight per page and putting the rest on a second page.
9. **Strings (all bands).** Rehearse one eight-dot pairing on the K–1 page 3 mat. If the tails do not fold away cleanly, cut K–1 strings to about 12 cm, since the longest K–1 chord is 8.6 cm. The 20 cm length is set by the 2–3 row. This is a materials change, not a page change.
10. **4–5 connection to the shared hook (whole band).** Outline: for the organizer. A page edit cannot fix this. The 4–5 band never touches the strings, the circle or the mountain paths that the launch demonstrates, so the week's central surprise never happens at that table. The outline's U/D cards could be used for P3–P4 decoding without a page change. Whether 4–5 should meet pairings at all is the organizer's call.
