Kid-fit review of the draft packets in `draft/`, rendered to `renders/` and read page by page. This is a filter, not a pilot result. Nobody has watched children use these pages, and the string and disc materials have not been laid on a printed mat.

## K–1 (W12-K-v5)

**Activity: strong.** Laying strings between numbered dots so they don't cross is something a five-year-old can do within a minute. The best questions here are ones children answer with their hands. In Problem 2 ("Pair all the dots in each circle if you can. If you cannot, show what gets in the way.") the 3-, 5- and 7-dot circles always leave one dot alone. In Problem 3 the drawn 1–3 and 1–5 strings trap dots 2 or 2–4 on one side. Children see these obstructions; nobody has to announce them.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1–4 give a clear action, a big mat for the strings and small circles to record on. Problem 5 ("Check your six-dot pairings from Problem 1. Show how you know that no pairing is missing.") is an empty circle with an abstract question. A child who reads almost nothing has no move to make there, so the volunteer would be running it. The strings also don't fit the rule printed at the top (see Precision below).

**What keeps K–1 below the bar.** The core move happens within a minute. Some rules are not enforced by the materials, though, so the parent volunteer has to police them:
- Floppy 20 cm strings on a 6.4 cm circle (page 1) can't follow "Strings stay inside the circle". A neighbor pair 3.2 cm apart leaves about 17 cm of tail inside the circle, lying across the other strings. Crossings then stop being something the materials show.
- Deciding whether a pairing is new is not enforced by anything. The 4-dot example shows that turned copies count as different. Even so, children will say that 12/34/56 and 23/45/61 are "the same, just turned", and only the adult can settle it.
- Problems 5 and 6 end in "show how you know" and "show why your answer is right". Those questions belong to the adult.

**Patterns found**
1. *Hands-on start turning into record-keeping*: mild. On every page the child copies each string layout onto a 3 cm circle in pencil (Problem 1 page 1, Problem 3 page 3, Problem 4 pages 4–5). That serves a goal the child cares about, which is not finding the same pairing twice. Problem 4's nine recording circles are a lot for a kindergartner, but "as many as you can" lets them stop when they like.
2. *Precision*: the string length against the circle sizes, as above. The same thing happens on the 8 cm mats on pages 3, 4 and 7. Pencil chords on the small circles are fine: the dots are well apart and crossings are obvious.
3. *Procedure instead of choices*: Problem 3 (page 3) is a two-step routine before the real choice. The child copies a drawn chord onto the large circle, leaves it there and then completes the pairing. The volunteer will demonstrate it several times. Once the chord is in place, the choices belong to the child.
4. *Told, not felt*: Problem 6 (page 7). Children will feel that a neighbor pair "always sneaks in" after a few failed tries. The reason (look at the shortest string) will come from an adult. That is acceptable as the last problem.
5. *Adult runs it*: Problem 5 (page 6) as written, and the explanation half of Problem 6. The opening rules are three sentences read aloud once, which is fine.

**Minute by minute.** At minute 5 the child lays three strings on the six-dot mat and then wants to know whether a new layout counts. At minute 15 they are copying pairings onto small circles and asking "is this one different?". At minute 30, if the order holds, they have the odd circles in Problem 2 and say "one's always left over". That is the most likely laugh.

**Most likely delight**: the lonely leftover dot on the 3-, 5- and 7-dot circles in Problem 2, and the trapped dot 2 inside the drawn 1–3 string in Problem 3.

## Grades 2–3 (W12-23-v4)

**Activity: strong.** Finding the pairings, then turning each one into a "mountain code" and rebuilding pairings from codes, makes a code-breaking activity third graders like. The real decisions come in Problem 3 ("Draw a pairing for each path. Could a path come from two different pairings without crossings?") and Problem 5 ("Which strings of letters can be codes for pairings of eight dots? … explain what goes wrong"). In Problem 5 a child sees the D in UDDUUDUD "with nobody to close".

**Page: workable.** Pages 1–3 and 5 are clear and well sized. Problem 4 asks the children to redraw what they have already drawn. Everything after Problem 1 is pencil only, and the U/D cards from the outline never appear.

**Patterns found**
1. *Record-keeping*: Problem 4 (page 4). Problem 2 has already drawn the five six-dot paths (UUUDDD, UUDDUD, UDUDUD), and so has Problem 3 (UUDUDD, UDUUDD). Problem 4's six blank grids and "Match your paths to your six-dot pairings" mean flipping back to pages 1–3 and redrawing to confirm. In Problem 2 the child writes the code in boxes and then draws the same information as a path. That is light but still a double record.
2. *Precision*: low on paper. On the large row in Problem 1 (dots 2.4 cm apart) strings must bow "above the row", and the tails of 20 cm strings have nowhere obvious to go. Only a rehearsal will show whether the strings help or just get in the way.
3. *Procedure*: Problem 2 is a short procedure: read left to right, write U or D, step the path. Its payoff, the question about going below the line, only comes after three full conversions. Six steps per pairing keeps that tolerable.
4. *Told, not felt*: none. "Never below the line" and "only one way back" both show up in the children's own drawings.
5. *Adult runs it*: no. The rules are short, and the worked example on page 2 shows input, letters and path. The mathematician at this table will mostly be having conversations.

**Minute by minute.** At minute 5 two children are moving strings on the big row and arguing over whether 1–4 with 2–3 counts as different from 1–2 with 3–4. At minute 15 they are writing U/D under arcs and drawing peaks. At minute 30 the quick pair is on Problem 5 or starting the 14 eight-dot pairings in Problem 6, which is a long pencil enumeration on tightly spaced rows. Splitting the cases among the four children would make Problem 6 a table project, not a slog.

**Most likely delight**: Problem 5. A code that "runs out of U's" (UDDUUDUD) or ends up in the air (UUUUDDUD). Possibly also the end-of-session discovery that the 2–3 table's 5 and 14 match the other tables' counts.

## Grades 4–5 (W12-45-v5)

**Activity: strong.** For two fourth graders and a fifth grader with the organizer, the activity has room for real thinking: trees, walk codes, the question "Can a code describe two different trees?" (Problem 3) and the climb from 5 to 14 to a guess of 42. Problem 4's mix of legal and broken codes (DUUUDDUD, UUDUDUDU, UUDDDUUD) gives them concrete cases to argue from.

**Page: workable.** The writing is clear and the rules read well to a ten-year-old. But this table never touches the materials from the whole-group launch: there are no strings, discs or cards anywhere in the packet. Problem 6 ("Find the number of trees with five edges, then six edges. Give a counting rule…") is a blank page. As printed, it asks for 42 and 132 trees to be counted by hand or for a recurrence to appear out of nowhere.

**Patterns found**
1. *Record-keeping*: Problem 6 (page 6). Without the splitting idea, the five-edge count means drawing and checking 42 trees. That is bookkeeping, not discovery. Problem 5 (14 trees, 16 root dots) is at the edge of what holds a ten-year-old's attention, though "organize the count so someone can check" gives it a purpose.
2. *Precision*: none. Drawn trees are exact enough.
3. *Procedure*: the walk code in Problem 2 is a procedure, but each tree takes eight steps and it leads straight into the reconstruction in Problem 3, where the choices are.
4. *Told, not felt*: Problem 6's count of 132 for six edges can only be computed, not seen. That is acceptable as the final stretch for the quickest child.
5. *Adult runs it*: partly, in Problem 6. Getting from "14" to a counting rule will mostly be the organizer's conversation. The page leaves the third child (the rotating referee) with no role.

**Minute by minute.** At minute 5 the children are drawing three-edge trees and debating whether a mirror image counts. "Left-to-right order matters" settles it. At minute 15 they are tracing a finger around a tree and filling code boxes. At minute 30 they are decoding codes into trees and testing Problem 4's codes. One child may be guessing the next count.

**Most likely delight**: Problem 3 ("Can a code describe two different trees?"), when nobody can make a second tree. The other candidate is predicting 42 from 1, 2, 5, 14 and finding that the other tables also got 5 and 14.

## What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle

- Whether 20 cm strings can lie on the K–1 6.4 cm and 8 cm circles and the 2–3 row without the tails creating false crossings. The guide's own check, laying one eight-dot pairing on K–1 page 3, has not been done.
- Whether 15–20 mm discs leave the numbers readable on the 2–3 large row, where the dots are 2.4 cm apart.
- Whether kindergartners accept turned pairings as different.
- How long the following take: K–1 Problem 1, 2–3 Problem 6 (14 pairings) and 4–5 Problem 5 (14 trees).
- Whether 4–5 children reach the splitting idea in Problem 5 or need the organizer to suggest it.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1 page 1 rules, plus materials (all K–1 mats).** "Strings stay inside the circle" can't hold with 20 cm strings. Smallest change: say "Strings do not cross" and let the tails run out past each string's dots, beyond the circle. Alternatively, cut K–1 strings shorter (about 10 cm, enough for the 8.6 cm longest chord). Rehearse on page 3 either way.
2. **K–1 Problem 5 (page 6): make the completeness argument something children do.** Replace the single blank circle with five small six-dot circles, each with a string from dot 1 already drawn: to 2, to 3, to 4, to 5 and to 6. Ask "Find every way to finish each one." Move it straight after Problem 1. The children then see that 1–3 and 1–5 trap a dot and that the others finish in 2, 1 and 2 ways, which adds up to their five. The adult says "So how do you know none are missing?" aloud. This uses the outline's fixed-chord idea and doesn't add a new activity.
3. **2–3 Problem 4 (page 4): stop the re-enumeration.** The five paths are already on pages 2–3. Smallest change: use the outline's U/D cards. "Lay three U cards and three D cards in a row. Which rows make a path that never goes below the dark line? Find them all and match each to a pairing from Problem 1." The cards enforce three of each, and the hands do the searching. Keep fewer grids for recording.
4. **4–5 Problem 1 (page 1): give the table the concrete start the others get.** Name the pieces: "Use three strings and four discs, one disc marked as the root, to make every different tree with three edges." Children build first and draw what they built. Discs and strings are already on the materials list.
5. **4–5 Problem 3 (page 3): give the third child a role.** Add one final case: one child makes a four-edge tree out of sight and reads its code aloud, a partner draws it, and the referee compares. This is the "can a code describe two trees?" question played as a game, and it fits the table's pair-plus-referee rotation.
6. **2–3 Problems 2 and 5 (pages 2, 5): bring back the cards.** A sentence in the page-2 rules, "Put a U or D card under each dot", lets children lay the code under a string pairing before writing it. Optional if the mathematician prefers pencil.
7. **4–5 Problem 6 (page 6): keep it late, but don't ask for 42 trees to be drawn.** Smallest change: "Find the number of trees with five edges without drawing them all, then six edges…" The page then says what kind of answer counts, and the method stays open. This holds up as written only for the quickest child, with the organizer.
8. **4–5 Problem 2 (page 2):** "Which features of a tree can you read from its code?" is very open. It can stay, but the organizer should be ready to name one feature (the number of edges) to get the conversation going. That belongs in the facilitator guide, not on the page.
9. **K–1 Problem 3 (page 3):** keep it, but it needs a demonstration. The volunteer should show "copy this string, then finish" once with the 1–4 circle before children start.
10. **Facilitation, all bands:** the whole-group launch shows strings on a circle, but the 2–3 pages use a row and the 4–5 pages use trees. The 2–3 and 4–5 adults each need a 30-second re-launch at their table. In the closing share, put the counts side by side: K–1 and 2–3 found 5 six-dot pairings, 4–5 found 5 three-edge trees, and 2–3 and 4–5 found 14 at the next size. It costs nothing and is likely to be the session's best shared moment.

No band's activity needs to go back to the organizer as an outline question. Page changes can fix every weakness found here.
