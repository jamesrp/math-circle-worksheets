I rendered all 27 pages (`renders/`, 70 dpi) and read every page. I have not watched children use this packet, so these verdicts are a filter, not a pilot result. Only a rehearsal or a pilot can settle the open questions listed at the end.

One thing about this week works in children's favour throughout. The theorem says that some split always works, and it stays true when a child's pencil is off. Noise in placing or drawing can change which split works, but never whether one does. So the placing game and "make the regions meet" hold up at the precision children have. Precision matters only in the problems that count every working split, or that depend on dots lying exactly on a line.

---

## K–1 (6 pages, F22-K-v7)

**Activity: workable.** In the 2+2 case the core move suits five-year-olds well. Pick two letters, join them in blue, join the other two in red, and see whether the lines cross. The opening key shows exactly this (K, M against L, N, with a ring where they cross). The other cases need more than that. When D is inside the triangle, the move is a single dot against a shaded triangle, and the K–1 page never shows a one-letter group or a triangle. The degenerate placements and the dots-in-a-row case depend on fine distinctions that a parent volunteer has to explain.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1, 2, 3 and 5 are short and pictorial, and a child can act on them after one hearing: "In each picture, make two groups whose regions meet. Draw both regions and mark a point they share." Problem 4 ("For each place of D, find two different ways…") and Problem 6 ("Draw one group just above the dots and the other group just below") are the weak pages.

**K–1 bar: not met.** Within a minute, a child who reads nothing can join two pairs of dots and see a cross. That is close to the bar. Three things keep it below:
- The rules are not enforced by the materials. "Each letter in one group, both groups nonempty" lives only in the adult's head. Nothing on the page or in the materials makes a child give every dot exactly one color.
- On Problem 1, board 2 (D inside the triangle), all three pairings fail. Finding the answer needs the one-dot-against-a-triangle move, which the K–1 key does not show. The parent volunteer has to know it and show it.
- The two late pages (Problems 4 and 6) need adult explanation of why touching an edge counts and why lines drawn apart "meet".

**Patterns found**
- *Pattern 2 (precision), p. 4, Problem 4.* All three placements of D are degenerate: on segment AB, on line AB beyond B, and on segment AC. The second "way" on each board exists only because D lies exactly on a line. A five-year-old's hand-drawn side AC passes a millimetre to one side of D, so whether "D alone against A, B, C" works depends on the pencil. The horizontal cases (boards 1–4) are readable. The diagonal case (boards 5–6, D at the midpoint of AC) is not.
- *Pattern 4 (told, not felt), and Pattern 5, p. 6, Problem 6.* With A, D, C, B in a row, the child draws one group above the line and one below. The two regions never touch on paper. The adult has to announce that they "meet" because their stretches overlap. A K–1 child cannot see this; they would have to accept it.
- *Pattern 5 (adult runs it), pp. 2–3, Problems 2–3.* The games need someone to judge whether the other player succeeded. That is fine for 2+2 crossings. When the answer is a point inside a triangle, the parent will have to find it for the child. The page also gives one board for repeated rounds, so after two rounds the drawings pile up on top of each other. Tracing paper, which is in the materials, would keep one sheet per round, but the page does not use it, and sliding tracing paper is fiddly at five.
- *Pattern 4, ordering.* "It always works" is surprising only after a child has seen it fail. The failure comes in Problem 5 (three dots, p. 5), after both games. In the games the K–1 child is mostly confirming something that never looked in doubt.
- Patterns 1 and 3: not present. There are no tallies or tables.

**Most likely delight.** Problem 1, board 2: "None of my crosses work… oh, D is hiding inside the triangle." After that, in Problem 2, the placer finds that wherever D goes, the partner still makes it work. That moment comes only if the child has first seen the triangle trick.

## Grades 2–3 (10 pages, F22-23-v7)

**Activity: strong.** "Take turns placing A, B, C, and D in the box. Try to make an arrangement that the other player cannot split successfully" (Problem 2) is a real two-player challenge. Third graders will try lines, clusters and a dot inside a triangle, and keep failing to beat it. They feel the guarantee instead of being told it. Then three dots in a triangle fail at once (Problems 6–7), which is the gasp. A mathematician at the table can run all of this.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1 and 2 start well. The middle of the packet leans on checking lists. The contrast that makes the game surprising sits on pages 9–10, beyond where most children will get in 35–40 minutes.

**Patterns found**
- *Pattern 5 (reading load), p. 1, opening rules.* There are six rule sentences and a four-part key before Problem 1. That includes "When dots lie on one line, draw one group's region just above…", which is not needed until page 4. A third grader will skim it, and the adult will re-explain it at page 4.
- *Pattern 3 (procedure), p. 1, Problem 1.* "Move the D marker to each cross in turn… use the markers and your finger" adds a step that does not help. A, B and C are already printed, and page 2 repeats every board with D printed. Children will go straight to drawing on page 2. The step is harmless, but it makes the page look procedural.
- *Pattern 1 (record-keeping), pp. 4–5, Problem 3.* With A, D, B, C in a row there is one large board and six small boards, which is exactly seven: one per split. The number of boards gives away the count, and the work becomes filling in seven boards with segments above and below a line. "Find every split" is a worthwhile goal, but at minute 15 the child is copying intervals, and nothing on these pages pulls them forward.
- *Pattern 1, pp. 7–8, Problem 5.* "Make two arrangements of A, B, C, and D that have no working split in common." To check this, a child has to know every working split of both arrangements: up to fourteen checks. The double negative ("no … in common") and the new term "working split" make the goal hard to keep in mind. This is likely to need the adult to restate it.
- *Pattern 2, p. 6, Problem 4.* Sharing a whole segment needs four dots placed exactly on a line. The ruler handles this, so the risk is low. The filled-triangle question is pure reasoning, and a good one: a triangle needs three letters, which leaves the other group as a single dot.
- *Pattern 4, ordering.* The three-dot failure (Problems 6–7, pp. 9–10) is what makes the game's "always" surprising. It comes after about 25 minutes of other work.

**Most likely delight.** Problem 2: the placer's "I'll put D right in the middle… no, a line… still no", followed by Problem 6, where three dots cannot be split at all. Only the first is likely to happen in a typical hour as the pages are ordered.

## Grades 4–5 (11 pages, F22-45-v7)

**Activity: strong.** Existence, uniqueness in general position, coincident and collinear cases, the three-point counterexample, and "smallest number of dots" form a serious theorem that children can test with their own hands. The organizer at the table can take a ten-year-old's case argument for Problem 6 as far as it will go. Three children and a rotating referee suit the game.

**Page: workable.** The pages are clear and ten-year-olds can read them. The order delays the hook, though, and Problem 5 asks for heavy checking.

**Patterns found**
- *Pattern 1 (record-keeping), pp. 3–4, Problem 2.* This is the first problem after the warm-up: "Find every split whose regions meet. Explain how you know your list is complete." The four dots are collinear on a diagonal (A, C, B, D), with one large board and six small ones. The child spends minutes 10–20 drawing seven splits before reaching the game (Problem 3). Here the game comes after the enumeration, while the K–1 and 2–3 packets put the game right after Problem 1. Drawing "above" and "below" a diagonal line, with the labels set just below and to the right of each dot, also invites mistakes.
- *Pattern 2, p. 3, Problem 2.* Some children will not notice that the dots are exactly collinear and will draw "triangle A, C, B" as a sliver. That is a good conversation for the organizer, but it is a precision-dependent start to the packet.
- *Pattern 1, pp. 7–9, Problem 5.* There are three arrangements to make, and "exactly one successful split" has to be checked across all seven splits each time: 21 checks. This is the uniqueness question, so the checking serves the mathematics, and a ten-year-old who sees why only the diagonals of a convex quadrilateral (or a point inside a triangle) can work can cut it short. Three blank pages is generous.
- *Pattern 5, p. 10, Problem 6.* A general explanation will mostly be organizer-led conversation. That is fine this late in the packet.
- *Pattern 4: not present.* The coincident case (Problem 4, six boards with A and D at one location) and the three-dot failure can both be felt.

**Most likely delight.** The game (Problem 3), followed by Problem 7: the same move that always worked with four dots fails with three. Problem 8 then falls out of Problem 7 almost at once, which a quick child will enjoy.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **2–3, Problems 6–7 (pp. 9–10): move them to follow Problem 2 (the placing game).** The contrast with three dots is what turns "it always worked" into a surprise, and most children never reach page 9. This is only a reordering, with no new mathematics.
2. **4–5, Problems 2 and 3 (pp. 3–5): swap them so the placing game comes right after Problem 1.** Put the enumeration of every split after it, and consider bringing Problem 7 (three dots) forward to just after the game, for the same reason as fix 1.
3. **K–1, opening key (p. 1): add the one-dot-against-a-shaded-triangle example beside the K-L-M-N cross.** 2–3 already has the point, segment and triangle diagrams, so this reuses existing art. Without it, Problem 1, board 2 and every game round with D inside are stuck until an adult steps in.
4. **K–1, Problem 4 (p. 4): replace the D-on-AC boards (boards 5–6) with a second horizontal case, or cut the problem to the two horizontal placements.** The diagonal-edge case depends on pencil precision. If the problem is kept, ask for one meeting grouping per board, not two.
5. **K–1, Problem 6 (p. 6): mark as a return-visit page or cut it for K–1.** The above/below convention asks five-year-olds to accept that regions drawn apart "meet". No page change makes that something they can see. Outline: for the organizer.
6. **2–3, Problem 5 (pp. 7–8): reword without the double negative**, for example: "Make two arrangements of A, B, C, and D. Find a split that works for the first one and fails for the second, and a split that works for the second and fails for the first." Leave the full comparison to the adult.
7. **2–3 and 4–5, Problem 3 / Problem 2 enumeration boards: print four small boards rather than six,** and add a line for the list, so the number of boards stops giving away the seven splits and the child organizes the list themselves.
8. **K–1 and 2–3 games (Problems 2–3 and Problem 2): say "on tracing paper" in the problem** ("Each round, lay a new sheet of tracing paper on the box"), so rounds don't pile up on one board. Rehearse whether five-year-olds can manage tracing paper; if not, give each round its own small printed box.
9. **2–3 and 4–5 opening rules (p. 1): move the "dots on one line" sentence to the first collinear problem,** where it applies, so the opening block is about three short rules.
10. **2–3 and 4–5, Problem 1 (p. 1): cut "use the markers and your finger" and the separate search board,** and let the children work on the page-2 record boards directly. Keep the markers for the games.
11. **All bands, materials: a rubber band and pegs or a pegboard per pair would make "stretched-band region" physical,** and would let the materials enforce the region instead of a child's drawing. The outline lists one band, for the launch only. Outline: for the organizer.

## What only a rehearsal or a pilot can settle

- Whether the A–D markers fit the K–1 boards. In Problem 5, A, the middle cross, B and the right-hand cross are about 3.5 cm apart. Rehearsal should also check whether a child can draw lines between markers sitting on the page, or must mark the dots and lift the markers first.
- How long one round of the placing game takes, including the search over up to seven splits, at each band. Also whether K–1 children stay engaged in a game the placer can never win.
- Whether five-year-olds draw and shade a triangle neatly enough to tell "D inside" from "D just outside" on Problem 1, board 2.
- Whether tracing paper helps or hinders at K–1.
- How far a typical 2–3 or 4–5 child gets in 35–40 minutes, which decides whether fixes 1 and 2 matter as much as I expect.
