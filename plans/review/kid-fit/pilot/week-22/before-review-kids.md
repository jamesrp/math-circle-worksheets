# Kid-fit review: Week 22, Meeting regions (draft v6 packets)

Basis: I rendered and looked at all 9 + 9 + 12 pages and read the problem sources in `draft/src/problems/` for the exact positions of the dots. I have not watched children use this packet. These verdicts filter the packet before a pilot and are not pilot results. Only a rehearsal or pilot can settle the following:

- whether the A–D markers sit on a 12.6 cm board without hiding the printed dots and crosses;
- what tracing paper is used for at the table, since no page says;
- whether a five-year-old can shade a triangle through three dots on a 5.35 cm recording board;
- how long "find every split" takes;
- whether children believe the placing game's result or keep thinking they almost beat it.

What the week has going for it: the basic generic move (draw two lines and see them cross, or see a lone dot inside a shaded triangle) can be seen at any drawing accuracy. The placing game ("Try to make an arrangement that the other player cannot split") has a built-in opponent who checks the rule and a surprise that children can feel: you keep losing. Most of the risk is on the pages. Much of the packet is built on exact cases (dots on a line, dots on an edge, the line where C may go) and on listing all seven splits, and the game comes last in K–1 and grades 2–3.

## K–1 (F22-K-v6, 9 pages)

**Activity: workable.** A child can draw "a line from A to D and a line from B to C" and see them cross, or see D sitting inside the triangle ABC. The Problem 4 game ("Keep A, B, and C fixed, and take turns placing D. Can the other player always make two regions that meet?") gives a partner who checks the result. With a capable adult, first graders could enjoy this. It stays workable rather than strong because the idea of a split is not physical. Each label used once, both groups nonempty, and swapping the groups does not count as new: nothing on the table enforces these rules, so the adult does. The "stretched band" exists as a real rubber band only in the launch.

**Page: weak.** The opening is five rule sentences ("Use each label once in two groups, with at least one label in each… Use the centers of the marks… Swapping the two groups is the same split") and a four-panel key labelled E / F, G / H, I, J / K–N with arrows. The pages then twice ask a K–1 child to "Find every split whose regions meet" (Problems 2 and 3) on nine and six recording boards about 2 inches square. Every case in those two problems is an exact incidence or a collinear row.

**K–1 bar: not met.**
- **Reading:** a non-reading child cannot hold the five rules after one hearing.
- **Materials:** nothing on the table stops an illegal grouping or decides whether two shapes "meet". The parent volunteer has to judge every attempt.
- **Recording:** within a minute of the launch, a child can make the core move of placing D and drawing two lines. But the page then asks for every split on tiny boards with exact cases. A non-mathematician parent cannot judge those cases without the guide's answer gallery.

**One K–1 child, minute by minute**
- **Minute 5:** she has put marker D on the upper-right cross of Problem 1. The adult has re-explained "two groups". She draws A–B and C–D, which are the sides of the quadrilateral and do not touch. She tries again and finds the crossing pair, which is a nice moment. It is unclear whether she draws on tracing paper or on the page. Page 2 then asks her to draw it all again on a small board.
- **Minute 15:** she is on Problem 2 (page 4). D is printed exactly on segment AB, and the board is about 2 inches square. "Every split" means the adult reads out pairings one at a time while she draws lines. Whether her pencil line from A to B passes through D's dot decides whether D | ABC "meets". She is waiting for the adult to say whether it counts.
- **Minute 30:** she most likely never reaches the game on page 7. If she does, it is the best thing in the packet.

**Patterns found**
1. **Record-keeping**
   - Problem 1, pages 1–2: the child searches with a marker on the large board, then redraws the result on page 2.
   - Problem 2, page 4: nine boards to record "every split", and each position has only two splits that work.
   - Problem 3, page 6: six boards for one row of four dots.
   - In both Problems 2 and 3, the boards confirm a list the adult is running rather than serving a goal the child cares about.
2. **Precision**
   - Problem 2: all three crosses are exact incidences. D is on segment AB, on the line AB beyond B, or on segment AC (source `k_edge`).
   - Problem 3: A, D, C, B lie on one line. Each split's two regions are segments drawn on top of each other on that line, so a child cannot see two regions or where they overlap.
   - Problem 5: the answer is "every place in the box" that lies on the line through A and B, a zero-width line.
   - "Use the centers of the marks" applies to markers that are physically bigger than the dots.
3. **Procedure:** Problems 2 and 3 "Find every split" is in practice running down the seven splits. A five-year-old cannot organise that, so the adult carries out the list.
4. **Told, not felt:** Problem 5 ("Find every place in the box where C can go so the two regions meet"). Children can test the three crosses, but "the whole line through A and B" is a fact the adult has to announce.
5. **Adult runs it:** the rules block and key on page 1, the word "split", and "swapping the two groups is the same split" (needed only for counting). Together with "every split" in Problems 2 and 3, the adult operates Problems 2–3 while the children draw lines on request.

**Most likely delight:** the Problem 4 game (page 7). A child places D somewhere sneaky and the partner still finds a crossing or "D is inside the triangle". The first time a child finds the crossing pair in Problem 1 is a smaller moment of the same kind.

## Grades 2–3 (F22-23-v6, 9 pages)

**Activity: strong.** Four third graders with a mathematician can absorb Problem 1 ("Put D on each cross. For each position, find a split… whose two regions meet"), where every cross is a generic case. Problem 6 ("Try to make an arrangement that the other player cannot split successfully. Can four dots always be split…?") is a real two-player contest. It ends in the felt surprise that you cannot win, and it leads naturally to "why not?".

**Page: workable.**
- Problem 1 is good.
- Problem 2 asks for all seven splits on a collinear row ("Explain how you know your list is complete"). That is a fair task for third graders, but the overlapping drawings are unreadable.
- Problem 4 ("No split may work for both arrangements") is hard to parse. It also asks the child to check every split of two arrangements.
- Problem 5 stacks three questions, and the last is the zero-width line.
- The best activity, the game, is the last page.

**One grades 2–3 child, minute by minute**
- **Minute 5:** placing D on crosses and finding crossing diagonals or D inside the triangle, with a partner arguing about which pairing works.
- **Minute 15:** on Problem 2's six small boards (page 4) for the row A D B C. He is drawing segments on top of segments and asking whether AB | DC "meets", because the two drawings coincide. He is listing.
- **Minute 30:** maybe on Problem 4, puzzling over the wording. Most children will not reach the game.

**Patterns found**
1. **Record-keeping**
   - Problem 1, page 2 redraws page 1's search.
   - Problem 2, page 4: six boards to confirm a seven-split list.
   - Problem 4: checking that no working split is shared means listing working splits for both arrangements.
2. **Precision**
   - Problem 2 (pages 3–4): the regions are segments on one line and overlap invisibly.
   - Problem 5 ("Find every place in the box where C could go"): the answer is the line through A and B.
3. **Procedure:** Problem 2 is a seven-case check. That is acceptable here, because "how do you know your list is complete" is the point.
4. **Told, not felt**
   - Problem 5's locus.
   - Problem 3's "Can two groups… share a filled triangle? Explain": at this age it is reasoned, not seen. It is acceptable as a late question.
5. **Adult runs it:** Problem 4's sentence "No split may work for both arrangements" will need the adult to translate it. Problem 5's three stacked questions too.

**Most likely delight:** the Problem 6 game, when a child sets up what looks like an unsplittable arrangement and the partner finds the split anyway.

## Grades 4–5 (F22-45-v6, 12 pages)

**Activity: strong.** With the organizer at a three-child table, the sequence works:
- Problem 1 gives the generic cases.
- Problem 2 is a collinear row with "Explain how you know your list is complete".
- Problem 3 is the game ("Try to make an arrangement with no successful split. Decide whether your opponent must always be able to find one"), with the third child as referee.
- The later problems turn the game into existence versus uniqueness and a proof.

**Page: workable.**
- The game comes early (Problem 3), which is right.
- Problem 5 ("two… arrangements that each have exactly one successful split… a third with more than one… Explain how you checked") means checking seven splits in each of three arrangements, across three identical pages.
- Problem 4 offers six identical boards for the coincident case.
- Problem 7 packs three separate questions together, the last a "smallest number of dots" proof.
- The rules block and key are the same dense block as K–1, which is fine for readers of this age.

**One grades 4–5 child, minute by minute**
- **Minute 5:** finishing Problem 1.
- **Minute 15:** deep in the Problem 3 game, trying near-collinear placements to trap the opponent, with the referee checking. This is the best stretch.
- **Minute 30:** either explaining why she never wins (Problem 6 territory, which is good) or grinding through Problem 4's six boards. A ten-year-old with a pencil can live with that, but it is confirmation work.

**Patterns found**
1. **Record-keeping**
   - Problem 1, page 2 redraw.
   - Problem 4 (pages 6–7): six boards to list the four working splits of a coincident placement.
   - Problem 5 (pages 8–10): counting splits in three arrangements. The counting serves a real question (exactly one versus more than one), so it is milder.
2. **Precision**
   - Problem 2: the row A, C, B, D lies exactly on a slanted line (source `u_line`). The segments overlap invisibly, and a child copying it freehand gets four points that are not collinear, which changes the answer.
   - Problem 5's "more than one successful split" can only come from an exact degenerate placement, which a hand-drawn arrangement only roughly achieves.
3. **Procedure:** none serious. The enumerations serve completeness arguments.
4. **Told, not felt:** none serious. The degenerate facts are reasoned rather than seen, which suits the band.
5. **Adult runs it:** none serious with the organizer at the table. Problem 7's stacked questions may need splitting aloud.

**Most likely delight:** the Problem 3 game. The referee rotates, and the moment a child says "you can't ever win this" comes from their own repeated losses.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1 Problem 4 and grades 2–3 Problem 6: move the placing game to directly after Problem 1** (reorder only). It is the emotional engine of the week. Its partner checks the rule, and the generic cases it produces can be seen at any drawing accuracy. Grades 4–5 already has it as Problem 3. Stopping anywhere then still leaves every child having played it.
2. **K–1 Problems 2 and 3 (pages 3–6): remove "every" for K–1, and move both after the game.**
   - Problem 2: use "Try D on each cross. Find two different splits whose regions meet". This keeps the outline's goal ("Make legal groups and point to an actual shared location") without the seven-case run.
   - Problem 3 (the collinear row): no page change rescues it for five-year-olds. The regions overlap invisibly, and the lesson needs exact collinearity. Put it last or drop it for K–1 (outline: for the organizer).
3. **K–1 Problem 5 (page 8) and grades 2–3 Problem 5 (page 8): stop at the crosses.**
   - K–1: delete "Find every place in the box where C can go", leaving "Try C on each cross. Which crosses work?". The children feel the answer there: two crosses on the line work and the one above fails.
   - Grades 2–3: same change, and split the three-dot question from the locus. Leave the full line to the adult, as a question for children who ask what happens between the crosses.
4. **Collinear boards: make the two regions visible.**
   - Affected: K–1 Problem 3 (page 6), grades 2–3 Problem 2 (page 4), grades 4–5 Problem 2 (page 4).
   - On the recording boards, print each row of dots with room above and below. Add one shared-rule sentence that on a line one group is drawn just above the dots and the other just below (the guide already describes this convention).
   - Without it, a child cannot see whether two segments overlap.
5. **K–1 page 1: cut the shared rules and the key for the K–1 packet.**
   - Rules: two sentences, using each letter once in two groups and drawing each group's stretched-band shape. Drop "Use the centers of the marks" and "Swapping the two groups is the same split" once K–1 no longer counts splits.
   - Key: keep only the K–L–M–N panel, which shows a whole split and its shared point. Keep E, F–G and H–I–J for the adult's board demonstration.
6. **Problem 1, all bands (pages 1–2): one drawing, not two.** Let the search happen with markers and a finger or a rubber band on page 1, and the drawing happen once on page 2. Or, for K–1, drop the markers and draw straight onto the page 2 boards. Also give K–1 page 2 larger boards, two per row, since the 5.35 cm boards are too small for crayon shading of a triangle. Check in a rehearsal whether markers hide the crosses and what tracing paper is for.
7. **Grades 2–3 Problem 4 (pages 6–7): reword.** Change "No split may work for both arrangements" to "Make two arrangements of A, B, C, and D that have no working split in common. Show a working split for each." The mathematics stays the same and is easier to read.
8. **Grades 4–5 Problem 7 (page 12): split it.** Make the three-dot question its own problem and the "smallest number of dots" guarantee a separate final problem, so each is one clear task.
9. **Grades 4–5 Problem 4 (pages 6–7): trim to one page of boards.** The 2+2 pair of pages is more record space than the four working splits need. Fewer boards puts the weight on the "always exists… Explain" question.
10. **K–1 materials (outline: for the organizer).** K–1 meets the bar only if the materials enforce "stretched band" and "meet". Two rubber bands per child on a pegboard or pushpin board, in place of drawing, would make legal groups physical and the meeting visible. The outline has one rubber band for the launch only, so no page change can do this.
