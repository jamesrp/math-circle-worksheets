Reviewed from the draft PDFs rendered at 70 dpi (all 7 + 8 + 8 pages) and the problem sources. Not piloted or rehearsed. Run lengths quoted below were checked by brute force (0–4 starts: 4 moves is the most common length, 376 of 625, and 7 moves is rare, 16 of 625; 0–5 starts: 768 of 1,296 last 4 and 32 last 7). Both printed orders in Problem 2, (0,2,1,4) and (0,1,4,2), last 4 moves; every order with 2 opposite 0 lasts 7.

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** The single comparison is excellent for this age: two towers side by side, "the extra part is 3, so new A is 3", with the two mats keeping the old ring intact so the in-place error from the lamp session cannot happen. But a full move is four comparisons, a wraparound arrow, placing each extra part "at the arrow's start", clearing, sliding four towers across and dropping a counter, and the thing the problems ask about (how long a run lasts) appears only after several such moves. That is a procedure the parent volunteer will sequence for them, not a move a five-year-old makes alone.

**Page: workable.** The pages are mostly large mats and reference rings, which suits non-readers, and the problems are short ("Find different starts that become all zeros in one move."). Against that, the rule paragraph on page 1 is four sentences of new vocabulary (old mat, new mat, extra part, arrow's start, cup), Problem 1 asks no question at all ("Build these starts and keep making new rings. Stop when all four towers are 0."), Problem 6 uses a term the K–1 rules never introduce ("Try both starts with the same gap rule"), and Problem 2's page has no mat to build on.

**K–1 bar: not met.** A child can make the core comparison within a minute of the launch. What keeps it from strong: the rules a child cannot hold at once (which pair next, which station gets the extra part, all four before clearing, D back to A), none of which the materials enforce except "keep the old ring"; and the payoff of each run arriving only after 8 to 28 compare-and-build actions. With the parent pointing at each arrow it will run; without that it stalls after the first arrow or two.

A K–1 child, minute by minute (a kindergartner in a pair):
- Minute 5: standing towers 1 and 1 side by side, saying "no extra", unsure whether that means an empty station or no tower; partner builds. The adult points to the next arrow.
- Minute 15: still in Problem 1, third or fourth start. Hands busy and happy, but each start is the same routine; the gasp is when (1,1,1,1) turns into nothing.
- Minute 30: in Problem 2 or 3, running a seven-move run with heights to 4, or tired of building. Decisions are only "which start", and most choices come out at 3 or 4 moves.
Would ask to do it again: the vanishing ring and "make it disappear in one move", yes. Another four-start run, probably not.

**Patterns found**
1. Record-keeping: page 3 (Problem 2) and page 4 (Problem 3) have rows for each start plus "Moves to zero: ___"; page 6–7 (Problem 6) has 6-row tables for 4 and 3 stations. The adult writes all of these; for Problem 6 the table is what tells you "the whole ring repeats", so it is the adult who spots the repeat. The cup counter is the right kind of record and serves a goal the child cares about.
2. Precision: not present. Cubes are discrete and matching towers is self-checking. Whether 2 cm cubes sit comfortably in 36 mm stations and stay put when slid across needs a rehearsal.
3. Procedure instead of choices: pages 2–4, Problems 1–3. Problem 1 is four full runs with no decision; Problems 2 and 3 offer one decision (the start) followed by up to seven moves of fixed procedure. The choice-rich, quick-feedback tasks (Problem 4, one move; Problem 5, two moves) come last, at minute 30+.
4. Told, not felt: mostly avoided. The three-station cycle on pages 6–7 is visible in the cubes (the empty spot walks around the triangle), though a five-year-old will likely declare "it repeats" at (1,1,0) after (1,0,1), before the whole labelled ring returns, which is harmless. Problem 5 (exactly two moves) needs backward reasoning ("what becomes all the same?") that the parent volunteer is unlikely to prompt without the guide.
5. Adult runs it: page 1 rules (four sentences read once), the cycle order of arrows, the record rows, and the two stopping conditions in Problem 6 ("Stop at all zeros or when the whole ring repeats").

**Most likely delight:** a ring collapsing to nothing all at once, and then Problem 4, choosing four equal towers (say 4,4,4,4) and watching all sixteen cubes disappear in one move. Second: the triangle that never stops.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Third graders can run the move with cubes or switch to numbers, and the problems have goals they will care about: "Which order lasts longest?", "Find a ring that lasts as many moves as you can", and the puzzle-like "Could each ring be the result of one move? Find an old ring that makes it, or explain why no old ring can work." With a mathematician at the table the predecessor obstructions and the three-station contrast give real explaining.

**Page: workable.** Problems are short and well-ordered, but Problem 2 (page 3) has two reference rings and half a page of nothing: no mat and no rows, so children flip back to page 2 to build and keep run lengths in their heads while comparing orders. Problem 3 records only starts, so counting a seven-move run is mental. Page 1's "becomes the new height at its tail" leans on the word "tail"; the worked example rescues it.

A grade 3 child: at minute 5, building (1,1,0,0) and calling out gaps fast, moving to numbers soon. At minute 15, in Problem 2 having found two orders that both last 4, trying a third (a one-in-three chance of 7). At minute 30, on Problem 4 trying to build an old ring for (1,1,1,0) and failing repeatedly; an adult has to say why, or the group settles for "we couldn't".

**Patterns found**
1. Record-keeping: light. Page 6–7 (Problem 5) tables of 6 rows serve the "does it repeat" question and are fine.
2. Precision: none.
3. Procedure: pages 2–4, Problems 1–3, are runs of a fixed rule; the choice is the start. Third graders tolerate this better than K–1 and most will compute mentally, but Problem 3's open search mostly yields 4s (60% of starts), so it can feel like the same answer repeatedly unless the child brings Problem 2's 7 into it.
4. Told, not felt: page 5, Problem 4, ring (1,1,1,0): the impossibility comes from an even-total argument children will not see by trying; the adult will likely announce it. Ring (0,0,0,2) is the opposite: "three zero gaps make all four towers equal" is an explanation a third grader can find and say.
5. Adult runs it: not a concern at this table.

**Most likely delight:** finding an order of 0, 1, 2, 4 that keeps going to 7 after two that stopped at 4; and the three-station ring that never ends.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** "Do four nonnegative whole-number heights always reach all zeros?" is a question ten-year-olds want answered, and the packet builds to it: runs, longest search, predecessors, the triangle that never ends, then parity, doubling and a bound. Problem 7 ("Could any new height exceed the largest starting height?") is felt in the cubes, since the extra part is part of the taller tower. The doubling comparison in Problem 6 can be seen directly: each tower of the doubled run is exactly two of the original's.

**Page: workable.** Three gaps. Problem 5 (page 7) moves the rule onto paper letters ("Replace even heights by E and odd heights by O. Use this two-letter gap rule."), a rule that exists only on the page, with two five-row tables for a task that means checking every kind of start for four moves. Problem 6 (page 8) asks to "compare these two starts through several moves" with no mat and no rows, and the doubled start needs 36 of the pair's 40 cubes, so the two runs cannot be built side by side and must be compared by writing them down somewhere. Problems 7 and 8 share the bottom half of page 8 with about an inch of space each, though Problem 8 is the culminating explanation.

A grade 4 child: at minute 5, running (0,0,0,2) with cubes, quickly to numbers. At minute 15, in the longest-ring search and the predecessor puzzles, arguing with the referee. At minute 30, on page 7 writing E/O rows: a careful child grinds through a couple of starts and loses interest before covering all kinds; an impatient one asks the organizer what the point is.

**Patterns found**
1. Record-keeping: page 7, Problem 5, the E/O tables. Covering all sixteen parity starts for four moves each is bookkeeping that confirms a fact, unless the trio splits the starts.
2. Precision: none.
3. Procedure: page 7, Problem 5, letter-by-letter updating for 4 moves per start, where the interesting fact (everything even) shows only at the end. Page 8, Problem 6: two six-move runs, including towers of 10.
4. Told, not felt: page 8, Problem 8, the general termination argument (four rounds make everything even, then halve) will be the organizer's to assemble from children's parity and doubling observations; that is appropriate at the end of the packet and with that adult.
5. Adult runs it: not a concern; the referee role suits the predecessor puzzles.

**Most likely delight:** the three-station ring repeating forever after every four-station ring died, which makes "do four always die?" a real question; and seeing the doubled run stay exactly doubled.

## What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle

- How long one move takes a K–1 pair (my guess is 60 to 90 seconds), and so whether Problem 1's four runs fit before attention runs out.
- Whether 2 cm linking cubes fit and stay upright in the 36 mm stations, and whether towers slide to the new mat without labels getting mixed.
- Whether K–1 children will consistently put the extra part at the arrow's tail, and whether the D-to-A wraparound gets missed. (Putting every gap at the head instead only rotates the ring and gives the same run length; mixing the two does not.)
- Whether the cup and counters exist: the K–1 page relies on them, but the outline's materials list only cubes and pencils.
- Whether third graders find 7 in Problem 2 within a few tries, and how they react when nothing beats 7 in Problem 3.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1, pages 2–5, order of problems.** Move the one-move task (Problem 4) to directly after Problem 1, and cut Problem 1 to two starts, (1,1,0,0) and (0,0,0,2), as the grades 4–5 page already does. Children then get the "make it vanish" choice and its payoff by minute 15, and the procedure-heavy runs of Problems 2–3 come after it. Page change; keeps the activity.
2. **K–1, page 2, Problem 1.** Give it a question the children care about, matching the older bands: "Which one takes the most moves?" The cup already counts moves.
3. **Grades 4–5, page 7, Problem 5.** Keep the parity idea but make it buildable: an even height is an empty station and an odd height is one cube, so the ordinary gap rule with cubes of 0 and 1 is the E/O rule. State that correspondence in the problem, drop the printed four-line letter rule, and let the trio split the starts. Page change; rescues this problem.
4. **Grades 4–5, page 8, Problems 6–8.** Give Problem 6 two record tables of six rows each, one for each start; move Problems 7 and 8 to a new page with real writing space, Problem 8 getting most of it.
5. **Grades 2–3, page 3, Problem 2 (and K–1 page 3).** Add the paired mat, or at least run rows with "Moves to zero" as on K–1 page 3, so children are not building on the previous page and remembering lengths. The 2–3 page has the space.
6. **K–1, page 1, rules.** Cut to what the launch does not already show: two sentences ("Stand the two old towers on an arrow side by side. Build the extra part on the new mat where the arrow starts."), and let the demonstration carry clearing, moving and the cup. Add the cup and counters (or a spare cube colour as the counter) to the preparation list.
7. **K–1, page 6, Problem 6.** Replace "with the same gap rule" with words K–1 have heard: "Try both starts. Stop when all the towers are 0 or the ring comes back."
8. **K–1, page 5, Problem 5 (exactly two moves).** Keep it last; it needs a backward question the parent volunteer may not know to ask. A page change cannot fix that; it needs a one-line hint in the adult guide (outline: for the organizer).
9. **Grades 2–3, page 5, Problem 4, ring (1,1,1,0).** No page change needed; the facilitator should expect to supply the even-total reason, while (0,0,0,2) is the one children can explain themselves.
