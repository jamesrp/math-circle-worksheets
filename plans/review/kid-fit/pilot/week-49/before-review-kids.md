This review comes from the rendered pages and the outline only. Nobody has piloted these pages. How long one move takes at each table, whether children keep the old ring untouched, and whether 40 cubes per pair are enough once moves are counted can only be settled by a rehearsal or a pilot.

I checked a few facts to ground the judgements. Problem 1's starts take 3, 2, 2 and 4 moves. For starts from 0 to 4 (K–1 Problem 2), 400 of 625 take 3 or 4 moves and only 16 take 7. For 0 to 5, 32 of 1,296 take 7. The two orders printed in K–1 Problem 6 (2–3 Problem 5), (0,2,1,4) and (0,1,4,2), both take 4 moves. The unprinted order (0,1,2,4) takes 7, and it is a third of all 24 arrangements. (2,5,1,0) and (4,10,2,0) both take 6 moves, and the second run is exactly double the first at every step. One fact will help adults: putting the gap at an arrow's head instead of its tail only relabels the stations, so move counts don't change. A child who mixes up head and tail but does it the same way every time loses nothing. Adults need only check that they are consistent.

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** The core action really is concrete: stand two cube towers side by side, see the part that doesn't match, and build a tower that tall. A five-year-old can do one such comparison within a minute of the launch. One *move*, though, is four comparisons. The child also has to know which pair feeds which new station (the arrow-tail convention exists only on paper), must leave the old ring alone until all four are built, then clears the old mat, moves the towers across and keeps count of moves. The materials enforce none of that sequence. With a non-mathematician parent and two pairs, the adult will probably run the cycle ("now C and D… now clear… that's move 3") while children build single gaps.

**Page: weak.** The opening rules are an adult paragraph: "Follow each arrow: the gap between its two old heights becomes the new height at its tail. Make every new tower before changing any old tower. An all-zero start takes 0 moves." The volunteer has to turn "tail" into an action. The worked example ("Edge A to B has gap 3, so new A is 3") helps. Problem 1 ("Build these starts and keep making new rings") has no working mat on its page; the mats are on page 2. Problem 4 ("first become all zeros on the second move") is hard to follow after one hearing. Problem 2's open search comes before Problem 6, which hands children a contrast that produces a long run.

**K–1 bar: not met.** A child can make one gap within a minute, but not the full move. The rule about *which* pair goes to *which* new tower, and the old/new sequencing, are enforced by the adult, not by the cubes or a partner. Counting moves across a 4–7 move run is also held in someone's head.

**Patterns found**
- *3. A procedure instead of choices.* p. 1, Problem 1: four given starts, 11 moves in all, each move four builds plus a clear-and-move. The child decides nothing; the payoff ("all gone") comes at the end of each run. That payoff is real and repeats four times, which softens this, but at K–1 speed this one problem could take 15–20 minutes. Only a pilot can say.
- *5. The adult runs it.* p. 1 rules paragraph; p. 2 Problem 2 "count the move that makes all four towers 0"; p. 4 Problem 5 "Stop at all zeros or when the whole ring repeats". Spotting a repeat means comparing the current ring with earlier rows of the 1–6 table, which the adult will do.
- *1. Record-keeping.* Light, and the page handles it well: one A–B–C–D row and "Moves to zero" per try (pp. 2, 5). The table on p. 4, Problem 5, does serve a goal (seeing the repeat), but a kindergartner won't fill it in.
- *3 again, as a plateau.* p. 2, Problem 2 ("Find a start that lasts as many moves as you can"): almost every start a child picks takes 3–4 moves, so trying again keeps giving the same result. The surprise of a 7 is unlikely to turn up by chance.
- *4. Told, not felt.* p. 3, Problem 4. Random tries almost never take exactly two moves, so the child needs the backward idea "what ring turns into 1,1,1,1?", which an adult will probably supply. Problem 3 (all towers equal, gone in one move) is felt directly: no gaps anywhere.
- *2. Precision.* Not found. Cubes are discrete and the 36 mm stations are generous.

**Most likely delight:** the last towers vanishing at the end of a run ("they're all gone"). Next comes Problem 6 if a child finds the 0-next-to-1-and-4 order and their ring runs 7 moves where the printed orders ran 4. Then the three-station triangle in Problem 5, whose pattern keeps going round and never empties.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Third graders subtract within 5 instantly. The record hunt ("Find a ring that lasts as many moves as you can") becomes a table contest. Problem 3's backward puzzles ("Could each ring be the result of one move? … or explain why no old ring can work") give them two "impossible" findings they can actually explain. For (0,0,0,2), three zero gaps make all four towers equal. Problem 4's triangle that never stops ("Does the number of stations change what can happen?") is seen, not announced. With the mathematician at the table this should hold four children.

**Page: workable.** It is clean, and the tasks are open and substantial. Problem 5 (orders of 0,1,2,4) comes after Problem 2 even though it is what makes Problem 2's search pay off. Problem 6 says "Give an explanation or an example for each claim" after two questions, not claims, so children will ask what the claims are.

**Patterns found**
- *3. A procedure instead of choices.* pp. 1–2, Problems 1–2: running a ring is mechanical, but here it serves a goal the child cares about (longest run). Third graders may drop the cubes and run the numbers in their heads, which is fine. Risk: in Problem 2, most tries give 4 moves, so the contest stalls without Problem 5's contrast (see above).
- *4. Told, not felt.* p. 3, Problem 3, ring (1,1,1,0): the impossibility comes from "every output total is even", which third graders won't find alone. They will feel it ("we tried a lot and none work"), and the adult can let that stand as evidence. The other impossible ring, (0,0,0,2), they can explain themselves.
- *5. The adult runs it.* Not found beyond normal reading. Sentences are one or two lines.
- *1, 2.* Not found.

**Most likely delight:** the three-station triangle on p. 4 coming back to the same ring ("it never ends"), and beating the table's record in Problem 2 or 5 with a 6 or 7.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** Problems 5–8 build toward a real proof: the parity shadow, then doubling, then the bound, then "always reaches zero". The organizer is at this table and the third child can referee each new ring (checking a move is a natural referee job). The doubling comparison in Problem 6 is felt directly: lay the (2,5,1,0) and (4,10,2,0) runs side by side and every step is exactly double. With cubes, the 10-tower makes it physical.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1–4 repeat the 2–3 packet word for word, so a 4–5 table spends its first 20–25 minutes on material it will find easy, and the distinctive work (Problems 5–8) is reached late. Problem 5's four 5×4 letter grids ("Which letter rings are possible after four moves? Explain why you have checked every kind of start.") are 80 cells of E/O copying. Problem 8 ("Explain why every such start finishes") is a proof that only the organizer can steer.

**Patterns found**
- *1. Record-keeping.* p. 5, Problem 5: the grids confirm "everything becomes all E" one start at a time. The case check *is* the mathematics, but each child filling 16 letters per start is bookkeeping. Splitting the starts among the pair and the referee and pooling them suits this table.
- *4. Told, not felt.* p. 6, Problems 7–8. Problem 7 can be felt: a gap is the leftover part of the taller tower, so it can't be taller than that tower. Problem 8's chain (all even after four moves, halve, repeat, bounded) will probably be told by the organizer to most children. Every run they try does end, so the claim itself is felt; only the "why" is told.
- *3. A procedure instead of choices.* pp. 1–2, Problems 1–2 for this age: quick and low-choice. It's not harmful, but it delays the band's real content.
- *5. The adult runs it.* p. 6, Problem 8 (see above). Elsewhere not found.
- *2.* Not found.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 6, when a child notices that the doubled ring's run is the first run doubled at every step, and then that tripling must do the same thing.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1 p. 5 Problem 6 → move before p. 2 Problem 2; 2–3 p. 5 Problem 5 → move before Problem 2.** This change is page order only. The children first get a concrete contrast (the same four numbers in a different order last 4 or 7 moves) and then the open search, so the search has a target and a way in. It keeps the activity and fixes the plateau.
2. **All bands: give every pair a loose working-mat sheet.** Print page 2 (four-station old/new mats) and the p. 4 three-station mats as separate sheets, or put Problem 1's starts on the mat page. Problem 1 (p. 1) and K–1 Problem 5's four-station start currently have no mat beside them. Consider shading or outlining the "new" mat differently from the "old" one: old C and new D sit close together (p. 2), and a pilot should check whether children put cubes on the wrong one.
3. **K–1 p. 1 rules: rewrite for one hearing.** Use short spoken sentences, for example "Put the two towers on an arrow side by side. Build the extra part on the new mat, at the arrow's start. Keep the old towers until all four new ones are built." Let the worked picture carry the rest, and drop "tail" and "An all-zero start takes 0 moves" from the read-aloud. This page change helps, but it won't lift K–1 to the bar on its own.
4. **K–1: move counting done by an object (pp. 2, 5, "Moves to zero").** Have the partner drop one counter into a cup per move. Use counters kept apart from the 40 cubes, since a K–1 old ring plus new ring can use 32 cubes. Each child then has a job, builder or counter/checker, and the partner, not the adult, checks the sequence. This is a preparation change, not an outline change.
5. **K–1 p. 3 Problem 4: rewording.** "Find starts that take exactly two moves to become all zeros." The guide's backward hint ("what turns into 1,1,1,1?") stays with the adult.
6. **2–3 p. 5 Problem 6: wording.** Replace "for each claim" with "for each question".
7. **4–5 p. 5 Problem 5: lighter bookkeeping.** Keep the task and have the facilitator split the starts among the three children and pool them, since case coverage is the point. If the page changes, two grids per child are enough.
8. **4–5 pp. 1–2: shorten the shared opening so the band reaches Problems 5–6.** For example, cut Problem 1 to the (0,0,0,2) start and one other, or let the organizer move them on once Problem 1 is done in their heads. This is a page or route change; the activity stays.
9. **K–1 overall: outline, for the organizer.** Even with fixes 1–5, a full four-tower move is a multi-step procedure whose station assignment no material enforces. Whether K–1 children can run whole moves themselves, rather than building single gaps inside an adult-run cycle, is the open question. A pilot should settle it before any redesign; a page change can't.
