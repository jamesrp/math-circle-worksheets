Kid-fit review of the Week 16 draft (three-color triangles): F16-K-v3 (6 pages), F16-23-v3 (7 pages), F16-45-v3 (7 pages). I rendered and looked at every page. I have not watched children use these pages. The verdicts below are a filter, not a pilot result. I enumerated the boards to check what children will run into: on the 4-cell board every legal filling has exactly 1 all-three cell. On the 9-cell board the counts are 1, 3 or 5, and 5 is reached in 12 of 192 fillings. On the 16-cell board every one of the 16 cells can be the only all-three cell. On the fan board the fixed letters allow up to 7. The Grades 4–5 starred board has a zero filling in only about 1% of fillings, so random trying will not find one.

## K–1 (two kindergartners, two first graders, parent volunteer)

**Activity: workable.** Children place coloured counters on tinted dots and hunt for "a little triangle with R, B, and Y". That suits five-year-olds: the counters are in their hands right away, and the goals (most, none, move it somewhere new) are ones a child cares about. The hard part is judging success. Each problem needs someone to look at every little cell, upside-down ones included, and decide whether it has all three letters. A five-year-old will miss cells, and the parent volunteer will end up doing the checking.

**Page: workable.** Each page is one big board and one or two spoken sentences. "Problem 3: Fill the dots to make as many little triangles with R, B, and Y as you can" works after one hearing. Problem 1 does not: "put a little triangle with R, B, and Y in each of the four places, one at a time" is hard to follow aloud. Problem 6 asks children to record "four different fillings" on four tiny boards they cannot write on.

**K–1 bar: not met.** The core move does meet the bar: placing a red counter on a pink-tinted dot is something a non-reader can do within a minute. The tints make the counter colours enforce the side rules, as long as the counters are coloured as well as lettered. The bar fails on checking. Every problem's outcome depends on scanning 9 to 16 cells for three different letters, and nothing in the materials or the pairing does that check. Problem 2 makes it worst: the answer is "no", so a child who misses one cell will think they succeeded, and the parent volunteer, who is not a mathematician, has to recount the board each time. Three other things also keep it from strong. Problem 5 gives no way to keep track of places already found. The fan board in Problem 4 has thin cells, and "a little triangle has no lines inside" is hard to apply there. Problem 6 has the recording problem above.

Patterns found:
- **1, record-keeping.** K–1 p6, Problem 6: after finding a filling with counters, the child has to copy it onto one of four triangles about 1.5 inches wide whose dots are a few millimetres across. A kindergartner will be writing letters, or colouring dots, into specks while the real fun (finding the next filling) waits.
- **1, inverted: no record where one is needed.** K–1 p5, Problem 5: "put it in as many different places as you can" on 16 cells. Once the counters move, the child has nothing to show which places they have already done, so the count of "how many places" lives in memory.
- **2, precision.** K–1 p6, Problem 6 recording boards: the boards are too small for K–1 hands. K–1 p4, Problem 4 fan board: the thin cells around the printed letters make it hard to see which three dots belong to one little triangle.
- **5, adult runs it.** K–1 p2, Problem 2, and p5, Problem 5: whether "no triangle with R, B, and Y" or "just one" is true depends on checking every cell. The parent volunteer will be the one checking, and children will wait for a verdict.
- 3 (procedure) and 4 (told, not felt): not found. The "there's always one" surprise can be felt in Problem 1, where every filling of the 4-cell board has exactly one, and in Problem 2, if the checking is reliable.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 1, where one swap of a counter makes the all-three triangle jump to a new place, and the child notices it never disappears. Next is Problem 2, a hide-and-seek moment: "It snuck in again."

## Grades 2–3 (four third graders, mathematician)

**Activity: workable.** Two parts should land with a capable adult: a min/max hunt for all-three cells, and the rows of R…B dots where the number of doors always comes out odd (Problem 4: "Can any row have an even number of doors?"). Routes through doors can be a good maze game if an adult demonstrates them. The local-case enumeration and the final proof are where interest may drop.

**Page: weak.** The order works against the children. Problem 2 comes straight after the first hunt and is a five-sentence definition: "An edge with R at one end and B at the other is a door… A route must leave a two-door triangle through its other door; it stops at a one-door triangle or outside." Children meet it before they have used a door anywhere. The most concrete door task, the rows, waits until Problem 4. Problem 3 is a ten-case catalogue "counting turns and flips as the same". Problem 5 strings four steps together: fill 15 dots, keep exactly three all-three cells, mark doors, draw all routes.

Patterns found:
- **5, adult runs it.** 2–3 p3, Problem 2: the definition of doors and routes needs an adult to read it and demonstrate it. Children will watch the adult trace the first route, then copy it. The dashed example ("crosses edges; it does not follow them") helps but is itself a convention to decode.
- **3, procedure.** 2–3 p3, Problem 2, and p6, Problem 5: following a route is rule-following, and the payoff (one route comes back out, one ends in an all-three cell) comes only after every door is marked and every route traced. In Problem 5 that comes only after the child has also built a filling with exactly three all-three cells and checked all 16 cells.
- **1, record-keeping.** 2–3 p4, Problem 3: twelve blank triangles to fill with every labelling up to turns and flips (there are 10). The children's job turns into making sure the catalogue is complete and has no duplicates. Spotting duplicates up to flips is hard for eight-year-olds. The question that matters ("Could a triangle have three doors?", and which triangles have exactly one door) needs only a handful of cases.
- **1/2, pencil exploration.** 2–3 p1–2, Problem 1: the hunt is done by writing and erasing letters in 0.3-inch rings. Each try costs erasing, so children will try fewer fillings than they would with counters.
- 4 (told, not felt): mostly avoided. The rows problem lets children feel "always odd". Problem 6's "reason that covers every filling" will mostly be adult-led, which is fine as the last problem.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 4, trying hard to make a row with an even number of doors and failing every time. Next is Problem 2: the moment a route the child expected to be trapped walks back out of the big triangle.

## Grades 4–5 (two fourth graders, one fifth grader, organizer)

**Activity: strong.** Problem 1 is the hook: "Which counts between your two records can you make?" Children who find 1 and 5 and then fail to make 2 or 4 find the odd-count theorem for themselves. Problem 4's rows, with R…B and R…R ends, give the reason. Problem 6's starred dot ("Find a filling with no little triangle that has R, B, and Y") is a hard puzzle: random filling almost never works, so it rewards the door idea. With the organizer at the table this has real depth and a way in for every child.

**Page: workable.** It has the same order problem as 2–3: the route definition is Problem 2, before local cases (Problem 3) and rows (Problem 4). Problem 3 is again a full catalogue ("Find every different filling… Record the number of doors in each filling"). Ten-year-olds can read and manage this, but the catalogue is the slowest part of the packet.

Patterns found:
- **3, procedure.** 4–5 p3, Problem 2: tracing all routes on a 16-cell board with a fixed labelling is mechanical once doors are marked. It comes before children have a question that routes answer, because the odd-count puzzle from Problem 1 is only explained later.
- **1, record-keeping.** 4–5 p4, Problem 3: the ten-case catalogue up to symmetry. The useful fact (exactly one door exactly when the cell has R, B and Y) comes after a lot of bookkeeping.
- **5, adult runs it (mild).** 4–5 p3, Problem 2: three sentences of route rules plus the dashed-route convention. The organizer will probably demonstrate them. With three children, the referee role does not fit this problem well.
- **1/2, pencil exploration (mild).** 4–5 p1–2, Problem 1: "which counts can you make" needs many fillings, and each one has to be erased and rewritten.
- 4 (told, not felt): not found. The odd counts are felt in Problem 1, and the boundary condition is felt in Problem 6.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 1, when a child says "We can't make 2 or 4!" Next is Problem 6, finding the zero filling after the star breaks the rule.

## What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle

- Whether 3/4-inch counters fit the 15-dot K–1 board (Problem 5) without children knocking neighbours. Once counters cover the tints, a child cannot re-check the side rule.
- Whether kindergartners see upside-down cells as "little triangles", and whether they wrongly count the large triangles made of several cells.
- How long one full check of a 9-cell and a 16-cell board takes a K–1 child, and whether the parent volunteer can keep up with four children.
- Whether third graders can trace routes after one demonstration, and how long Problem 1 holds each band.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1 p2, Problem 2: let a partner do the check.** Make the problem a hiding game: one child fills the board trying to have no triangle with R, B and Y, and the partner hunts for one. Swap roles. The checking then comes from the other player instead of the parent volunteer, and every "found it" becomes part of the game. Same board, counters and question.
2. **2–3 and 4–5: reorder so doors get concrete work before routes.** Move the rows problem (now Problem 4) to Problem 2 and define "door" there in one sentence. Next comes the single-triangle problem (now Problem 3), then routes (now Problem 2). The route problem can then drop the door definition and keep only the route rule.
3. **2–3 p4 and 4–5 p4, Problem 3: drop the complete catalogue.** Replace "Find every different filling, counting turns and flips as the same" with a task that keeps the point: label these triangles in different ways and mark the doors; which ones have exactly one door, and could one have three? Two pairs can split the cases and pool them, as the shared-catalogue practice allows. Keep the boards.
4. **K–1 p5, Problem 5: give children a way to record places found.** Have children put a pencil X inside each cell once it has been the only all-three triangle. The cell interiors are already left clear, so the X does not interfere with the counters.
5. **K–1 p6, Problem 6: remove the tiny recording boards, or replace them with two boards large enough to colour.** Children colour each dot with the three coloured pencils instead of writing letters. Alternatively, keep the four fillings on the big board with counters and have the adult watch each one. Ask for two fillings, then more if the child wants to continue.
6. **K–1 p1, Problem 1: make the sentence work aloud.** For example: "Move the counters so the triangle with R, B, and Y is in a new place. Find all four places." Same board and task.
7. **2–3 and 4–5, Problem 1: let children use counters for the hunt.** If each K–1 pair shares one counter set, two sets are free for the 2–3 table. The 4–5 table can share them later. Writing letters is then needed only to keep a record of a best or worst filling. This stays within the listed materials, but whether the sets are enough is a preparation question for the organizer.
8. **2–3 p6, Problem 5: shorten the multi-step task.** Keep "exactly three little triangles have R, B, and Y" and the question "Can all those routes end outside?" Drop "draw all routes": ask only about the routes from the outside doors on the bottom side, which is where the question lives. The page change is enough; the activity does not need redesigning.
9. **K–1 p4, Problem 4 fan board: no page change; check this one in the pilot.** If kindergartners cannot pick out the thin cells, use this board only with the first graders. Grades 4–5 Problem 5 already uses the same board.

No band needs "outline: for the organizer". The activity is sound in every band, and the weaknesses above can be fixed on the page.
