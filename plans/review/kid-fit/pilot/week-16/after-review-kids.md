Kid-fit review of the draft only. I rendered and looked at every page of all three PDFs. To see how long the searches are, I enumerated every allowed filling of the printed boards: the 6-dot board always has exactly 1 all-three cell (8 fillings); the 10-dot board gives 1, 3 or 5 (192 fillings, 12 reach 5); the 15-dot board gives 1 to 9 (13,824 fillings, 21% have exactly one, 45% exactly three); the relaxed-bottom 10-dot board has 52 of 432 fillings with none. I have not watched children use these pages, so every verdict below is a filter, not a pilot result.

## K–1

**Activity: strong.** Lettered counters on a small board, plus the question "is there a little triangle with R, B and Y?", is a move a kindergartner can make with their hands and check by eye. Problem 2 turns the theorem into a game children can feel: "One of you fills the dots and tries to make no little triangle with R, B, and Y. The other hunts for one … Can the filler ever win?" The filler loses every time, and the impossibility arrives as repeated losses, with nobody announcing it.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1, 2 and 4 are right for this table. Problem 1 has only three loose dots, and "Find all four places" is a finite goal they can finish. On Problem 4 (fan board), each of the four empty dots is its own small decision about which letter lights up two cells. Two things pull the page down. Problem 5 ("Make just one little triangle with R, B, and Y … Move the counters to put it in as many other places as you can") sits on the 15-dot, 16-cell board and needs a full rescan after every move. Problem 6, the payoff that lets the filler win, comes last, where this table will rarely get to it.

**Why the K–1 bar is not met.** The core move is quick: within a minute a child can put three counters on Problem 1 and point at the R-B-Y triangle. What keeps it from strong is that the side rule lives only in the words "R or Y" / "B or Y" / "R or B" and the split tints, and no partner is asked to check it. In Problem 2 a filler who puts a Y counter on a bottom dot can make zero, so the game's whole lesson depends on the parent volunteer catching that. This is the lamp-puzzle failure mode (a rule that exists only on paper). The second reason is Problem 5: checking "just one" across 16 cells falls to the adult.

**Patterns found**
- 1, record-keeping: mild. Problem 5 (page 5) asks for an X in each new place. The marks serve the goal, but they pile up next to 12 counters on a crowded board.
- 2, precision: none in the mathematics. Pilot only: whether ¾-inch counters on 1.31-inch spacing leave the down-pointing cells of the 15-dot board visible enough to hunt in, and whether an X can be drawn between counters.
- 3, procedure: Problem 5 (page 5). The child shuffles 12 counters, and after each change someone has to scan all 16 cells to confirm that exactly one is all-three. The interesting thing (where can it go?) shows up only after a long rebuild-and-check loop.
- 4, told, not felt: none. The guarantee is felt in the Problem 2 game. The contrast in Problem 6 is felt too, but only if the table reaches it.
- 5, the adult runs it: the side rule (top of page 1, used everywhere), and the "just one" check in Problem 5.

**Moment most likely to delight:** Problem 2, when the hunter finds an R-B-Y triangle yet again and the filler says "Again, I'll beat it this time." Second: Problem 6, when the filler suddenly can win, if the table gets there.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: workable.** The opening is good for third graders. In Problem 1 they chase "as few … as you can" and run into the fact that zero never comes. In Problem 2 ("Can any row have an even number of doors?") they try to break a pattern and can't. The middle of the activity is the door-and-route argument, which is a long chain (door, door count per triangle, route rule, pairing of outside doors). It needs the mathematician to carry its meaning, and for some children the explanation will be told rather than felt.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1 and 2 will hold attention. Problem 3 is a catalog: twelve blank triangles, three letters each, then doors marked. Problem 4's rule is dense for an eight-year-old: "A route must leave a two-door triangle through its other door; it stops at a one-door triangle or outside," plus a two-line example caption. Problem 5 stacks a filling puzzle on the 16-cell board on top of route tracing.

**Patterns found**
- 1, record-keeping: Problem 3 (page 4). The child writes about 36 letters into twelve small triangles and marks doors to confirm a classification. Only the last question ("Could a triangle have three doors?") is something a child would chase for its own sake.
- 2, precision: none significant. Route lines through 1.3-inch cells are fine in pencil. Repeatedly erasing letters on small dot circles in Problems 1, 5 and 6 is friction if no counters are given (see fix 7).
- 3, procedure: Problem 4 (page 5). Mark 9 doors, then follow three routes cell by cell. The interesting thing (one route ends inside an all-three cell; the other two pair up and leave) appears only at the end of each trace. Problem 5 (page 6): fill 12 dots, check 16 cells for exactly three, mark doors, then trace every bottom-door route.
- 4, told, not felt: Problem 4's question "Which outside doors lead back outside?" asks for a fact about one printed board. Why the answer matters (outside doors pair up, so an odd one is left over) has to come from the adult. Problem 6 ("Give a reason that covers every filling") is a mathematician-led conversation for most third graders.
- 5, the adult runs it: Problem 4's rule paragraph and example caption, and the two-stage Problem 5.

**Moment most likely to delight:** Problem 2, trying hard to make an even row, erasing, trying again, and finding it always comes out odd. Close behind: Problem 1, when "as few as you can" stalls at one.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** With the organizer at the table, this band can act, observe and check all the way to the proof. Problem 1 adds the question that makes parity felt: "Which counts between your two records can you make?" A child with counters finds 1, 3 and 5 but never 2 or 4. Problem 6 lets them break the theorem by relaxing one starred dot: "Find a filling with no little triangle that has R, B, and Y. Explain why this does not contradict your answer to Problem 5."

**Page: workable.** Problems 1, 2 (rows ending R…B against R…R) and 6 are strong. The page loses momentum at Problem 3, the same twelve-triangle catalog, which a ten-year-old will reach around minute 15 to 20. Problem 4 ("Draw all the routes") on the 15-dot board means marking 14 doors and tracing 11 two-door cells. That is purposeful, but long. Problem 5's proof is an organizer conversation. That suits this table, but the printed sentence ("on this board and on any division into little triangles that meet only at corners or along whole edges") is a paragraph of hypotheses.

**Patterns found**
- 1, record-keeping: Problem 3 (page 4), as in grades 2–3. Problem 1's two recording boards are light (10 dots) and serve a goal.
- 2, precision: none.
- 3, procedure: Problem 4 (page 5), 14 doors marked before any route is drawn. Acceptable at this age because the question at the end is real.
- 4, told, not felt: none serious. Parity is felt in Problem 1 and the boundary hypothesis is felt in Problem 6.
- 5, the adult runs it: Problem 5 (page 6) is by design an adult-led proof conversation. The risk is low with the organizer at the table.

**Moment most likely to delight:** Problem 1, "we can make 1, 3 and 5 but never 2 or 4." Then Problem 6, beating the rule with one starred dot.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1, Problem 6 (page 6): move it to just after Problem 2 and pose it as the same game.** For example: "Play the game from Problem 2 again. Now the bottom side allows R, B, or Y." The contrast (the filler can finally win) is the K–1 table's best felt surprise, and right now it is last, where most children won't reach it. This is a page change only.
2. **K–1, shared rule at the top of page 1: make the side rule something a child can check, and give the hunter the job of checking it.** For example, add to Problem 2: "The hunter also checks that every counter matches a color on its dot." This makes the partner enforce the rule. One caveat: the outline says letters, not tints, decide. If tint matching is unacceptable (for example for a colorblind child), demonstrate the side check in the launch and have the hunter check letters against the side labels instead.
3. **K–1, Problem 5 (page 5): drop "just one".** The smallest version: "Draw an X in every little triangle that you can make have R, B, and Y." Or keep "just one" and move it to the 10-dot board, where there are 9 cells to check, not 16. Either removes the rebuild-and-rescan loop. Problem 1 already covers moving a single all-three cell at the small scale.
4. **Grades 2–3 and 4–5, Problem 3 (page 4): shrink the catalog.** Print one large triangle to fill with counters, plus about six small recording triangles. Keep both questions, so children try fillings with counters and write down only the ones they want to keep. This keeps the local classification and removes most of the transcription.
5. **Grades 2–3, Problem 5 (page 6): use the 10-dot board instead of the 15-dot board.** Exactly three all-three cells is reachable there (72 of 192 fillings). There are 9 cells to check, not 16, and fewer doors to trace, so the bottom-door route question keeps its weight.
6. **Grades 2–3, Problem 1 (page 1): add the 4–5 question "Which counts between your two records can you make?"** Finding only odd counts is the cheapest felt version of the theorem, and this band currently never asks for it directly.
7. **Grades 2–3 and 4–5, Problem 1 (page 1): reconcile "Try fillings with R, B, and Y counters" with the materials.** The materials list has counters only as four K–1 sets. Either give the older tables the two sets the K–1 pairs won't need (the outline's four sets for four K–1 children allow this), or say "pencil". This is a preparation question to settle in rehearsal.
8. **Grades 2–3, Problem 4 (page 5): trim the reading.** The second caption sentence ("The dashed route crosses edges; it does not follow them.") can go if the launch or adult shows a route crossing a door. Leave the rule sentence in the problem. This is a page change. The activity's reliance on the mathematician to give routes their meaning is a matter for the outline (outline: for the organizer), not something the page can fix.

**What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle:** whether ¾-inch counters leave cells visible on the 15-dot K–1 board, and whether K–1 children notice down-pointing cells when hunting. Also: how long one round of the Problem 2 game takes; whether hand-lettering 180 counters is realistic preparation for the volunteer; how long third graders need to absorb the route rule in Problem 4; and whether the K–1 table reaches anything past Problem 4 in forty minutes.
