I rendered every page of all three draft PDFs at 70 dpi into `renders/` and read every page. These verdicts are a filter, not a pilot result. Nobody has watched children use this packet.

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** The core move is quick and physical: put counters on a grid so the circled numbers come out right. Problem 1 opens on a 2-by-2 board with every count 1, and two different answers (the two diagonals) exist. The best idea for this age is the game in Problem 6, "Can you make one so that your partner cannot make a different picture with those counts?" There the partner, not the adult, judges the result. The activity stays at workable for two reasons. The counts are a two-direction counting check that no material enforces: a miscounted column looks just like a correct picture. And a kindergartner has to hold the row numbers and the column numbers in mind together, which is the "two or three things" limit.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1, 3, 4 and 5 are short and read well aloud, and the boards carry the task. In Problem 3 (three boards per count set, each set with exactly 3 answers) and in Problem 4 (six 3-by-3 boards for the six one-per-row, one-per-column pictures), the number of boards gives a young child a target. The weak spots are page 1 and the wording of Problem 6. Page 1 spends a whole page on an example whose key is a dashed-arrow and dotted-arrow legend, "Across each row: - - - >" and "Down each column: ……>". A child who can't read can't use the difference between dashed and dotted lines. Problem 6 stacks two negatives ("so that your partner cannot make a different picture"), so it is hard to follow after one hearing.

**K–1 bar: not met.** Within a minute of the launch, a child can place counters without reading. What they can't do on their own is know whether the picture is right. The materials enforce only "one counter per cell". Matching the counts is checked by the child's own counting, so at a table run by the parent volunteer, wrong pictures will be accepted unless a partner or the adult checks every line. The page-1 legend also needs an adult to explain it, and Problems 2 and 6 ask the child to write four or five digits into small circles before the partner's turn can start.

**Child at the table (a kindergartner paired with a first grader):**
- **Minute 5.** Their hands are moving counters on the 2-by-2 board in Problem 1. They make the diagonal picture, then hear "make a different one" and slide the counters to the other diagonal. They are waiting for the adult to confirm the counts.
- **Minute 15.** In Problem 3 or 4 they are building one-per-row pictures and then drawing dots on the board where the counters were. On 3-by-3 boards they repeat pictures and need help spotting duplicates.
- **Minute 30.** If the adult reaches Problem 6 and runs it as a stump-your-partner game, the children are deciding where to put four counters. This is the part they would ask to play again.

**Patterns:**
1. *Record-keeping, mild.* Problems 3 and 4 (pp. 4–6) say "Find and draw every", so the child copies each counter picture into dots. Each pair has about 12 counters (24 per table), and Problem 4's six pictures need 18, so the catalog cannot stay built and children must draw each picture before they reuse its counters. The drawing serves the goal of filling the boards, but lifting a counter and then dotting where it was is fiddly at age 5.
2. *Precision:* not found. The cells are about 22 mm and the counters 15–16 mm.
3. *Procedure:* not found. Every placement is a choice.
4. *Told, not felt:* mostly not found. The surprise that the same numbers can come from different pictures can be seen and handled at 2-by-2. Problem 5 includes two count sets with only one picture (rows 3,1 with columns 2,1,1, and rows 2,1,0 with columns 2,1,0). Children can feel that impossibility, because every try comes back to the same picture. Some kindergartners may decide they failed and wait for an adult to say "it can't be done".
5. *Adult runs it, partly.* The page-1 legend and the unenforced count check both put the parent volunteer in the checking role. In Problems 2 and 6 (pp. 3, 8) the child also writes the counts before the partner's move can begin.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 6, when a child builds a picture (for example a full row A and a single counter at B1) and the partner cannot find another one: "you can't change it". Problem 1's diagonal flip on the 2-by-2 board is a close second.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** The order goes from finding ambiguity to proving a list complete, then moving two counters, then a switch, then why fewer switches can't work. That is a real line of discovery, and every step is something the hands do with counters. Problem 1 mixes a count set with two pictures and one with three, plus "rows 3,1 / columns 2,1,1", which has only one picture ("if you can"). So in the first five minutes, third graders meet both "there are more" and "there's only one". With a mathematician at the table, Problem 6, "Explain why one switch cannot reach it", is within reach.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1–4 and 6 are clean and give room to work. Problem 3, "Move exactly two counters … Find and draw every way", is a good concrete introduction to the switch before it gets a name. Problem 5 (pp. 6–7) is where the page sags. It opens with a long definition: "A switch moves the two counters to the empty corners shown; every other cell stays fixed. The corners may use any two rows and columns." Then it asks children to "Find every switch in each picture and draw its rectangle". The rectangles are drawn on pre-printed dot pictures, and page 7 is four identical copies of the same picture with no word about why. A third grader will ask "these are all the same, what do I do?"

**Child at the table (a third grader):**
- **Minute 5.** Problem 1: they build a picture, swap a counter to get a second one, then fight with "3,1 / 2,1,1" and announce it can't be done. A gasp or a protest is likely here, in a good way.
- **Minute 15.** Problem 2 (rows 2,1,1 with columns 2,1,1; there are 5 pictures and the page has 6 boards): they are building and drawing, and arguing over whether they have all of them. "How can you be sure your list is complete?" is the real question, and it fits.
- **Minute 30.** Problem 4's stump-your-partner game, or Problem 5. In Problem 5 they are scanning a 3-by-3 picture for rectangles, which is a checklist of nine row-and-column pairs. Rectangles with non-adjacent corners (rows A and C, or B and C, with columns 1 and 3) are easy to miss. Unless the counters are actually moved, this is the slowest stretch.

**Patterns:**
1. *Record-keeping, in Problem 5 (pp. 6–7).* Drawing the rectangle of every switch on printed pictures confirms the switches without using them. The goal the child cares about ("which pictures can't change?") is reached through an exhaustive hunt.
2. *Precision:* not found.
3. *Procedure, mild, in Problem 5.* "Find every switch" in a 3-by-3 picture is a systematic search over row pairs and column pairs. In Problem 3 the same idea works because it is posed as moving counters.
4. *Told, not felt:* not found. Problem 6's "two switches, not one" can be felt on the 2-by-4 board, where each switch fixes one wrong counter in row A.
5. *Adult runs it, partly, in Problem 5.* The adult will have to demonstrate the definition, especially "any two rows and columns". The small "One switch" diagram helps, but its counters are not counter-sized.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 1's "if you can" case turning out impossible, and Problem 3, where moving two counters leaves every number unchanged. Problem 4 as a stump-your-partner game is also likely to land.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** This group is three children with the organizer. The sequence goes from all six pictures on a 2-by-4 board, to switch connectivity and distance, to Problem 3's full swap of a 2-by-6 board ("explain why fewer switches cannot work"), to Problem 4's forced columns (columns 1 and 3 never change), to a maximum-distance pair, and finally the general two-row rule. Each step can be checked with counters. In Problem 3, "each switch moves only one counter of row A from the left half to the right half" is an argument a ten-year-old can find and say.

**Page: strong.** The tasks are substantial and state what to produce. The boards are large enough, and with 24 counters this table can keep Problem 1's whole catalog built (6 × 4 = 24). In Problem 5 the count circles are blank, so the children choose the counts, which is a good open move. Minor points: Problem 1 prints exactly six boards for six pictures, which gives the count away, although "Explain why your list is complete" survives that. Problem 6 is a dense paragraph, but it is last and meant for the quickest child.

**Child at the table (a fourth grader):**
- **Minute 5.** Problem 1: they choose two columns for row A and fill in row B. They are organizing the list.
- **Minute 15.** Problem 2 or 3: they are physically making switches, counting moves, and starting to argue "can't be fewer than 3".
- **Minute 30.** Problem 4 or 5: they notice the columns that never move, and they try to build a pair at distance 4 on a 2-by-6 board and fail.

**Patterns:**
1. *Record-keeping, mild risk, in Problem 2 (p. 3) and Problem 4 (pp. 6–7).* "What is the largest number of switches ever needed between two of them?" tempts a careful child to tally all 15 pairs of the 6 pictures. The organizer can steer them toward reasoning instead.
2. *Precision:* not found. The guide reports cells of about 18.5 mm on pages 1–3. 16 mm counters leave little margin, so check the counters actually in hand.
3. *Procedure:* not found.
4. *Told, not felt:* not found through Problem 5. Problem 6 is abstract, but by then children have handled it in Problems 3–5.
5. *Adult runs it:* not found. This group reads short paragraphs.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 3's argument that three switches are needed, because each switch can fix only one counter. The discovery in Problem 4 that columns 1 and 3 are locked is a close second.

## Only a rehearsal or pilot can settle

- Whether the counters bought fit the 22 mm cells, and the 18.5 mm cells on grades 4–5 pages 1–3, without sliding or overlapping the lines.
- Whether kindergartners can check row and column counts reliably with only a partner. How long Problem 4's six-picture catalog takes K–1, and whether they keep track of duplicates.
- Whether third graders see switches whose corners are not adjacent.
- Whether 24 counters are enough when both K–1 pairs build catalogs at once.
- Whether K–1 children can write in the small count circles in Problems 2 and 6.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **Grades 2–3, Problem 5 (pp. 6–7). The activity is strong; this is a page fix.** Keep one filled copy of the page-7 picture and make the other three boards blank, with the counts printed. Change the task from "Find every switch in each picture and draw its rectangle" to making each switch with counters and drawing the picture it produces on a blank board. Keep "Which pictures have none?" This replaces a rectangle hunt on identical printed copies with the counter-moving that worked in Problem 3.
2. **K–1, page 1. Page fix.** Delete the dashed and dotted legend ("Across each row" / "Down each column") and the guide-lines drawn over the example. Keep the before-and-after pair of boards. The parent volunteer shows the meaning in the launch by running a finger along a row or down a column to its circle. That habit belongs in the facilitator notes, as the way a partner checks a picture, so the count check is not left to the adult alone.
3. **K–1, Problem 6 (p. 8). Page fix.** Remove the double negative so it makes sense after one hearing. For example: "Make a four-counter picture on each left grid and write its counts. Make one that your partner cannot change into a different picture with the same counts." Keep it as the last K–1 problem. It is the K–1 version of uniqueness and the most likely delight.
4. **K–1 count checking. Adult practice, not a page change.** For K–1 the rule is "your partner checks every row and column before you draw it". The guide should say so, so the partner carries the check and the parent volunteer does not have to. A page change cannot make the counts enforce themselves. If the organizer wants that, it is a materials question: *outline: for the organizer.*
5. **K–1, Problems 3–4. Materials.** 24 counters per table cannot keep a six-picture catalog built for each of two pairs, so kindergartners must copy every picture into dots. Giving the K–1 table about 36–40 counters would let them leave a whole catalog on the boards and draw it afterwards. *Outline: for the organizer* (counter quantity).
6. **Grades 4–5, Problem 1 (pp. 1–2). Minor page fix.** Add one board marked as extra workspace (not a seventh numbered board), so the number of boards no longer announces the answer 6.
7. **Grades 4–5, Problems 2 and 4. No page change.** In the facilitator notes, steer children away from tallying all 15 pairs and toward the reason the largest distance is 2.
