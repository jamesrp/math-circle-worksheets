# Week 25 kid-fit review: Row and column shadows

| Band | Activity | Page | K–1 bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| K–1 (W25-K-v5, 8 pp.) | workable | workable | not met |
| Grades 2–3 (W25-23-v4, 6 pp.) | strong | workable | |
| Grades 4–5 (W25-45-v4, 8 pp.) | workable | workable | |

Basis: I rendered all 22 pages and enumerated every count set in the packets by code. That shows what a child will run into: where a "find two" turns out impossible, how many pictures a catalog has, and how often a partner can be blocked. These verdicts filter the packet. They are not pilot results.

The core action is the same in every band, and it is a good one. A child puts real counters on a labelled grid until every circled row and column number matches. They then find that a different arrangement also matches. Placing the counters is easy and the counters themselves show the picture. The rule is only partly physical. "At most one per cell" is visible on the grid. Matching the counts is not: it lives in printed numbers that the child has to check by counting. This is milder than the failed lamp puzzle, because the counts are a check the child can run, not a hidden side effect. Still, nothing stops a child who matches the rows and forgets the columns.

---

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** First graders can place counters against small counts. They can also see the main surprise at its smallest scale. In Problem 1 ("Make two different pictures for each pair of grids. Match all the circled counts."), the 2-by-2 grid with every count 1 has exactly two answers, the two diagonals, and a pair can hold them side by side. Kindergartners can place counters but will often match rows and miss columns. The "find every" and "no other picture" questions need an adult to judge completeness and impossibility, which a five-year-old can't do.

**Page: workable.** P1, P2 and P4 use few words, big 22 mm cells and printed counts, and a parent volunteer can read each one in one breath. The trouble is order and the last page. The one partner game (P5) doesn't start until page 7. Before it come five pages and about 27 grids of the same solo move. P6 ("On the left grids, no other picture may fit your counts. On the right grids, another picture must fit them.") is three sentences built on a condition that only an adult can check.

**What keeps K–1 below the bar.** Nothing physical enforces the count rule. The child has to count four or five lines in two directions and compare each with a printed numeral. That is two simultaneous conditions for a five-year-old. "Different" is also judged only by comparing pictures. In the catalogs (P2, P3) that means comparing a new picture against every earlier drawing, and kindergartners won't do that unaided. The partner who could act as the enforcer only appears at P5.

### Patterns found
1. **Hands-on start turns into record-keeping: present, mild (K–1 pp. 3–5, P2 and P3).** "Find and draw every different picture" forces a place, check, draw dots, clear the counters, place again cycle. P3 has six 3-counter pictures, so a full catalog in counters needs 18, and the table has 24 for two pairs. Children will be drawing dots instead of comparing counter pictures. The drawing serves a real goal (fill all the grids with different pictures), which keeps this mild. One help: the number of grids equals the number of answers (three each in P2, six in P3), so "every" means "fill them all with different ones".
2. **Precision children don't have: minor (K–1 pp. 7–8, P5 and P6).** "Write its counts" means writing digits in circles about 7 mm across. That's a fine-motor job for kindergartners, though messy digits don't hide any mathematics.
3. **A procedure instead of choices: not found.** Every K–1 task is a placement choice.
4. **Told, not felt: present (K–1 p. 5, P4; p. 8, P6).** P4 asks "Can you make two different pictures?" On the 3 / 1 grid with column counts 2, 1, 1, the full row of 3 forces everything, and a child can feel that. On the 3-by-3 grid with counts 2, 1, 0 there is also only one picture, but a K–1 child who fails will just conclude they haven't found it yet, and an adult has to say "there is only one". P6's "no other picture may fit" can't be checked by the child at all.
5. **The adult runs it: present (K–1 p. 8, P6; also p. 4, P3).** In P6 the volunteer would have to explain uniqueness and then judge each child's left-grid picture. In P3, six different rook pictures on a 3-by-3 grid takes an organized search. With a non-mathematician volunteer, the adult will probably end up spotting the duplicates and the gaps.

**Minute by minute (a first grader paired with a kindergartner).** At minute 5 both are on P1, putting counters on the 2-by-2 grid. They count the rows, the volunteer points at the bottom numbers, and they fix one column. At minute 15 they're on P2 drawing dots. The first grader is making the second picture of three; the kindergartner has made a picture that matches the rows only and is waiting for the volunteer. At minute 30, with the order as printed, they're in P3 or P4 and the kindergartner is drifting. The packet's best moment, P5's hide-and-copy game, comes after most K–1 attention has run out.

**Most likely delight:** P1's first pair. "Mine is different from yours and the numbers are the same." Second is P5: a partner who can't make a different picture from your counts. That happens often. Of the 15 four-counter pictures on the 2-by-3 grid, 9 have only one picture for their counts, so the maker "wins" more often than not.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** It works as a sequence of puzzles that turns into a game. P1 ("make two different pictures that match the counts, if you can") includes an impossible pair (3 / 1 with 2, 1, 1), and a third grader will feel that from the full row. P2's catalog has five pictures on six grids, so the spare grid keeps "How can you be sure your list is complete?" honest. P5 asks for a picture that takes exactly two switches "and cannot be reached in one", and the answer is the complement, where all four columns are wrong. Third graders can explain that in their own words with counters in hand. P6 ("Can you choose your picture so that this is impossible?") is a real two-player contest.

**Page: workable.** The text is short and the grids are big. Three problems are placed or sized badly:
- P3 ("Move exactly two counters from the filled picture to make a different picture with the same counts") has one answer and takes about a minute.
- P4 packs the switch definition, the "any two rows and columns" clause, a find-all search and a comparison into one paragraph. Its bottom-left picture (counts 2, 1, 1) has three switches, so the child has to draw three overlapping rectangles on one printed picture.
- The game in P6 comes last, after the densest page.

### Patterns found
1. **Record-keeping: mild (2–3 p. 4, P4).** "Find every switch in each picture and draw its rectangle" is a systematic check of the rectangles in a 3-by-3 grid, nine to a picture, recorded as drawn rectangles. It serves the question "Which pictures have none?". The payoff only lands if the child notices that the no-switch pictures are the ones whose counts allow a single picture. The bottom-left picture carries P2's counts, which a child might spot.
2. **Precision: not found.** The counters fit 22 mm cells, and drawn dots and rectangles only need to be roughly right.
3. **Procedure: not found.** Switches are chosen, not prescribed.
4. **Told, not felt: possible (p. 4, P4).** The link "no switch means only one picture" is not something the page asks the child to test. Unless the adult connects P4's no-switch pictures back to P1 and P2, it will be announced, not discovered.
5. **The adult runs it: light (p. 4, P4).** A third grader will need the definition read, and probably the non-adjacent case pointed out, although P3 has already done exactly that move (A1 and C3 to A3 and C1). With a mathematician at the table this is ordinary help.

**Minute by minute.** At minute 5 a third grader has finished P1's six grids and is arguing that the 3 / 1 pair can't have two pictures. At minute 15 they're in P2 and hunting for the fifth picture. At minute 30 they're in P4 drawing rectangles, or in P5 trying to get from "A12 / B34" to "A34 / B12" in one switch. They're likely to ask to keep playing P6.

**Most likely delight:** in P6, finding a picture the partner can't copy (a full row or a "staircase"), and then the partner turning that trick back on you. Second is P5: "you can't do it in one. Every column is wrong and a switch only fixes two."

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: workable.** The mathematics is rich and well sequenced. P1 counts the six pictures on the 2-by-4 grid ("Explain why your list is complete") with choose-2-of-4 reasoning. P3 ("Change the upper picture into the lower picture in as few switches as you can... explain why fewer switches cannot work") has a clean argument a ten-year-old can make with counters: six columns are wrong and each switch fixes two. P4 adds forced columns (counts 2 and 0). With the organizer at the table, fourth and fifth graders can get there. It falls short of strong because after P1 every task uses the same action, switching counters on a two-row grid and recording a route. There is no contest or challenge between the children, even though the table is set up as a pair plus a referee. The payoff of each problem is an explanation, not something new the counters show.

**Page: workable.** It's short and readable for this age, with large 2-by-6 boards. Two problems need care:
- P2 ("What is the largest number of switches ever needed between two of them?") asks about all 15 pairs of P1's pictures but provides only three grids.
- P5 jumps to "any number of columns" right after P4, with no concrete step in between.

### Patterns found
1. **Record-keeping: mild (4–5 p. 3, P2; pp. 4–7, P3 and P4).** "Record a shortest route" means drawing each intermediate six-counter picture. In P2, the largest distance over all pairs can become a table of 15 distances unless a child spots the complements. This serves a question the child cares about, so it is mild, but it's the main writing load in the packet.
2. **Precision: not found.** The cells on pp. 1–3 are 18.5 mm, so 16 mm counters fit but 19 mm (3/4 inch) counters won't. This is a materials issue, not a hidden pattern.
3. **Procedure: mild (p. 4, P3).** Undoing 123 / 456 takes three switches done in sequence. It's short, and the choices are real.
4. **Told, not felt: not found for P1–P4.** Each claim can be checked with counters. P5's general rule is a proof task, and it's in the right place, late in the packet.
5. **The adult runs it: not found.** This is a three-child table with the organizer.

**Minute by minute.** At minute 5 the children are filling P1's grids and someone says "it's just which two go on top". At minute 15 they're in P2 or P3, sliding counters and arguing over whether three switches is really the minimum. At minute 30 the quick child is on P4's forced columns. The others are still drawing P3's route on the blank grids.

**Most likely delight:** P3's "you can't do it in fewer than three. Each switch only fixes two columns." It's an argument the child owns. P4's realization that columns with count 2 or 0 "don't play" is a quieter second.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1 p. 7, P5: move it to directly after P1, before the catalogs.** The hide-and-copy game is the K–1 packet's best enforcer: the partner checks "different" and "same counts". It also produces the uniqueness surprise by play. At page 7 it arrives after about 27 solo grids. This is a reorder only, with no change to the mathematics.
2. **K–1 p. 8, P6: replace the three-sentence left/right condition with one game sentence.** For example: "Make a four-counter picture and write its counts. Your partner tries to make a different picture with the same counts. Can you make one they cannot?" This keeps the unique-or-not idea, lets the partner be the judge instead of the volunteer, and uses the existing 2-by-3 and 3-by-3 grids.
3. **2–3 p. 6, P6: move the partner game before the switch page (current P4).** It needs only counting, and it is the problem third graders are most likely to want more of. As placed, children who slow down on P4's dense definition never reach it. Renumber after moving.
4. **2–3 p. 3, P3: give it more than one minute of work.** Add a second filled picture with more than one possible two-counter move, for example A1, A2, B3, C1 with counts 2, 1, 1 / 2, 1, 1, which has three switches. The children then search instead of finding the single answer. This also leads directly into P4.
5. **2–3 p. 4, P4: give the three-switch picture (bottom left) two or three extra copies.** Children can then draw one rectangle per copy instead of stacking three on one printed picture.
6. **K–1 pp. 3–5, P2 and P3: let the catalogs be built in counters, not transcribed.** The smallest change is materials, not page: about 36 counters per table instead of 24. A pair can then keep P2's three pictures (9 counters) and most of P3's six (18) on the grids at once, and spot duplicates by looking. Mark as materials for the organizer. Drawing stays available.
7. **K–1 pp. 7–8, P5 and P6 count circles: enlarge them, or allow count cards.** Count cards 0–6 are already on the materials list. Writing digits in circles about 7 mm across is the K–1 packet's only fine-motor demand. Whether the cards fit beside a 22 mm grid needs a rehearsal.
8. **4–5 p. 8, P5: put a concrete step on its two blank grids before the general rule.** For example: "On these grids, make two pictures with the same counts that need as many switches as possible." This stays within the outline's distance rule. It gives the pair-plus-referee table a challenge to set each other and makes the general explanation the follow-up, not the first move.
9. **4–5 p. 3, P2: no page change needed.** The organizer can steer from the all-pairs table to the complement insight. Flagging it only so that a pilot checks whether the children start tabulating 15 distances.

Nothing here calls for "outline: for the organizer". The activity holds up in every band, and the problems are on the page side.

## What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle
- Whether 16 mm (5/8 inch) counters can be bought, and whether the volunteer ends up with 3/4 inch counters, which overlap the 18.5 mm cells on 4–5 pp. 1–3.
- Whether kindergartners check columns as well as rows after the launch, or need the partner or volunteer every time. This is the main open question for the K–1 bar.
- How long K–1 P3's six-picture catalog takes, and whether the K–1 table stays with the matching move for 35 minutes.
- Whether third graders read P4's switch definition correctly, especially the non-adjacent case.
- Whether 4–5 children find the repeated two-row switch routes absorbing or repetitive by P4.
- Whether the P5 and P6 partner games are lopsided in practice, since most four-counter pictures on the 2-by-3 grid allow only one picture.
