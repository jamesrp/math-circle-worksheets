# Week 24, Three random decks: kid-fit review

| Band | Activity | Page | K–1 bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| K–1 (F24-K-v3, 8 pages) | workable | weak | not met |
| Grades 2–3 (F24-23-v2, 7 pages) | strong | workable | |
| Grades 4–5 (F24-45-v3, 7 pages) | strong | workable | |

This review comes from reading the PROMPT, rendering all 22 pages and checking a few answers by enumeration (listed at the end). No children have used this packet, so every verdict is a hypothesis.

One problem runs through all three bands. The hook is a game: draw from two bags, and the bigger card wins. The activity is at its best when a child chooses a deck, loses to the deck their partner chose, and then finds that there is no best deck. Across 22 problems, only one (K–1 Problem 4) has a child draw a card. Everywhere else, children count all the pairs on paper. The rules paragraph at the top of each packet describes drawing and returning cards, but no later problem uses that procedure. Counting pairs is good mathematics. Still, the randomness the week is named for, and the "I'll pick second and beat you" game that makes nontransitive dice a classic with children, are mostly missing from the pages.

---

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** Comparing two dot-and-numeral cards is a move a five-year-old can make, and drawing from bags is fun. However, the main surprise depends on results from three separate pages. A is 5–4 over B on page 1, B is 5–4 over C on page 2, and C is 5–4 over A on page 3. A child who reads almost nothing cannot hold all three results at once, so the parent volunteer would have to announce the cycle. The 5-to-4 margin also cannot be felt in a six-round game (Problem 4).

**Page: weak.** The first three pages are 27 printed pairs to circle ("Circle the winner in each pair. Which deck wins more pairs?" three times). The first draw from a bag comes on page 4, and that page gives no place to record the rounds. Problems 6–8 ask for every swap and for "show why it cannot be done," which leaves the adult doing the thinking and the recording.

**K–1 bar: not met.** A child can make the core move printed on page 1 (circle the bigger card in a pictured pair) within a minute. But no material or partner checks that move. The child must count circles by deck label to answer the question, and the question that matters ("Does any of A, B, and C win more pairs than both other decks?", Problem 3) depends on remembering two earlier pages. The concrete action the materials can enforce, drawing from the bags, comes fourth.

**Patterns found**

1. *Hands-on start turns into record-keeping:* pages 1–3, Problems 1–3. The child circles 9 cards three times. At minute 15 a five-year-old is still circling on page 2. The circling only confirms a count of 5 against 4, and the child has no stake in it yet. Problem 5 (page 5) works the same way: four candidate cards, and A against B is recounted from scratch for each one, about 36 comparisons. Problem 6 (page 6) is worse: nine possible swaps, each needing all 9 pairs recounted (81 comparisons), and the page has no space for recording swaps, so the adult writes them down.
2. *Precision children don't have:* mild, on pages 1–8. The dots for 7, 8 and 9 are small, 3-wide arrays (8 is 3+3+2 and 7 is 3+3+1). A kindergartner comparing the 8 card with the 9 card in Problem 1 must count small dots exactly. First graders will read the numerals. Some kindergartners will not.
3. *A procedure instead of choices:* Problems 1–3 are a fixed procedure (circle the larger card in each of 9 pairs, then tally). Problems 5–8 do give choices.
4. *Told, not felt:* Problem 3's cycle and Problem 4's advantage. The deck the child chooses wins only 5 of 9 pairs. In six rounds it wins more rounds about 45% of the time and loses more about 25% of the time. So "Must your chosen deck win more rounds?" will be answered by experience ("No, I lost"), but the advantage that justified choosing that deck will not be visible. Problem 8's impossibility for two-card decks can only be announced to this age.
5. *The adult runs it:* the rules paragraph ("Each card has the same chance. Mix the bags separately…") is read aloud before anything happens. Problems 6–8 ("Find every swap…", "show why it cannot be done") ask for complete searches and impossibility arguments. The non-mathematician volunteer would end up running them, and Problem 8's proof is out of reach for both the volunteer and the children.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 4. The child has picked "the deck that wins," draws against a partner from real bags, and loses. Also a smaller one in Problem 7, if a child notices that nine pairs cannot be split evenly ("there's one left over").

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Under a mathematician, four third graders building rock-paper-scissors decks is an absorbing puzzle. Problem 3 ("Keep 9 in A, 8 in B, and 7 in C. Place cards 1 to 6 so … Find two arrangements different from Problem 1") has exactly two other answers, so a search with movable cards has a clear finish line. Problem 4 has a counterintuitive answer: of 0, 2, 4, 9 and 10, only 2 and 4 complete the cycle, because a bigger card makes A too strong against C.

**Page: workable.** Problems 3 and 4 are good pages. However, Problem 1 has no play, the rules paragraph uses adult wording ("Each physical card is equally likely… The two decks in a comparison must not share a number. Card order in a deck does not matter."), Problem 2 is a thought experiment about a game that nobody plays, and Problem 5 is mostly bookkeeping.

**Patterns found**

1. *Record-keeping:* Problem 5 (page 5). The six-card decks are every card doubled. Comparing each pair of decks means 36 card pairs per comparison, 108 in all, to confirm that no winner changes. A third grader at minute 25 is tallying, not deciding. The idea that doubling changes nothing can be felt with one comparison. The other two add only bookkeeping.
2. *Precision:* none.
3. *A procedure instead of choices:* only in Problem 5. Problems 3, 4, 6 and 7 are open searches with choices.
4. *Told, not felt:* Problem 2 (page 2): "You hear only which bag wins. The left bag wins six draws in a row. Can you be sure…? Could any fixed number of these results make you sure?" Nothing is drawn. Whether a run of wins proves anything is a question children would feel after being fooled by a short game, but here it reaches them as an abstract question.
5. *The adult runs it:* the rules paragraph and Problem 2. The mathematician can carry both, but they are where the four children would listen instead of handle cards. Problem 7's "explain why there is none" (two-card decks) will be mostly adult-led at this age, which is acceptable that late in the packet.

**Most likely delight:** the end of Problem 1, when the table finds A beats B, B beats C and C beats A ("so which one is best?"). Second, in Problem 4, the bigger cards 9 and 10 ruin the cycle.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** With the organizer at a table of three, the sequence builds well. In Problem 1, all three deck totals are 15, so "Do the three deck totals settle this question?" has a clean, surprising answer. Problem 2 asks for a complete search with a completeness argument (there are exactly three solutions). Problem 3 asks children to design decks with repeated values. Problem 4 has a real twist: the four-card decks flip B against C to 7 of 16. Problems 5–7 move to small-case rules and impossibility proofs.

**Page: workable.** The tasks are substantial and briefly stated, but none involves playing. That leaves the pair-plus-referee rotation from the session context with no game to referee. Problem 4 asks for a lot of confirmatory counting before reaching its interesting change.

**Patterns found**

1. *Record-keeping:* Problem 4 (page 4): "Find each deck's chance … first with the six-card decks and then with the four-card decks." That is 108 card pairs for the six-card decks, where nothing changes, before 48 for the four-card decks, where something does. A ten-year-old will tire of the first half before reaching the surprise. Problem 2's "Find every arrangement" over 90 placements is also heavy, but there the bookkeeping serves the goal (proving the list is complete), so it is fine.
2. *Precision:* none.
3. *A procedure instead of choices:* only Problem 4's six-card half.
4. *Told, not felt:* the word "chance" and the meaning of "wins more than half its pairs" are never connected to actual draws. The organizer can bridge this in conversation. Problem 7's impossibility (the best achievable is 5 of 9 for all three) will be proved by argument, which suits this band.
5. *The adult runs it:* the rules paragraph (same as 2–3). Problems 6 and 7 are proof questions that the organizer will drive for most children. That is appropriate late in a 4–5 packet, but only the quickest child will reach them independently.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 1's "all three totals are 15," and Problem 4's four-card decks, where removing duplicates breaks the cycle.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1, page 4, Problem 4: put the game first and give it a record.** Move Problem 4 to become Problem 1 or 2. Rewrite it so that one child picks any deck and the partner draws against it with another deck for six rounds. Add a printed track of six counter spaces per matchup (the 18 two-style counters are already in the materials), so that who won more rounds can be seen at a glance. This keeps the activity and gives K–1 its core move within a minute, enforced by the bags.
2. **K–1, page 3, Problem 3: make the cycle visible at once.** Add a small A–B–C triangle with a space on each side where the child places a winner counter or deck letter, filled from Problems 1–3. Then the child can see the cycle on one picture instead of remembering it across pages. Smallest alternative: let Problems 1–2 be a single page each, and have the adult copy each winner onto Problem 3's triangle.
3. **Grades 2–3 and 4–5, Problem 1: add play.** Open each packet with a choose-second game: "Your partner picks a deck. Then you pick one. Play ten rounds. Switch who picks first." Then ask the current Problem 1 question. This uses the existing bags and gives the 4–5 referee a role. It is one new problem, and the analysis problems stay as written.
4. **Grades 2–3, page 2, Problem 2: make it a game.** "Your partner secretly puts two of the decks in the left and right bags and tells you only which bag wins each draw. After six draws, say which deck is in the left bag. Can you ever be sure?" The question stays the same, but children now ask it after being fooled.
5. **K–1, page 6, Problem 6: replace the open search with a printed 3×3 grid.** Put A's cards across the top and B's cards down the side, and have the child mark each swap that makes B win. There are 5 such swaps. The grid removes the transcription and shows the whole search at once. If that is still too much counting (81 comparisons), mark Problem 6 as for quick first graders only in the guide.
6. **Grades 2–3, page 5, Problem 5 and grades 4–5, page 4, Problem 4: cut the confirmatory counting.** In 2–3, ask for one six-card comparison (A against B), then add the four-card decks from 4–5 Problem 4, where B against C changes. In 4–5, ask for the four-card chances in full and only one six-card comparison. The doubling insight survives, and the surprise arrives sooner.
7. **Grades 2–3 and 4–5, rules paragraph: plain words.** Use K–1's wording ("Each card has the same chance. Mix the bags separately…"). Move "The two decks in a comparison must not share a number" into the problems where children choose numbers (2–3 Problems 6–7, 4–5 Problems 3, 5–7; 4–5 Problem 3 already states it). Drop "Card order in a deck does not matter."
8. **K–1, page 8, Problem 8 (two-card cycle impossible): outline: for the organizer.** A K–1 child can search a few splits with cards, but the "why" is a proof that neither the children nor the volunteer can carry. No page change rescues it. Problem 7 (equal split, impossible because 9 is odd) can stay: nine circles that will not split evenly is something a first grader can feel.
9. **K–1, all card drawings: larger dots in dice or ten-frame layouts for 6–9.** Then 8 against 9 can be told apart at a glance without exact counting.

## What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle

- How long K–1 children take to circle 27 pairs, and whether they are still willing by page 3.
- Whether a six-round K–1 game at a 5-to-9 disadvantage reads as fun or as unfair. The chosen deck loses more rounds than it wins about a quarter of the time and ties about 30% of the time.
- Whether third graders keep going through Problem 3's 90-placement search with physical cards, and how many find both answers.
- How long 4–5 Problem 2's complete search takes. Whether 4–5 Problems 6–7 are reached at all in one hour.
- Whether hand-made index cards in paper bags are hard to tell apart by touch (they need to be), and whether the drawing and returning goes smoothly at a table of four.

Enumeration checks (mine, for locating the claims above): 2–3 Problem 3 and 4–5 Problem 2 have three solutions in all, Problem 1's among them. 2–3 Problem 4 works only for 2 and 4. 2–3 Problem 6 has one solution. K–1 Problem 6 has 5 winning swaps. No two-card cycle from 1–6 exists. In 4–5 Problem 7, the best achievable minimum is 5 of 9. The four-card decks give A over B 10–6, C over B 9–7 and C over A 10–6.
