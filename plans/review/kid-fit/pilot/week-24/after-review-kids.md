# Kid-fit review: Week 24, Three random decks (draft)

| Band | Activity | Page | K–1 bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| K–1 (F24-K-v4, 9 pages) | workable | workable | not met |
| Grades 2–3 (F24-23-v3, 8 pages) | strong | workable | |

I rendered every page of all three draft PDFs. I checked the answer counts with a short script, because whether a problem is fun depends on how many answers it has and how much counting each try needs. This is a filter, not a pilot. Nobody has watched children use these pages.

A fact that shapes all three bands: each cycle edge is a 5-to-4 advantage (5/9). Over ten rounds, the deck with the advantage wins outright only about 52% of the time. It ties about 23% of the time and loses about 25%. Over six rounds it wins outright only about 45% of the time. So the games are fun, but they cannot show the cycle. Children will only see it by counting all the pairs.

---

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** The core move is to pull one card from each bag, see which has more dots and put a counter in that deck's row (Problem 1: "Each player takes one deck. Play six rounds of each game, and put a counter in the winning deck's row each round."). A kindergartner can do this within a minute, and the dot cards and the partner enforce the rule. The mathematics then comes from circling 27 pictured pairs. Each comparison is split 5 to 4, so one mistaken circle erases the cycle. Later problems ask for counting or explanations that the adult has to run.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1–6 are well pictured and need almost no reading. The pair pages (Problems 2–4) give the child everything to act on: "Circle the winner in each pair. Which deck wins more pairs?" Problems 7–9 are where the page stops working for this table. Problem 7 asks for nine swaps, each needing nine comparisons that are not pictured. Problems 8 and 9 end with "show why it cannot be done".

**K–1 bar: not met.** The core move passes the bar. Two things keep it from strong:
- The play cannot show the idea, and the counting that can show it is fragile. Three near-identical circling pages come before the reveal, and each result is a single circle's margin.
- The last third of the packet (Problems 7–9) asks for unpictured recounting and impossibility explanations. The parent volunteer would end up running these while the children watch.

**One child, a kindergartner:**
- Minute 5: drawing from bags, matching dots and pressing counters onto the circles in Problem 1. The child is absorbed, and laughs when a 1 is drawn.
- Minute 15: on Problem 2 or 3, circling the bigger card in each printed pair. This is a fine task, but it is the same page a second time. Counting nine circles by deck needs the adult.
- Minute 30: if the table gets to Problem 4, the adult fills in the triangle with the children. The possible gasp ("A beats B, B beats C, C beats A, nobody wins!") depends on all 27 circles being right. Problem 5 brings back the game, which the child would happily play again.

**Patterns found**
1. *Hands-on start turns into record-keeping.* This happens on pages 2–4 (Problems 2–4). It is three consecutive pages of nine circlings each, then a count of circles by deck. The circling has a goal, but by Problem 4 it is repetitive, and the payoff only comes after all 27. Problem 6 (page 6) adds four tries of nine unpictured comparisons (36 in all). Problem 7 (page 7) adds nine swaps of nine comparisons each (81 in all). Here the child keeps track of which swap is being checked while the adult tallies.
2. *Precision children don't have.* This is about counting accuracy rather than measuring. Every comparison in Problems 2–4 is decided 5 to 4. One slip, such as circling 5 over 6 in Problem 3, gives a tie or flips an arrow, and the triangle in Problem 4 then shows no cycle.
3. *Procedure instead of choices.* Problems 2–4 are the same compare-and-circle procedure, 27 times, and the interesting thing happens only after all three pages. Problem 7 is also mostly procedure: swap, recount, mark, nine times.
4. *Told, not felt.* Problem 1 asks "Is one deck better than both of the others?" from six-round games. At these odds the six-round results are close to coin flips. Children will often see a "best" deck that isn't one, and the adult has to say why. Problem 5 does turn this into a good question ("Must your chosen deck win more rounds?"). There, seeing the stronger deck lose is the point.
5. *Adult runs it.* This happens in Problems 7–9 (pages 7–9). Problem 9 asks why three two-card decks cannot form a cycle. A five- or six-year-old cannot explain that, so the adult would be explaining it. Problem 8 is better: nine pairs cannot be shared equally, and first graders can feel that with nine counters. But each try still needs nine unpictured comparisons.

**Most likely delight:** the end of Problem 5. The child picked the deck that "always wins more pairs" and still lost the game. Second choice is the Problem 4 triangle, if the circles are right.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Third graders can count nine pairs systematically, and a mathematician is at the table. The cycle in Problem 2 is a real surprise: "Is there a deck that wins more pairs than both of the others?" Problem 3's hidden-bag guessing game is a good way to explore what a short game can and cannot tell you: "Could any fixed number of rounds make you sure?" With six winner-only rounds a guess is right only about 60% of the time, so children feel the uncertainty for themselves.

**Page: workable.**
- Problems 2, 3, 4 and 5 are clear, open and substantial. Problem 4 ("Find two arrangements different from Problem 2") has exactly two other solutions among 90 placements, which makes it a real search.
- Problem 1's recording table ("First pick / Second pick", six blank rows) does not say what to write. Its question cannot be answered yet: the second picker does not know which deck beats which.
- Problem 6 is a long counting chore of 36 + 48 = 84 comparisons.
- Problem 7 has exactly one solution among a very large space, so it is likely to frustrate.

**One child, a third grader:**
- Minute 5: playing the Problem 1 game and arguing about which deck to take.
- Minute 15: on Problem 2, making their own list or grid of pairs. The child finds A beats B and B beats C, expects A to crush C, and finds the opposite.
- Minute 30: either playing Problem 3's guessing game, which is a good game to replay, or deep in Problem 4, trying placements with physical cards and recounting 27 pairs per try.

**Patterns found**
1. *Record-keeping.*
   - Problem 6 (page 6): 36 comparisons for the six-card decks, then three comparisons of 16 pairs. The six-card half changes no winner, so a lot of it confirms what children already know. The four-card half has a real reversal: C now beats B, 9 to 7. That reversal is buried at the end.
   - Problem 1 (page 1): the table invites tallying with no clear purpose.
2. *Precision.* The 5-to-4 margins in Problems 2, 4 and 5 make one miscount decisive. Third graders recount more reliably than K–1, so the risk is smaller.
3. *Procedure instead of choices.* This is mild. Problem 4's search is choices, but every rejected try costs 27 comparisons.
4. *Told, not felt.* This is in Problem 1. "Is it better to pick first or second?" has an answer (second, once you know the cycle), but the ten-round games cannot show it. In Problem 1 the second picker does not yet know what to pick. Even a correct pick wins a ten-round game outright only about half the time. The answer comes from Problem 2, so the adult has to link the two.
5. *Adult runs it.* Problem 7 (page 7) asks for nine different cards from 1–12 with "totals 15 in A, 18 in B, and 21 in C" and a cycle. Exactly one arrangement works (A = 3,5,7; B = 2,4,12; C = 1,9,11). Children will try many sets, so the adult ends up steering the search. Problem 8's "explain why there is none" is fine as the last problem with a mathematician present.

**Most likely delight:** the moment in Problem 2 when the third comparison comes out backwards: C beats A even though A beat B and B beat C. Second is Problem 3's reveal ("then look") when the guess was wrong.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** The table has three children, and the organizer is with them. The referee role in Problem 1 suits that ("while a third player draws the cards and keeps score. Rotate jobs"). The problems move from counting to explaining.
- Problem 2 asks "Do the three deck totals settle this question?" All three totals are 15, so this is a quick and satisfying "no".
- Problem 3 asks for every arrangement (there are three) and why none are missing.
- Problem 4 allows repeated numbers and has about 30 solutions, so children can invent their own.
- Problem 6 asks for a rule for two-card decks.
- Problems 7–8 are real impossibility arguments for the quickest child.

**Page: workable.** Most problems are short, open and substantive. The page loses its strong rating in three places:
- Problem 1 has the same unclear table and the same question that play cannot answer.
- Problem 5 is a long exact-chance count (36 + 48 pairs).
- Problem 7 puts three questions into one paragraph: one-card decks, two-card decks, and then "What is the fewest cards per deck that allows this, with all decks the same size?" This is acceptable with the organizer there, but it is the densest reading in the packet.

**One child, a fourth grader:**
- Minute 5: referee or player in Problem 1, keeping score.
- Minute 15: counting the nine-pair comparisons in Problem 2 and enjoying that the totals are all 15.
- Minute 30: on Problem 3 or 4, moving cards around to make a cycle. Problem 4 lets them write their own decks, which children of this age usually love. The fifth grader may reach Problem 6's rule.

**Patterns found**
1. *Record-keeping.* Problem 5 (page 5): 36 pairs for A against B in the six-card decks, then three four-card comparisons of 16 pairs. For 4–5 the exact fractions are the point (20/36 equals 5/9 shows that duplicating every card changes nothing). The labour is still large for one child.
2. *Precision.* Not significant. These children check fractions, and the adult is a mathematician.
3. *Procedure instead of choices.* Not significant. The tasks are searches and arguments.
4. *Told, not felt.* Problem 1 (page 1), as in grades 2–3: ten-round games will not reliably favour the second picker.
5. *Adult runs it.* Problem 8 (page 8): "at least six of its nine pairs" for every edge, which is impossible. A child can hunt for an example, but proving it impossible will be a conversation led by the organizer. That is acceptable as the last problem.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 2's "the totals are all 15, and still each deck loses to another one". After that, Problem 4, where a child invents a cycle like (3,3,3), (2,2,6), (1,4,4) and watches it work.

## What only rehearsal or a pilot can settle

- Whether a counter fits inside the printed circles on K–1 pages 1 and 5, and whether five-year-olds can place them without knocking the rows.
- How long 18 K–1 draws (Problem 1) take. My guess is 6–10 minutes.
- Whether K–1 children can tell the small printed dot frames apart on the pair pages.
- Whether children peek into the bags or remix them properly.
- Whether the 2–3 and 4–5 tables invent a usable pair record in Problem 2 without a demonstration.
- Whether the Problem 4 (2–3) and Problem 3 (4–5) searches hold attention or wear children out with recounting.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1, Problems 2–4 (pages 2–4): make the 27 comparisons robust and less repetitive.**
   - Have children put a counter of the winner's style on each pair instead of circling. Each deck's pile then shows its count, can be lined up to compare 5 against 4, and can be checked at a glance.
   - Facilitator route: the two K–1 pairs do Problems 2 and 3 at the same time, then come together for Problem 4. That way no child does three identical pages.
   - The activity stays the same.
2. **2–3 and 4–5, Problem 1 (page 1): give the table a clear job and do not let the question rest on noise.**
   - Replace the six blank two-column rows with one row per game, labelled for the deck picked first, the deck picked second and the rounds each won.
   - Facilitator note (no guide is in this run): replay Problem 1 after Problem 2, when the second picker knows what to choose.
3. **K–1, Problem 7 (page 7): reduce the work from 81 unpictured comparisons.** Keep only one row of the swap grid. For example, swapping B's 6 with each A card gives no, no, yes. That keeps a contrast at 27 comparisons. Otherwise, move Problem 7 after Problem 8.
4. **K–1, Problem 9 (page 9): remove it from K–1.** The two-card impossibility is beyond what this band can explain, and the volunteer is not placed to lead it. Keep Problem 8, whose impossibility (nine pairs cannot be split evenly) first graders can show with nine counters. The page change rescues the packet. The outline's K–1 emphasis (organize all pairs, contrast with a six-round game) does not need Problem 9.
5. **2–3, Problem 6 (page 6): cut the six-card half and keep the four-card decks.** The six-card half is 36 comparisons that change no winner. In the four-card decks, a winner really flips: C beats B, 9 to 7. Grades 4–5 Problem 5 can keep both halves, because there the matching 5/9 is the point. For 4–5, a facilitator note could split the four comparisons among the three children.
6. **2–3, Problem 7 (page 7): replace the exact totals with "A's total smaller than B's, B's smaller than C's".** That has 330 solutions instead of one. It still makes the point that totals do not decide who wins, and children can find it with cards.
7. **4–5, Problem 7 (page 7): split the closing question into its own problem.** Make "What is the fewest cards per deck…" a separate Problem 8, after the one- and two-card cases. Children then meet one question at a time. Renumber the strong-cycle problem accordingly.
8. **K–1, Problem 6 (page 6): minor.** The four tries need 36 unpictured comparisons. If time is short, the facilitator can start with 7 and 9, the instructive contrast: 7 gives A 4 pairs and 9 gives A 5. Then 3 and 5 go quickly. No page change is needed.
