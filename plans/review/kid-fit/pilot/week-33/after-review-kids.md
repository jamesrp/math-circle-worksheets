I rendered and looked at every page of all three PDFs (renders in `renders/`). These verdicts come from reading the pages. Nobody has watched children use them.

Physical facts I checked in `draft/src/figures.py`. The bead-to-bead spacing on the blank boards is about 3.0 cm on the three-bead boards, 2.5 cm on the four-bead boards on the 2–3 and 4–5 pages, and 2.5 cm on the nine-bead board. That should take ordinary 2 cm counters. A kit of six A and six B is exactly enough to build all four three-bead rings at once (AAA, AAB, ABB, BBB). It also covers every ring the pages ask for, including the nine-bead ones.

---

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** The core action is good for this age. Children put counters on a ring and decide whether two rings "are the same when turning makes them match". On Problem 3 ("two A beads and two B beads") and Problem 5 ("two A beads and three B beads"), the answer comes down to "the As are together or apart", which a five-year-old can see and argue. The weakness is in Problems 4 and 6. "Make four-bead rings with one, two, three or four different readouts" and "Find rings with two, three and six different readouts" ask a child to read a letter string from every start, remember it and compare it with the others. That is a reading and memory task for children who mostly do not read.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1, 2, 3 and 5 give a clear thing to build or sort, on large boards. Problem 2's eight cards are drawn as rings, so a child can turn a card to match it against another. That is the best page in the packet for this age. Problems 4 and 6 use the word "readouts", which the K–1 rules never define. The K–1 rule text is only "Read the beads going round from the start arrow. Rings are the same when turning makes them match." So the parent volunteer has to invent the meaning on the spot.

**K–1 bar: not met.** Within a minute, every child can place counters on a three-bead board. Three things keep the band short of strong:
- The materials do not enforce "same by turning". The boards are printed two or four to a page, so a child cannot turn one ring against another without lifting and replacing counters, and the printed lines invite writing letter strings. The ring cards in Problem 2 are the exception.
- Problems 4 and 6 depend on counting distinct readouts. The non-mathematician parent would end up doing that bookkeeping, and deciding when "three is impossible" has been shown.
- No K–1 page gives the parent a check for the core judgement, such as a card or a partner role that settles "same or different".

**Patterns**
- *1. Record-keeping:* page 1, Problem 1. Three writing lines sit beside each of the six boards. A kindergartner who has just made AAB will be drawn into writing A-A-B, A-B-A, B-A-A on lines when the task only asks them to make the rings. That is slow letter-writing that serves no goal the child cares about.
- *2. Precision:* pages 3–6. Turning a ring means rotating the paper under loose counters, or lifting and replacing them. A child who swaps one counter while rebuilding gets a "new" ring that is really a slip. That noise can hide whether two rings match. The sticky-note start arrow avoids this, but it turns the task back into reading.
- *3. Procedure:* page 4, Problem 4, and page 6, Problem 6. To count readouts, a child has to move the arrow step by step and compare four (or six) strings. The answer only appears at the end of that procedure.
- *4. Told, not felt:* page 4, Problem 4. "Exactly three readouts is impossible" is something a child meets only as "I couldn't". An adult has to say that it never can happen. This is fine as an experience, but the why will be announced.
- *5. Adult runs it:* pages 4 and 6. "Readouts" is undefined for this band, and judging "different" needs reading, so the parent reads, compares and announces.

**Most likely delight:** On Problem 3, two children each build a two-A, two-B ring that looks different. They turn one and find it is the same as the other's ("it's the same one"), and then find that the "apart" ring can never be turned into the "together" ring. Second: in Problem 2, the two lonely cards (all A, all B) against the two piles of three.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Third graders with a mathematician at the table get a real chase. They build rings and count how many different ways each one reads. On four beads and six beads they can make a ring that "repeats early" (page 3, Problem 3: "Which numbers are possible?"; page 6, Problem 7: "Find rings with two, three and six different readouts"). Then on five beads they cannot (page 4, Problem 4: "Can a five-bead ring using both A and B have fewer than five different readouts?"). The "32 divided by 5" question in Problem 6 lands squarely on arithmetic they own: 32 does not split into fives, and that is a puzzle they will feel.

**Page: workable.** The order and the cards are where it slips:
- Problem 2's eight cards and Problem 5's 32 cards are drawn as straight strips, not rings. A child cannot turn a strip to match it. They have to shift letters in their head, while the K–1 packet of the same week gets ring cards that turn.
- The 32 strip cards are small (about 4.6 × 2 cm, with 7-pt "start" labels) and need ten cuts.
- Problem 4 asks for the explanation ("Find one or explain why it cannot happen") before Problem 5's sort, which is where the children get the five-bead evidence.
- The four-bead boards on page 3 have three writing lines each, but rings such as AAAB have four readouts.

**Patterns**
- *1. Record-keeping:* page 3, Problem 3. Writing every readout of up to six four-bead rings is up to 24 four-letter words, on lines that are one short. The question is about which numbers are possible, so a count would serve it. Page 1, Problem 1 is lighter (three-letter words, three lines) and acceptable.
- *2. Precision:* page 5, Problem 5. The cards are tiny and there are 32 to cut. That is not precision that hides the pattern, but cutting and handling will take a large share of the time.
- *3. Procedure:* page 5, Problem 5. Sorting 32 strips means rotating each five-letter string up to four times in the head. The result (six groups of five and two singles) appears only after most of the sorting is done. Children who first sort by "how many Bs" turn this into choices, but the page does not force that and should not hint it.
- *4. Told, not felt:* page 4, Problem 4. The children feel the failure to build an early repeat. Why it cannot happen (a repeat would have to fit evenly into five) is likely to come from the adult. That is acceptable at this table, since it is the explanation that matters.
- *5. Adult runs it:* not found. The reading is light and the rules are stated once.

**Most likely delight:** Problem 6. "Someone divides 32 by 5": a child tries it, gets a remainder, and realises that AAAAA and BBBBB are the culprits. Close behind: on page 6, finding that ABABAB has only two readouts after the five-bead rings refused to repeat.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** With the organizer at the table, page 4, Problem 4 is a genuine investigation of choices: "Which numbers of different readouts can a ring using both A and B have on six beads? On seven? On nine?" A ten-year-old will try hard to make the seven-bead ring repeat and fail, while nine gives way to ABBABBABB. That contrast is felt, not told. Problems 5 and 6 (the prime rule, then three colours and divisibility) give the quickest child somewhere real to go.

**Page: workable.** Pages 1–3 repeat the 2–3 tasks with extra questions folded in. Page 1, Problem 1, for example, asks three things: find the rings, count the readouts, and "Someone divides 8 by 3… What went wrong?" Page 3 carries the same tiny 32-strip grid. Page 5 is two text-only proof and generalisation prompts on ruled lines, which the organizer will in practice run as a conversation. With a pair plus a referee, the 32-card sort is likely to be shortcut by calculation, and that is fine, since the prompt asks for one.

**Patterns**
- *1. Record-keeping:* page 2, Problem 2. The same four-bead boards with three lines each, and the same mismatch for four-readout rings.
- *2. Precision:* page 3, Problem 3. The same small cards. A board needs nine counters, which the kit covers.
- *3. Procedure:* page 3, Problem 3. Sorting 32 strips by mental rotation. These children are more likely to replace it with "how many Bs, then split", which is the mathematics.
- *4. Told, not felt:* page 5, Problem 6. There are 243 three-colour readouts, which cannot be built, and "p divides c^p − c" is a fact children reach by calculation and the organizer's framing, not by handling. That suits its place at the end for the quickest child.
- *5. Adult runs it:* page 5, Problems 5–6. The organizer will lead the proof. This is acceptable for this band and this adult, but most of the three children will watch for that stretch.

**Most likely delight:** Page 4. The seven-bead ring that will not repeat early however it is coloured, beside a nine-bead ring that does.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1, page 4, Problem 4 and page 6, Problem 6: replace the undefined word "readouts" with what the child does.** For example: "Make four-bead rings that read in one, two, three or four different ways from the start arrow. Which can you make?" Problem 6 should get the same change. This keeps the activity and removes the parent's need to invent a definition. It does not lift these two problems to strong for kindergartners. They stay return-visit material, as the route already marks them.
2. **Grades 2–3, page 2, Problem 2: print the eight cards as ring cards (`three_ring_cards()`, already used for K–1) instead of strips.** The first sort then uses turnable rings, the same object as the launch and the boards. The strip representation can wait for the 32-card sort, after children have sorted rings.
3. **Grades 2–3: move page 5 (Problems 5–6, the 32 cards and "divide 32 by 5") ahead of page 4 (Problem 4, "fewer than five readouts").** Children then sort and feel the five-bead families before they are asked to explain why a mixed five-bead ring cannot repeat early. Renumber the problems.
4. **Grades 2–3 page 3, Problem 3 and grades 4–5 page 2, Problem 2: give each four-bead board four writing lines, or replace the lines with a single small box for the number of readouts.** The box is the lighter record, and the question is about which numbers occur.
5. **K–1, page 1, Problem 1: drop the writing lines beside the six three-bead boards.** The task is "Make every different three-bead ring". The counters and the blank bead circles (which a child can fill with A or B) are the record.
6. **K–1 preparation, outside the page: supply the Problem 2 ring cards pre-cut, and cut one or two single four-bead and five-bead mats for Problems 3 and 5.** A child can then turn one ring against another without lifting counters. The colour-marking no-cut option loses the turning, which is the point for this band.
7. **Grades 4–5, page 3, Problem 3, and the same card grid in grades 2–3:** leave the strips, but expect cutting to take about ten minutes. A pre-cut set per table would save that time for sorting. Whether the 32-card sort takes 15 or 30 minutes is a pilot question.
8. **Grades 4–5, page 5, Problems 5–6: no page change.** The organizer leading the proof is acceptable here. If he wants the children doing more of it, this is outline: for the organizer, since a page change would not make Fermat's divisibility something children can handle.

## Questions only a rehearsal or a pilot can settle

- Whether the actual counters cover the bead circles without crowding on the 2.5 cm spacing of the four- and nine-bead boards.
- Whether children can read counter colours as A and B quickly. The printed beads are white and grey, and the counter colours are not specified.
- Whether counters slide when a mat is turned.
- How long cutting and sorting the 32 strips takes.
- Whether kindergartners can use the sticky-note start arrow without the parent operating it.
- Whether the K–1 return problems (4 and 6) can be run by the parent volunteer at all after fix 1.
