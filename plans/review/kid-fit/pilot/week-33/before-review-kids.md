I rendered and looked at all 18 pages (6 per band). The review is from the pages and PROMPT.md only. Nothing here comes from watching children use the packet. Counter fit, how long the cutting takes, and how long each problem takes can only be settled by a rehearsal or a pilot (see the end).

The same core move runs through all three bands. Children lay A and B counters on a printed ring, move a sticky-note start arrow, read the beads clockwise, and decide whether two arrangements are "the same ring" by turning. The materials enforce the colours. They do not enforce the reading order or the sameness judgment, which happen in the child's head. That limits every band, and K–1 most.

## K–1

**Activity: workable.** Putting counters on a three-bead ring and finding "every different three-bead ring" (P1) is a quick, physical, findable list of four. "Do all groups have the same number of cards?" (P2) has a visible answer: AAA and BBB are each alone, and the others come in threes. "Find every different four-bead ring with two A beads and two B beads" (P3) is a good two-answer hunt. With the parent volunteer, a first visit on P1–P4 can work. It falls short of strong because "same ring by turning" and "different readouts" are two different kinds of sameness. A five- or six-year-old has to hold both, and an adult has to keep sorting them out.

**Page: workable.** The boards are large and the first four problems are one or two sentences. Three things weaken the page at the table. The shared rules are five sentences read aloud, with "readout", "clockwise" and "one-color rings are allowed". P2's cards show each readout as a straight row, so a child must wrap the row into a circle in their head. P5 and P6 ask "How many different readouts does each have?" but leave nowhere to keep five-letter readouts, so the parent ends up writing them.

**K–1 bar: not met.** Within a minute of the launch a non-reader can put counters on the ring. The core decision is whether two arrangements are the same ring, or whether this start gives a new readout. That decision needs one of two things:
- turning a paper mat with loose counters on it, which a five-year-old will likely scatter (this is untested), or
- reading letters clockwise from a moved arrow and comparing them with an earlier reading held in memory.

Neither is enforced by the materials or by a partner. The parent volunteer, who is not a mathematician, would have to judge sameness for the children and keep "different ring" apart from "different readout" for them.

**Patterns found**
- **5. The adult runs it** (K–1 p.1, shared rules). Five rules are read aloud before P1, and the word "readout" only becomes meaningful through the picture. P5 (p.5) puts both meanings of "different" in one problem: "Find every different five-bead ring… How many different readouts does each have?" The adult will run that distinction.
- **1. Record-keeping** (K–1 p.5 P5, p.6 P6). To count the readouts of AABBB, a child moves the arrow five times and must compare five five-letter words. A kindergartner cannot hold those in mind and the page has nowhere to write them, so the adult transcribes. P6 is the same with six letters.
- **3. Procedure** (K–1 p.2 P2). To sort a card, the child builds it on a ring counter by counter, then moves the start or turns the mat to test it against each existing group. It works, but it is the same routine eight times with the question waiting at the end.
- **4. Told, not felt** (K–1 p.4 P4). "Which are possible?" has the answer that exactly three readouts is impossible on four beads. A K–1 child tries two or three rings and stops, so the impossibility will be announced by the adult, not felt.
- **2. Precision**: not a real problem. The beads only need to be on the right spots.

**Moment most likely to delight:** P2, when AAB, ABA and BAA turn out to be one ring once the arrow moves, and AAA and BBB are left "all alone" while the other cards come in threes.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** The packet works as a run of quests that a mathematician can run as play. The four-bead challenge "Make four-bead rings with exactly one, two, three or four different readouts. Which numbers are possible?" (P3) holds a real dead end at three. "Can a five-bead ring using both A and B have fewer than five different readouts? Find one or explain why it cannot happen" (P5) invites children to try to beat the five. P7 then shows that six beads repeat early easily. Third graders can count arrow moves until a ring reads the same again, so they need not keep every readout.

**Page: workable.** The order works against the best moment. P4, the 32-card sort ("Group these five-bead cards by the rings they make. Find the size of each group…"), comes before P5. So the long cutting-and-sorting job happens before children have a reason to care whether the groups are all five. The 32 cards are drawn with gaps between them, so cutting them out means four cuts per card, about 128 cuts. No five-bead ring board appears until P5, after the sort that needs one.

**Patterns found**
- **1. Record-keeping** (2–3 p.4 P4). Children cut out and sort 32 cards to confirm group sizes. Once they notice that cards in one family have the same number of Bs, the piles become 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1. Splitting each 10 into two fives is the real thinking, and the rest is clerical. P1 (p.1) also asks children to write every readout of every three-bead ring, about twelve three-letter words. That is short, so it is a mild case.
- **3. Procedure** (2–3 p.4 P4). For each card the routine is: build it, rotate it, check it against the groups, file it. That is 32 times, and the result only shows at the end.
- **5. The adult runs it** (2–3 p.5 P6). "Someone divides 32 by 5 to count the five-bead rings. What went wrong?" is an adult-voiced question. It is fine late in the packet, but the mathematician will probably lead it.
- **2. Precision and 4. Told, not felt**: neither is significant. The "always five" fact is visible at five beads, and the six-bead contrast in P7 is buildable.

**Moment most likely to delight:** P3, discovering that no four-bead ring has exactly three readouts. A close second comes when a child who has been trying to "beat five" in P5 gets ABABAB down to two readouts at once on six beads in P7.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** With the organizer and three children, the contrast "Which numbers of different readouts can a ring using both A and B have on six beads? On seven? On nine?" (P5) is the heart of the packet. Seven beads refuse to repeat early, while nine beads give AABAABAAB with three readouts. That leads straight into "What numbers of different readouts are possible for a ring of prime length…? Find a rule and explain it" (P6). The children can act, observe and check, and the proof grows out of rings they have built.

**Page: workable.** The first two problems cover the same three-bead ground twice. P1 asks for the three-bead ring list and P2 sorts the same eight readouts as cards. That is about ten minutes before anything new for a 4th–5th grader. P4 repeats the 32-card sort. The packet has no five-bead ring board at all, though P4 asks children to group by turning and P7 is about five-bead rings with three colours. P7 packs three tasks into one problem: the three-colour count, a rule in c and p, and divisibility.

**Patterns found**
- **1. Record-keeping** (4–5 p.4 P4). This is the same 32-card cut and sort as 2–3, for three children. The only new part is the request to count the rings with a calculation.
- **3. Procedure** (4–5 p.2 P2). Sorting eight cards whose families the children already found in P1 is a repeat, not a choice.
- **4. Told, not felt** (4–5 p.6 P7). "Find a counting rule for c colors and any prime length p, and explain what it tells you about divisibility." The divisibility consequence (Fermat) cannot be seen at a buildable scale; three colours on five beads already gives 243 readouts. The adult will name it. That is acceptable as the last, readiness-dependent problem.
- **5. The adult runs it** (4–5 p.6 P7). The variables c and p and the three-part ask make this organizer-led. That is also acceptable this late in the packet.
- **2. Precision**: possible on the 9-bead board (p.5). The bead spots are about 2.5 cm apart from centre to centre, so 1-inch counters will touch. This is untested.

**Moment most likely to delight:** P5. Children try hard to make a seven-bead ring repeat early and cannot, while nine beads give AABAABAAB with three readouts at once.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **K–1 p.2 P2: draw each card as a small three-bead ring with its start mark, not a straight row.** A child can then turn one card until it looks like another, so the card itself shows sameness and the five-year-old no longer has to wrap a row in their head. Keep the eight readouts and the question unchanged. This is the change most likely to move K–1 toward the bar. Keep rows for 2–3 and 4–5, where reading a word is easy.
2. **2–3 pp.4–5: swap P4 and P5.** Put "Can a five-bead ring using both A and B have fewer than five different readouts?" before the 32-card sort. The sort then answers a question children already care about, whether the groups are always five, instead of confirming sizes in advance. Renumber the problems to match.
3. **K–1 p.1 shared rules: cut to two short read-aloud sentences.** For example: "Read the beads going round from the start arrow. Rings are the same when turning makes them match." Leave "one-color rings are allowed" and "do not flip" to the adult's launch. The AAB/ABA/BAA picture already carries "readout".
4. **K–1 p.5 P5 and p.6 P6: give the readout count a form a non-writer can keep, or move it to the adult guide.** The smallest page change is to drop "How many different readouts does each have?" from K–1 P5, so the problem stays "find every ring". The six-bead problem P6 can be marked return-visit material in the facilitator guide, as the guide already suggests. If the readout count stays, an adult needs a way to record it, and that belongs in the facilitator guide.
5. **2–3 p.4 and 4–5 p.4: draw the 32 cards edge to edge on a grid.** Cutting then becomes 3 + 7 straight cuts instead of about 128. Alternatively, print the colour-marking no-cut option as the default in the adult preparation notes.
6. **4–5 pp.1–2: merge P2 into P1** and drop the eight-card sheet for 4–5. For example: "Find every three-bead ring using A and B. How many different readouts does each ring have? Someone divides 8 by 3 to count the rings. What went wrong?" This gets the band to four, five and six beads ten minutes sooner.
7. **4–5 packet: add one five-bead ring board.** Put it on p.4 beside the cards, or on p.6 beside P7, since P4 asks children to group by turning and P7 is about five-bead rings. Add the same board for 2–3 next to the 32-card sort, since P5's boards come after it.
8. **All bands, blank working boards: reconsider the fixed printed "start" arrow at the top of each board.** A child who moves the sticky-note start now sees two starts. Either drop the printed start from blank boards and keep the clockwise arrow, or have the launch say that the sticky note covers it.
9. **2–3 p.3 and 4–5 p.3: the four boards look like one per answer** (one, two, three and four readouts). Some children may force a "three". This is low priority, and a single spare board, or labelling the extras as workspace, would remove the cue.

No band needs an outline-level change. K–1's shortfall is mostly the page plus the lack of a self-checking material for sameness. Fix 1 addresses that within the existing cards and counters. Whether it is enough to meet the K–1 bar is a pilot question.

## Only a rehearsal or a pilot can settle

- Whether the actual counters fit the printed bead spots (8.4 mm circles). They fit within about 2.5 cm spacing on the 7- and 9-bead boards, but this is untested.
- Whether the counters stay put when a child turns a paper mat. If they do not, the arrow-moving method has to carry the whole "same ring" check.
- How long cutting 32 cards takes a pair of 3rd graders, and whether they find the same-number-of-Bs shortcut on their own.
- Whether K–1 children read clockwise from a moved arrow reliably, or skip and reverse.
- Whether "try to beat five" (2–3 P5) feels like a puzzle or like failure after several attempts.
- Whether 4–5 reach P6 inside 35–40 minutes once P1 and P2 are merged.
