# Kid-fit review: Week 77, Persistent holes (draft grades-4-5.pdf, F77-45-v4)

| Band | Activity | Page | K–1 bar |

| Grades 4–5 | workable | weak | |

There is only one packet. The outline says there are no K–1 or Grades 2–3 editions, so both of those bands are **skip** (no packet), and the K–1 bar does not apply.

I looked at all six rendered pages (80 dpi, with a zoom on the page-1 token diagram). `draft/src/` is empty, so I worked from the PDF alone. I have not seen children use this packet, and the verdicts below are a filter, not a pilot result.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: workable.** The table has two fourth graders, one fifth grader and the organizer. The core action is concrete and checkable by a partner: lay yarn edges on printed places, then place a paper tile only once all three of its edges are present. The questions underneath are real ones. Problem 6 asks children to "Save two loops ... Both must survive now and first disappear at the last filling, whichever of the two unfilled triangles your partner fills first", which is a design challenge with an opponent. Problem 3's contrast between "0, 1, 2, 1, 0 holes" and different death stages is the point of the week. The boards are tiny, though: two to four triangles, and each problem has two to four builds. So the hands get little to do between decisions, and the activity depends on the organizer to make the stakes feel real. Run as "build it, fill it, is my loop still there?" with the organizer improvising, it can carry the hour for this table. It is unlikely to be a week children ask to repeat.

**Page: weak.** Page 1 is about 230 words in five paragraphs. It sets up three rule systems before any problem: what a saved loop is, the building rules, and a token test with "any selection of the currently filled triangles ... each at most once". Its picture strip also gives away Problem 1: "One filled half does not fill the saved square rim. Both filled halves together do." After that, Problems 2 and 3 run the same scripted four-stage square schedule. Problem 3's best question is answered by the page itself ("Every build on these two boards has 0, 1, 2, 1, 0 holes"). Problems 4–6 are better, but a fourth grader will have to sit through the adult reading page 1 aloud before reaching them.

### Minute by minute (one fourth grader, after the launch)

- **Minute 5.** Still on page 1. The organizer reads the rules and the token test aloud while the child holds yarn. Problem 1 takes about two minutes: lay ABC, fill it, done. The second loop is the rim, which page 1 has just shown. The child writes "AB, BC, CD, DA" and has nothing left to decide, with half of page 2 blank.
- **Minute 15.** Problem 2. The child works through the four scripted builds (DA or AC first, then ABC or ACD first), with the referee checking each fill. The interesting moment is the DA-first build: after ABC is filled, a triangular hole is still visible and the saved rim is "still there". After that it is copying schedules onto lines. "Explain why you have all of them" amounts to "two choices times two choices", which takes a few seconds.
- **Minute 30.** The child is partway into Problem 3, rebuilding the same idea on the bowtie, then flipping back a page to "Answer the same question for the square in Problem 2", which was effectively settled in Problem 2. The fifth grader may reach Problem 6, the one page where the child is choosing rather than running a script. That is where real engagement is most likely.

### The five patterns

1. **Hands-on start that turns into record-keeping:** present, but moderate.
   - Page 3, Problem 2: "Find every schedule ..." with eight answer lines.
   - Page 4, Problem 3: four schedule slots (AB first / DE first / Square, AC first / Square, DA first).
   - In both, the child is mostly writing out which of two edges and which of two triangles came first. The records confirm what the build already showed and serve no goal the child set. The square slots in Problem 3 repeat the result of Problem 2.
2. **Precision children don't have:** not found in the mathematics. The only fit risk is physical. Problem 1's and Problem 2's boards are about 2.4–2.7 inches across, small for yarn plus paper tiles plus duplicate edge tokens. The page does not say whether children build on the printed board or on the separate large boards listed in the materials. Each board needs its own matching set of tiles (two square halves, two thin bowtie triangles, four quarter triangles). Letters are reused with different meanings: CD is a rim edge on the square and a spoke on the bowtie. Only a rehearsal can settle whether this fits and whether the tokens get mixed up.
3. **A procedure instead of choices:** present.
   - Pages 3–4, Problems 2 and 3, where the page supplies the schedule: "Stage 0: AB, BC, CD / Stage 2: one of DA and AC / Stage 4: the other edge / Stage 5 ... / Stage 8 ...". The child's only choices are two binary switches, and what happens to the loop shows only at stage 5 or 8.
   - The page-1 token test is a second procedure. On every board in this packet it confirms what the child can already see, namely whether everything inside the yarn loop is covered by tiles. Copying tokens, adding three more per tile and crossing out pairs is bookkeeping a ten-year-old will skip, or hand to the adult, once they notice the shortcut.
4. **Told, not felt:** present in two places.
   - Page 1's four-picture strip and sentence ("Both filled halves together do") state the rim fact before Problem 1 asks for it.
   - Page 4, end of Problem 3: "Every build on these two boards has 0, 1, 2, 1, 0 holes after stages 0, 2, 4, 5, 8." This is the punchline of the week (same counts, different histories), and the page announces it instead of letting children find the matching counts in their own builds.
5. **The adult runs it:** present on page 1. The page asks children to hold several rules at once: printed edge places only, no removal, a full boundary before a fill, AB = BA, a saved list kept unchanged, the token test with any selection used at most once, and later a separate "hole" definition (Problem 3) and simultaneous stages (Problem 4). The organizer will end up reading and operating page 1. From Problem 1 on, with the referee child enforcing fills, children can probably run the builds themselves. Whether they use the token test at all is something only a pilot can show.

### Moment most likely to delight

The most likely is Problem 6, second part: realizing that the obvious first pair (rim plus A-O-C-D) cannot work, and finding that the two five-edge loops (A-B-O-C-D and A-O-B-C-D) let either one die first. Partner-picks-the-fill gives it a game feel. A smaller "huh" is likely in Problem 2/3 on the DA-first square: one half is filled and a hole is plainly still there, yet the hole you can see is the newer triangle and the old rim is the one still alive. Problem 4's hidden two-hole moment ("move exactly one of those two additions") is a neat, short find. Only the quickest child will reach Problem 6, so the reliable delight is weak for most of the table.

### What only a rehearsal or pilot could settle

- Whether yarn stays put and tiles fit each board at the printed sizes.
- Whether children use the token test or simply look at what is covered.
- How long each problem takes. My guesses are P1 about 3 minutes, P2 about 8, P3 about 10, P4 about 6, P5 about 4, P6 12 or more. That is likely enough for the hour, but P1 is thin.
- Whether children accept "the rim survives while a triangle hole remains" as meaningful or as an arbitrary rule.

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **Page 1: cut to the building rules and one definition, and move the token test off the student page.** State "a saved loop is gone when the filled tiles cover everything inside it" (the tiling act the outline endorses; on these planar boards it is the same test). Drop the "any selection ... at most once" paragraph. Give the token cancellation to the organizer as the referee's check for disputes and for Problems 5–6 explanations. Keep at most a two-token mini-example if the organizer wants it shown. This removes about half of page 1's reading. Whether the token mechanism must appear on the student page at all is **outline: for the organizer**.
2. **Page 1 strip: stop giving away the rim.** Keep "Build the rim / Add the diagonal" and the definition, but drop or neutralize the "Fill one half / Fill both halves" panels and the sentence "One filled half does not fill the saved square rim. Both filled halves together do." Show the definition on a single filled triangle instead.
3. **Page 2, Problem 1: replace the thin task with a choice-rich opener on Problem 6's board** (square with centre O, four quarter triangles, same materials). Ask: "Build a loop that needs exactly 1 filled triangle to disappear, one that needs exactly 2, one that needs 3, and one that needs 4." This gives every child several builds and real choices. It includes the surprise that A-B-O-C-D-O-A is a legal loop needing ABO and CDO. It also prepares Problem 6. Size the board so tiles and yarn fit, and say "build on the large board" if the separate boards are meant.
4. **Page 4, Problem 3: let children find the hole counts.** Delete "Every build on these two boards has 0, 1, 2, 1, 0 holes after stages 0, 2, 4, 5, 8." Ask instead: "Find two builds that have the same number of holes after every stage, but whose first loops disappear at different stages." Keep the hole definition. Also drop the "Square, AC first / Square, DA first" slots, which repeat Problem 2.
5. **Page 3, Problem 2: shrink the recording.** Ask for the schedules where the saved loop survives stage 5, with two short lines instead of eight. Or merge Problem 2 into Problem 3 so the square and the bowtie are compared in one problem rather than scripted twice.
6. **Page 6, Problem 6: make the second paragraph's link explicit.** Write "Use the same two saved loops." in place of "Keep the two saved edge lists unchanged.", so a child who chose the rim in part 1 knows they may need to go back and choose differently. Leave the rest. This is the packet's strongest task.
7. **Stage numbers 0, 2, 4, 5, 8 (pages 3–5):** these mean nothing to children; only Problem 4's "stage 3 or 5" uses the gaps. Leave them, but the organizer should know they read as arbitrary. Renaming them to consecutive stages outside Problem 4 is **outline: for the organizer**, since the outline fixes the schedule.

A page change can lift this packet to workable. Whether the activity rises above workable for 9–10-year-olds depends on whether a two-to-four-triangle board gives enough to do with the hands. That is **outline: for the organizer** and a question for a pilot.
