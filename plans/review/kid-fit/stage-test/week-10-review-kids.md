I based this on renders of every page of the three draft PDFs. `draft/src/` is empty, so I could not read the sources. I have not watched children use these pages. Everything below is a prediction. Only a rehearsal or pilot can settle the points listed under "What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle".

The core action is the same in all three bands. A counter sits on each bridge, a token crosses a bridge, and the walker picks up that bridge's counter. This is the best kind of material for this group: the counters enforce "never twice" and show what is left, much as the pattern blocks did in the tiling week. Every band's activity rests on it, so every activity is strong. Each page loses ground in a different place.

---

## K–1

**Activity: strong.** Within a minute of the launch, a child who cannot read can put the token on a circle, slide it along a grey road and hand the counter to their partner. A stranded counter shows a failed walk with no adult needed. Problem 3's three-legged town ("Can you pick up every counter?") strands the token after the first leg, which is the quickest "stuck!" in the packet.

**Page: workable.** Problems 1, 3 and 4 are close to ideal. Each has one or two sentences, big islands, a check box and several contrasting towns. Problem 2 ("Color every island where your token can start…") and Problem 8 ("Color each bridge where that works") ask young children to test every case. Problem 5's tracing rule exists only on paper. The best task, Problem 7 (building a town for a partner), sits on page 10, where most of the table will never get to it in 35 minutes.

**K–1 bar: met** for the core move in Problems 1, 3, 4, 6 and 7. Two conditions apply:
- Problem 8's meaning of two counters is never stated.
- A parent volunteer with no answer sheet cannot tell whether a child's "X" in Problems 3 and 4 is right. That parent may keep children pushing at an impossible town or accept a wrong X. The fix is a one-page answer key, which is a preparation item, not a page change.

**One child at the table (a kindergartner, paired with a first grader):**
- **Minute 5.** Problem 1. The triangle and the bow-tie work on the first try, which is satisfying. The diamond fails when started from the top. The partner puts the counters back and they try again from the side.
- **Minute 15.** Problem 2's 2-by-3 grid, a sixth or seventh reset of 7 counters. The child is choosing a starting circle each time, which is a real choice, but the task is wearing thin.
- **Minute 30.** Probably Problem 4 or the tracing in Problem 5. Very likely they have not reached the building in Problem 7.

**Patterns found**
1. **Record-keeping (K–1, p. 2, Problem 2).** "Every island where your token can start" means testing all 6 starts in both towns: about 12 walks and 12 resets of 7 to 9 counters. The contrast is worth having. In the grid only the two middle islands work, and in the triangle every island works. The cost is many repeated walks before the contrast shows. **Same pattern, p. 11–12, Problem 8.** Three towns with 6, 6 and 8 bridges make 20 bridge-by-bridge cases, each needing several attempts before a child can say "doesn't work".
2. **Precision (K–1, pp. 7–8, Problem 5).** This is mild. The drawings are a usable size, but a five-year-old's pencil wanders, slips over an earlier line or lifts without noticing. Each picture has one spare copy, so a child gets two attempts at an impossible figure before crossing it out.
3. **Procedure instead of choices.** Not found. Every walk is a sequence of the child's own choices.
4. **Told, not felt.** This is mostly avoided. Impossible towns are felt as a stranded counter. The one place an adult may have to rule is the four-island town at the bottom of p. 4 (Problem 3), where every island has 3 bridges. A five-year-old tries a few times and then wants to know whether it is really impossible. The parent needs the answer key to say so with confidence.
5. **Adult runs it (K–1, p. 11, Problem 8).** "Put two counters on one bridge" depends on the child working out from the rules that the bridge can now be crossed twice. A kindergartner won't, so the parent will explain and then supervise 20 cases. Problem 5 has a smaller version of the same problem. Its rule ("without lifting the pencil and without going over a line twice") is the kind of rule that exists only on paper, which is what failed in the lamp-puzzle session. A watching partner can enforce it, but the page does not set that up.

**Most likely delight:**
- Problem 7: "make a town … where your partner cannot." Children like stumping each other, and they get to build with the hexagons they already love.
- The three-legged town in Problems 3 and 6, where the token gets stuck almost at once and then a single drawn bridge rescues it.
- The house-with-an-X drawing in Problem 5, which many children already know as a party trick.

## Grades 2–3

**Activity: strong.** Problem 2's first two towns differ by one bridge (C–F). Without it there is no walk. With it, a walk must start at A or D. A third grader who walks both can feel how one bridge changes everything. Problem 8 ("Build each of these towns … for your partner to walk") gives four design specifications that are real puzzles and set up a partner game.

**Page: workable.**
- Problem 3 says "decide without walking" straight after two problems that never ask children to look at bridges per island. A child who has not seen the rule yet can only guess, or wait for the adult to say "count the bridges".
- Problem 2 asks for every start and end in four towns of 6 or 7 islands.
- The packet has a lot of writing for 8-year-olds: Problem 6 has six ruled lines, Problem 7 asks for an explanation under each picture, and Problem 9 has ten lines.

**One child at the table (a third grader):**
- **Minute 5.** Problem 1 houses. Walking and writing "A B D E C A" is light and fun.
- **Minute 15.** Problem 2. The first town is the impossible one, so this stretch starts with a run of failures from A, B, C and so on. Then one added bridge makes it work from A, and they gasp.
- **Minute 30.** Problem 3 or 4. Either they are predicting with a rule they half-noticed, or the adult has given them "count the bridges" and they are checking it with counters. Drawing a new bridge to rescue a town in Problem 4 is satisfying.

**Patterns found**
1. **Record-keeping (2–3, pp. 2–3, Problem 2).** This means about 25 walks across four towns to find every possible start. The goal is real, and the "none" town and the "anywhere" town are good surprises. Still, a third grader may stop testing after three starts and guess. Children could split the starting islands between the two pairs and pool what they find. That is a facilitation choice, not a page change.
2. **Precision (2–3, pp. 9–10, Problem 7).** This is mild. The pentagon-with-star and the Olympic rings are about 1.4 inches across, with many crossings. A slip over an earlier line is easy to miss in the same colour.
3. **Procedure instead of choices.** Not found.
4. **Told, not felt (2–3, p. 4, Problem 3).** "Decide without walking whether a walk can pick up every counter" assumes a predictor the child may not have yet. If they don't have one, the adult announces it and the child only checks.
5. **Adult runs it.** Mostly not found. The shared rules paragraph is five sentences, which is fine for this table once it has been demonstrated. Writing in Problems 6, 7 and 9 may push children to dictate to the adult. That is fine for an explanation, but the ruled lines suggest more writing than most 8-year-olds will enjoy.

**Most likely delight:**
- Problem 2: one bridge turns a hopeless town into a walkable one.
- Problem 8: building "5 islands and 5 bridges. No walk picks up every counter" and watching the partner fail.
- Problem 7's pentagon-with-star turns out to be traceable after all.

## Grades 4–5

**Activity: strong.** These are the classic delights at the right depth:
- Königsberg in Problem 5 ("Can someone walk through the city crossing every bridge exactly once?")
- the fewest strokes for the cube and the 3-by-3 grid in Problem 7
- the delivery truck with "extra counters" in Problem 10, where counters make the doubled crossings concrete

The organizer at this table can run the proof conversations (Problems 6, 9 and 11) aloud.

**Page: workable.**
- Problems 1 and 2 repeat the 2–3 towns. Together with Problem 3, a ten-year-old walks 11 towns before reaching anything new. Königsberg, the hook, is on page 6 and will be reached around minute 30.
- Four problems (4, 6, 9 and 11) are written explanations with six or seven ruled lines each.
- Problem 8's ordering condition ("Lee's nine bridges still come in the order Lee crossed them") can only be checked by reading back a 19-letter string.

**One child at the table (a fourth grader in the pair):**
- **Minute 5.** Problem 1, easy and quick.
- **Minute 15.** Problem 2 grids. This is useful, but it is still repeated walking for a child who may already have the rule by now.
- **Minute 30.** Problem 4 or 5. They write a rule, then try Königsberg with counters on the map. That moment of seeing the rule settle a famous puzzle is where the excitement is, and it is late.

**Patterns found**
1. **Record-keeping (4–5, p. 9, Problem 8).** The splicing idea is good. However, the condition "Lee's nine bridges still come in the order Lee crossed them" lives only in the text. Nothing on the map marks Lee's bridges, so the child must check a long letter string against A B C D E F G H I A. That is transcription, and it distracts from the loop-joining idea.
2. **Precision (4–5, p. 6, Problem 5).** The Königsberg map draws each pair of A–B and A–C bridges as one continuous strip from B to C passing behind A. The B–D and D–C bridges are drawn the same way, as one strip behind D. A child may count three long bridges instead of seven, or place counters wrongly. The tracing pictures in Problem 7 (cube, 3-by-3 grid) are small but adequate for fourth and fifth graders.
3. **Procedure instead of choices.** Not found.
4. **Told, not felt.** Mostly avoided.
   - Problem 6 ("exactly one island with an odd number of bridges?") can be felt by building with sticks, where every stick adds two ends, but the page does not invite it.
   - Problem 9, the sufficiency proofs, is abstract. With the organizer it is a good late extension for the fifth grader. For a fourth grader it is likely to be told.
5. **Adult runs it.** Not found for most of the packet. Four written explanations is a lot of writing for three children, so expect them to be done aloud.

**Most likely delight:**
- Königsberg in Problem 5: "the famous one is impossible, and now I know why".
- Problem 7's cube needs 4 strokes, which children don't expect.
- Problem 10's truck: one extra counter for the four-island town if the route may end elsewhere, two if it must come back.

## What only a rehearsal or pilot can settle

- **Counters on bands.** Islands print about 0.74 inch across and bands about 0.5 to 0.6 inch wide. The shortest gaps between islands, for example the three-legged town and the bottom town on K–1 p. 4, are about 1.2 inches. A standard 1-inch counter overhangs the band and may crowd neighbouring counters near busy islands, such as the centre of the K–1 p. 6 fan and J in the 4–5 Problem 8.
- **Counters on craft sticks.** It is untested whether a counter stays put on a 3/8-inch craft stick, and whether 4.5-inch sticks between hexagons fit a 6-island, 8-bridge town (2–3 Problem 8) on a shared table without sticks crossing.
- **Timing.** It is unknown how long the "every start" problems take: K–1 Problem 2, 2–3 and 4–5 Problem 2.
- **The parent volunteer.** It is unknown whether the parent can judge K–1 checks and X's without an answer key, and whether resetting counters after each failure feels like play or like chores to five-year-olds.
- **The token.** The materials list does not name what the K–1 token is.

## Fixes, most important first

1. **Move K–1 Problem 7 to follow Problem 4** (K–1, p. 10). The building task is the strongest K–1 action and the partner game, and the outline names it. Most children will not reach page 10. This is a reorder only. Tracing and the two-counter problem then follow.
2. **State what a second counter means in K–1 Problem 8,** and shrink the search (K–1, p. 11). Add "Then the token can cross that bridge two times." Change "Color each bridge where that works" to "Find a bridge where that works in each town, and color it." Finding every such bridge in the square town can stay with the adult as an extension.
3. **Reword 2–3 Problem 3 as predict-then-check** (2–3, p. 4). Change "decide without walking whether…" to "Before you walk, guess whether…, then check with counters." This keeps the structure but no longer presumes a rule the child may not have. The 4–5 wording can stay.
4. **Bring Königsberg forward in 4–5** (4–5, p. 6). Make the first question of Problem 5 a new Problem 2: the map, counters on its seven bridges, and "Can someone walk … crossing every bridge exactly once?" Keep the "Explain", new-bridge and closed-walk questions after the rule in today's Problem 4. Children then feel the impossibility early and the rule answers a question they care about.
5. **Redraw the Königsberg bridges as seven separate bands** (4–5, p. 6). Each bridge should end at the shore of A or D rather than run behind it, so a child counts 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 bridges without help.
6. **Mark Lee's loop on the 4–5 Problem 8 map** (4–5, p. 9). Number the nine outer bridges 1–9 beside the bands, or draw them in a darker shade, so the ordering condition can be checked by eye or with a second colour of counter.
7. **Make the new bridge in K–1 Problem 6 a laid stick before a drawn line** (K–1, p. 9). Change the task to "Lay one craft stick as a new bridge in each town so that you can. Then draw it." A child can then try and move it freely instead of erasing pencil.
8. **Put the impossible-then-possible pair in 2–3 and 4–5 Problem 2 in success-first order** (p. 2 in both). Show the town with C–F first and the town without it second. Children then lose a bridge and feel the walk break, instead of opening Problem 2 with a run of failures. This is optional, since either order gives the contrast.
9. **Cut the explanation lines in 2–3 Problem 7** (2–3, pp. 9–10). Use one short line per picture, or none, so the explanations stay mostly oral. Do the same for the 4–5 written problems if the organizer expects to discuss them aloud.
10. **Provide a one-page K–1 answer key for the parent volunteer** (outline: for the organizer). This is a preparation item, not a student-page change. The workflow does not produce a guide, but this table depends on one.

Two points outside kid-fit, for the format reviewer: the 2–3 footer reads F10-M-v6 where the outline gives F10-M-v4, and 2–3 pages 8 and 10 carry "Problem N, continued" labels.
