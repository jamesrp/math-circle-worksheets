Week NN: Poisoned cookie (Chomp)

Mathematical kernels

1. Strategy stealing. A rectangle of cookies has a poisoned cookie in its bottom-left corner. A move picks any remaining cookie and eats it together with every cookie above it and to its right; whoever has to eat the poison loses. On every rectangle bigger than one cookie the first player can win. Suppose not: then eating only the top-right cookie loses, so the second player has a winning reply. But the top-right cookie lies in every bite, so the position after that reply is one the first player could have made directly as the first move, and it is a win for whoever made it. That contradiction proves a winning first move exists without saying which one (Gale, 1974). The argument needs only that the game always ends and cannot be drawn.

2. Explicit strategies on special boards, and none in general. On a square board, eat the cookie diagonally next to the poison: an L with two equal arms is left, and copying the opponent's bite on the other arm keeps the arms equal until the opponent must eat the poison. On two rows, the losing positions for the player to move are exactly those with the top row one shorter than the bottom row (checked up to rows of 8), so the first player bites the top-right cookie and then restores "one shorter". On one row, eat everything but the poison. On other rectangles the winning bite can be unusual and is not known by any formula: with 3 rows and 4 columns the only winning first bite is at row 2, column 3 (counting from the poison corner), leaving rows of 4, 2 and 2; with 3 rows and 5 columns it is at row 3, column 4; with 4 rows and 5 columns, at row 3, column 3 (all by computer). Even three-row Chomp has no known simple rule (Zeilberger).

Library: Week 7 classifies take-away piles by working backward, Week 8 keeps a token on the diagonal by copying (the square trick here is its cousin), Week 1e copies on half-turn-symmetric boards, and Week 83 values partizan positions. New here: strategy stealing, a proof that the first player wins with no strategy in hand, and a game where explicit strategies exist only on special boards.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 plays on 2-by-3, 2-by-4 and 3-by-3 boards with counters, using a corner card to show each bite, plays the adult, and hunts for the adult's winning first bite on squares and two-row boards; no reading, counting to 12. Grades 2–3 find the square trick and the two-row rule and test each against every reply. Grades 4–5 classify small boards by working backward, meet the strategy-stealing argument as an explanation of why the first player always has a win, and look at three-row boards where nobody knows a rule.

Materials

Printed boards of 25 mm squares at 100%: 2 by 3, 2 by 4, 2 by 6, 3 by 3, 3 by 4, 4 by 4 and 3 by 5, poison square marked in the bottom-left; one counter per square in a single colour plus one clearly different poison counter (about 25 counters per pair, five sets). A bite card per pair: a large L-shaped card corner (about 15 by 15 cm) whose inner corner is placed on the chosen cookie, so that everything under the card is eaten; this enforces the bite rule with the material. Boards are rebuilt after each game, so children need a cup of counters each. Real crackers on the same grids are an adult option, not required. The launch shows one bite on a non-task board, such as 2 by 5, with the bite card.
