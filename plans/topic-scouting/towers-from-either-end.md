Week NN: Towers from either end

Mathematical kernels

1. The colouring guarantee. A row of cube towers stands on a strip of slots. Two players take turns removing one whole tower from either end of the row; when the row is empty, whoever has collected more cubes wins, and equal hauls tie. When the number of towers is even, shade the slots alternately (slot 1 red, slot 2 blue, and so on). The two ends of an even row are different colours, so the first player can take an end of whichever colour holds more cubes in total. After that move both ends of the remaining row are the other colour, so the opponent must take that colour, and this exposes the first player's colour again. The first player can therefore collect every tower of one colour, never less than half the cubes: an even row is never a loss for the first player. Checked by computer for every row of 2, 4 or 6 towers of heights 1 to 4. The guarantee is not best play: on towers 2, 1, 2, 3 the red and blue totals are both 4, so the colour plan only promises a tie, but the first player can win 5 to 3 by taking the 3, answering a 2 with the other 2.

2. Odd rows and best play. With an odd number of towers the first player can lose: on 1, 5, 1 either first move hands over the 5, and the first player loses 2 to 5, while 5, 1, 5 is a first-player win. (Among the 1,024 rows of five towers of heights 1 to 4, the first player loses 143 and ties 147, by computer.) Best play on any row is found by working backward over shorter rows: the most the player to move can win by on a row is the larger of "left tower minus the best margin on the rest" and "right tower minus the best margin on the rest". Rows of one tower are immediate, rows of two are "take the taller", and a triangle of n(n + 1)/2 inner rows solves a row of n. This is interval dynamic programming, the same backward reasoning as take-away games but with scores instead of win/lose labels. No simple rule is known for odd rows (Winkler).

Sources: Peter Winkler, Mathematical Puzzles: A Connoisseur's Collection (A K Peters, 2004), the puzzle "Coins in a Row" (page not checked); Ivars Peterson, "Coins in a Row", Science News (Math Trek), May 13, 2005 (web page, accessed October 24, 2026, https://www.sciencenews.org/article/coins-row-0), which reports Winkler's even-row strategy and the odd-row examples; Alexander Bogomolny, "A Game With Coins", cut-the-knot (web page, accessed October 24, 2026, https://www.cut-the-knot.org/Curriculum/Games/Coins.shtml). The interval recurrence is standard dynamic programming. New here: a scoring game in which a colouring of the slots guarantees the first player at least a tie without knowing best play, and a backward table of margins over inner rows rather than win/lose labels. Numbers in this outline are checked by `check_claims.py`.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 plays with rows of four towers of 1 to 5 cubes, compares hauls by snapping each player's cubes into one stack (no adding), plays the adult who uses the colour plan on a shaded strip, and finds that 1, 5, 1 is a trap for whoever starts; needs counting to 10 and no reading beyond slot numbers. Grades 2–3 test the colour plan on even rows against every reply, break it on odd rows, and find rows where the plan ties but better play wins; adding to 20. Grades 4–5 explain why both ends always show the other colour after your move, solve rows of four and five completely by working backward from short rows, and ask what can be said about odd rows; the margin table is the deep end.

Materials

Snap cubes of one colour (or mixed colours that carry no meaning), about 30 per pair; five sets for the room. Printed slot strips at 100%: rows of 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8 square slots about 25 mm across (a 2 cm cube tower stands inside), slots numbered from the left, in two versions: plain, and alternately tinted red and blue for the colour-plan problems. The tinted version must not appear before children have played on plain strips, because the tint is the discovery. Tower heights stay between 1 and 6 so that hauls can be compared by stacking. Each player keeps taken towers in front of them and snaps them into one stack at the end. Pencil and paper only for recording a row as a list of heights. The launch shows one game on a non-task row of four towers (for example 3, 1, 4, 2), taking only end towers and comparing the two stacks at the end. Children play in pairs; at the 4–5 table the third child keeps the record or plays the winner, rotating.
