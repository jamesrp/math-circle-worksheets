Week NN: Counting-out circles (the Josephus problem)

Mathematical kernels

1. Every second person sits down. People numbered 1 to n stand in a circle. Counting starts at person 1, who stays; person 2 sits down, 3 stays, 4 sits, and so on round and round the circle, skipping anyone already sitting, until one person is left standing. For n = 1 to 16 the last person standing is 1, 1, 3, 1, 3, 5, 7, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 1. Write n as a power of two plus a remainder, n = 2^m + L with L smaller than 2^m; the last person standing is 2L + 1 (checked by computer up to 256). Why: with a power-of-two circle the count always comes back round to person 1 at the start of each lap, and each lap halves the circle, so person 1 survives; in general, once L people have sat down, a power of two remain and the next person to count is 2L + 1. The same halving gives J(2k) = 2J(k) − 1 and J(2k + 1) = 2J(k) + 1, and in binary the answer moves the leading 1 of n to the end (n = 13 = 1101 gives 1011 = 11).

2. Other counts. If every third person sits down there is no simple closed formula (the survivors for n = 1 to 16 are 1, 2, 2, 1, 4, 1, 4, 7, 1, 4, 7, 10, 13, 2, 5, 8), but each answer comes from the previous one: add 3 and wrap around the new circle, J₃(n) = (J₃(n − 1) + 2) mod n + 1. Children can find this step pattern from a table; the doubling rule for every second person is the deep end. Choosing where to stand so as to be last is the inverse question.

Sources: Ronald L. Graham, Donald E. Knuth and Oren Patashnik, Concrete Mathematics, 2nd ed. (Addison-Wesley, 1994), §1.3, "The Josephus Problem" (recurrences and the binary rotation; pages not checked here). A possible ancestor: the 2025–26 group's "solitaire card game" (year 1, Handouts 6.7 and 7.8) asked how to order a deck so that a dealing procedure gives 1, 2, …, n; if that deal moves every other card to the bottom, it is this elimination run backward. The handouts do not state the deal, so check with the organizer before calling this a return. Library: Week 4 hops a fixed number of dots round a ring, Week 89 grows patterns layer by layer, and Weeks 8 and 30 use binary and ternary. New here: an elimination process on a circle whose answer obeys a doubling recurrence and a binary rule.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 acts out circles of 3 to 8 counters (or children) on a circle mat, flipping each counter that sits down, and records who is last; spots that 2, 4 and 8 always leave person 1; counting to 16, no reading. Grades 2–3 build the table up to 16 or 20 by acting it out, find the pattern, and predict 20, 25 and 32 before checking. Grades 4–5 explain why powers of two leave person 1 and why 2L + 1 follows, try every third person, and find where to stand to be last.

Materials

Circle mats with 16 numbered spots about 3 cm across on a circle about 22 cm across, one per pair, and a second mat with 24 or 32 spots for the older tables. Two-sided counters (one side marked "sitting"), 16 to 32 per pair: a counter that sits is flipped, not removed, so the circle keeps its shape and the counting visibly skips it. A record strip "people / last one standing" for pooling a table across pairs. The launch is the whole group itself: about eight children stand in a circle and play every-second-person once, with an adult counting.
