Week NN: Who beat whom (round-robin tournaments)

Mathematical kernels

1. A line-up always exists. In a round-robin tournament every pair of players meets once and there are no ties; draw an arrow from each winner to the loser. Rédei (1934): the players can always be lined up so that each beat the next. Proof by insertion: line up some of the players; a new player goes at the front if they beat the first, at the back if they lost to the last, and otherwise there is a first place in the line where the new player beat the next person, and the person before that place beat the new player, so the new player fits in between. Rédei proved more: the number of such line-ups is always odd (checked by computer for every tournament of up to 6 players). A line-up is unique exactly when the results are a strict ranking; cyclic results give several.

2. Kings. A king is a player who beat every other player either directly or through someone they beat. Every tournament has a king, and a player with the most wins is always one (Landau, 1953): if a top scorer x reached y in neither way, then y beat x and everyone x beat, so y has more wins than x. A tournament can have one king, or three or more, but never exactly two, and no four-player tournament has four kings (Maurer, 1980); checked by computer for up to 6 players.

3. Which win counts can happen. A list of win counts, sorted from smallest, belongs to some tournament exactly when the first k numbers add to at least k(k − 1)/2 for every k, with total n(n − 1)/2 (Landau's theorem): the k weakest players already play k(k − 1)/2 games among themselves. Four players have 4 possible sorted lists, five have 9, six have 22 (computer).

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 decides each pairing of four animal cards by a quick game, lays an arrow card between every pair, then lines the animals up so that each beat the next by following arrows; finds a tournament with only one line-up and one with several; no reading beyond animal pictures. Grades 2–3 ask whether a line-up always exists, add a fifth and sixth animal by insertion, and find kings. Grades 4–5 explain the insertion argument and why a top scorer is a king, decide which win-count lists can happen by building tournaments for them, and count line-ups.

Materials

Animal or picture cards about 5 cm square, six per pair. Arrow cards: paper strips about 2 by 10 cm with an arrowhead at one end, six per pair for four players and up to fifteen for six (index cards cut in thirds work; preparation about 15 minutes for five sets). A way to decide each result that children enjoy and that cannot be revised: rock-paper-scissors between the two children holding the cards, or a face-down result card dealt by the adult; once an arrow is down it stays. A line-up strip with six boxes per pair. For the older tables, printed tournaments drawn as dots with arrows (four to six players, dots 2 cm, arrowheads at the middle of each link so they are not hidden by the dots). The launch plays out a three-animal tournament with arrow cards and lines the animals up.
