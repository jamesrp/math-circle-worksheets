Week NN: Pancake flips

Mathematical kernels

1. Sorting by flipping the top of a stack. A stack of n pancakes of different sizes is to be sorted with the largest at the bottom and each pancake on a larger one. One move slides a spatula under any pancake and flips everything above the spatula over as one block. Any stack can be sorted: flip the largest unsorted pancake to the top, then flip it down into place, two flips per pancake, at most 2n − 3 flips for n ≥ 2 (the last two pancakes need at most one flip). A lower bound comes from counting breaks: neighbouring pancakes whose sizes are not consecutive, plus one more if the bottom pancake is not the largest. A flip changes only the neighbour pair at the spatula, so it removes at most one break, and a stack with k breaks needs at least k flips. Both bounds are checked by computer for every stack of up to 7.

2. The worst stacks. The most flips any stack needs are 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8 for 2 to 7 pancakes (computer, matching OEIS A058986, which is known only up to 19 pancakes). With three pancakes only one stack needs three flips: small, large, middle from the top (1, 3, 2). With four, exactly three stacks need four flips (4 2 3 1, 2 4 1 3, 3 1 4 2 from the top), and with six, exactly two stacks need seven. Gates and Papadimitriou (1979) proved that (5n + 5)/3 flips always suffice; the exact worst case for large n is unknown. Burnt pancakes, each burnt on one side that must end facing down, need at most 1, 4, 6, 8, 10 flips for 1 to 5 pancakes (computer).

Sources: Harry Dweighter (Jacob E. Goodman), Problem E2569, American Mathematical Monthly 82 (1975), 1010; William H. Gates and Christos H. Papadimitriou, "Bounds for sorting by prefix reversal", Discrete Mathematics 27 (1979), 47–57; David S. Cohen and Manuel Blum, "On the problem of sorting burnt pancakes", Discrete Applied Mathematics 61 (1995); OEIS A058986, "Sorting by prefix reversal" (web page, accessed October 24, 2026, https://oeis.org/A058986). Library: Week 3's return visit finds the shortest sorting by neighbour swaps, Week 23 sorts with fixed comparator bars, and Week 3 runs permutations as machines. New here: prefix reversals, a two-flips-per-pancake method with a matching worst-case question, and a counted lower bound (breaks) that a 4–5 child can prove.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 sorts stacks of three and four card pancakes, counts flips with tokens, and finds the three-pancake stack that needs three flips; no reading or arithmetic beyond counting to 5. Grades 2–3 find a method that always works and say how many flips it can need, find the fewest flips for given stacks, and hunt for the worst four-stack. Grades 4–5 prove the two-flips-per-pancake bound and the breaks bound, find the worst stacks of four and five by pooling, and try burnt pancakes.

Materials

Pancakes cut from card or craft foam in six sizes about 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 cm across, one set per pair (five sets; preparation about 15 minutes with printed circles), each with its size number on both faces for recording; for burnt pancakes, one face coloured. A spatula is optional (a stiff card or the hands): the rule that only the top part of the stack can be flipped, as one block, must be demonstrated, and the partner checks it. Flip tokens to count moves. A stack is recorded as the size numbers from the top, for example 2 4 1 3. Sizes must differ by at least 1 cm so that order is visible. The launch flips a non-task stack of three (3 1 2 from the top) into order.
