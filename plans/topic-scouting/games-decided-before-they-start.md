Week NN: Games decided before they start

Mathematical kernels

1. A count that every move changes by one. A chocolate bar of m by n squares is broken into single squares; each break splits one piece along one full grid line. Every break turns one piece into two, so the number of pieces goes from 1 to mn and there are always exactly mn − 1 breaks, in any order. In the game "take turns breaking; whoever makes the last break wins", the first player wins exactly when mn is even: a 2-by-3 bar takes 5 breaks (first player), a 3-by-3 bar 8 (second player), a 2-by-4 bar 7 (first player). Nothing either player chooses matters. Breaking two stacked pieces at once would change the count, so stacking is not allowed.

2. Curve games on dots with a fixed length. In 2-Sprigs (JRMF) there are n dots; a move draws a curve from a dot to a dot, possibly the same dot, crossing no curve and touching no other dot, and no dot may have more than two curve ends. The game always lasts exactly n moves. Each move uses two of the 2n available ends, so there are at most n moves. While a dot still has a free end, a move exists: a dot with no curves can loop to itself; curves form paths and loops, the two free ends of an unfinished path lie in the same region (a path does not enclose anything), so they can be joined; and a single dot with one free end cannot be the only one left, because the used ends are always even in number. So the first player wins exactly when n is odd. In Brussels Sprouts the game starts with n crosses (each a dot with four arms); a move joins two free arms by a curve and draws a short bar across the new curve, making a new cross with two new free arms. It always lasts exactly 5n − 2 moves (3 for one cross, 8 for two): at the end every region holds exactly one free arm, and Euler's formula V − E + F = 2 then fixes the number of moves.

3. A game the players can change. In Sprouts (Conway and Paterson, 1967) each spot may have at most three curve ends, and every new curve gets a new spot in its middle. Give each spot three lives: a move uses two lives at its ends and the new spot keeps one, so the total drops by exactly one each move. The last new spot still has a life, so a game of n spots lasts at most 3n − 1 moves; it lasts at least 2n. Here choices matter: the 1- and 2-spot games are second-player wins, the 3-, 4- and 5-spot games first-player wins, and 6 and 7 spots second-player wins (Mollison's hand analysis of 6, then computer work); Applegate, Jacobson and Sleator (1991) conjectured that the first player wins exactly when n leaves remainder 3, 4 or 5 on division by 6. The point for children is the contrast: some games are fixed by a count that every move changes by the same amount, and some leave real choices within bounds.

New here: games whose length is fixed in advance by an invariant, so the winner is known before play, set against Sprouts, where a monovariant only bounds the length.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 breaks small bars, dropping a counter in a cup for each break, plays "last break wins", and plays 2-Sprigs with two end-tokens beside each dot, noticing that the same player always wins a given bar or board whatever anyone does; no reading, counting to 12. Grades 2–3 predict the winner of any bar or Sprigs board before playing and say why, then play Sprouts with life tokens and find that it is different. Grades 4–5 explain the counts (pieces up by one; ends used two at a time), use lives to bound Sprouts, play Brussels Sprouts to test 5n − 2, and meet Euler's formula as the late explanation.

Materials

Bars: printed grids of 25 mm squares on card (2 by 2, 2 by 3, 3 by 3, 2 by 4, 1 by 6, 3 by 4) cut with scissors along grid lines, one piece at a time and never stacked; or flat slabs of snap cubes pulled apart along a line. About six bars per pair, so five sets; preparation is printing and cutting out the rectangles (10–15 minutes), or snapping slabs. A cup and about 15 counters per pair to count breaks. Curve games: small whiteboards and markers, or paper and pencil, with 2 to 5 dots printed at least 4 cm apart. Life tokens: small counters placed beside each dot (two per dot for 2-Sprigs, three for Sprouts, one for each new spot); the partner removes one token for each curve end that arrives, so the end rule is enforced by the material, and checks that no curve crosses another. Brussels Sprouts crosses are drawn as plus signs at least 2 cm across. The launch shows one bar broken into squares on the board and one 2-Sprigs move with its tokens, on non-task examples.
