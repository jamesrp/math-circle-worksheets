Week NN: Hex, the game with no draws

Mathematical kernels

1. Someone always wins. Hex is played on a rhombus of n by n hexagons. Players take turns placing a counter of their colour on an empty hexagon; Red tries to join the top and bottom edges with a chain of touching red hexagons, Blue the left and right edges. The Hex theorem: when the board is full, exactly one player has a winning chain. Both cannot (two such chains would have to cross, and in the plane they cannot), and neither is impossible too (follow the boundary between the colours from a corner; Gale showed the statement is equivalent to the Brouwer fixed-point theorem). Checked by computer for every full 3-by-3 board (512) and 4-by-4 board (65,536): exactly one winner each time, half each by symmetry. So Hex can never be drawn.

2. The first player can win, but nobody can say how on big boards. Because there are no draws and an extra counter never hurts, the first player has a winning strategy on every n-by-n board (Nash's strategy-stealing argument): if the second player had one, the first player could place a counter anywhere and then follow that strategy as if they were second; if the strategy ever asks for the hexagon that already holds the extra counter, they play anywhere else, and the extra counter can only help. On 3-by-3, the centre and four other first moves win and the two sharp corners lose (5 of the 9 openings win, computer). For large boards no one can describe a winning strategy; computers have solved only small boards.

Sources: Martin Gardner, "The Game of Hex", in Hexaflexagons and Other Mathematical Diversions (University of Chicago Press, 1988; first published in 1959 as The Scientific American Book of Mathematical Puzzles and Diversions); David Gale, "The Game of Hex and the Brouwer Fixed-Point Theorem", American Mathematical Monthly 86 (1979), 818–827; Ryan B. Hayward and Bjarne Toft, Hex: The Full Story (CRC Press, 2019). Library: Week 16's Sperner lemma is the discrete cousin of the Hex theorem, Week 1e is a placement game, and the Chomp outline in this folder also uses strategy stealing (if both are taken up, teach Chomp first and treat Hex's strategy stealing as a return). New here: a topological no-draw theorem that children can test by filling boards.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 fills small boards with red and blue counters any way they like and checks that someone always has a chain, tries hard to make a board where nobody wins, and plays 3-by-3 and 4-by-4 games; no reading. Grades 2–3 try to build a full board with no winner or two winners, find which first moves win on 3-by-3, and play 5-by-5. Grades 4–5 explain why both cannot win, think about why someone must, and meet the strategy-stealing argument.

Materials

Printed Hex boards at 100% with hexagons about 25 mm across (a counter fits inside), in sizes 3 by 3, 4 by 4, 5 by 5 and 7 by 7, the top and bottom edges tinted red and the left and right edges tinted blue, labelled so the goal does not depend on colour alone. Red and blue counters, about 25 of each per pair (five sets). Spare boards for filling experiments. A chain means hexagons that share an edge; touching at a corner does not exist on a hexagon board, which is one reason the theorem holds, so the launch shows a chain on a non-task board and a place where a chain is blocked.
