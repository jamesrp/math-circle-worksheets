Week NN: Patterns you cannot avoid (first Ramsey numbers)

Mathematical kernels

1. Equally spaced threes (van der Waerden). Colour squares 1 to n of a strip red or blue so that no three equally spaced squares (such as 2, 5, 8) are all one colour. This is possible up to 8 squares and impossible for 9: the numbers of good colourings of 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 squares are 6, 10, 14, 20, 16, 6, 0 (computer), and the six of length 8 are RRBBRRBB, RBRBBRBR, RBBRRBBR and their colour swaps. Hand proof for 9: if squares 4 and 6 match (say red), then 5, 2 and 8 must all be blue, and 2, 5, 8 is equally spaced. If they differ, swapping colours and reversing the strip lets us take 4 and 5 red and 6 blue; then 3 is blue, 9 is red (3, 6, 9), 7 is blue (5, 7, 9), 8 is red (6, 7, 8), 1 is blue (1, 5, 9), 2 is red (1, 2, 3), and 2, 5, 8 are all red.

2. Sums (Schur). Colour the numbers 1 to n red or blue so that no x + y = z has all three the same colour, where x = y is allowed (so 2 + 2 = 4 counts). Possible up to 4, only as RBBR or BRRB (1 and 4 red, 2 and 3 blue, or the swap); impossible for 5: if 1 is red, then 2 is blue (1 + 1), 4 is red (2 + 2), 3 is blue (1 + 3 = 4), and 5 can be neither colour (1 + 4 and 2 + 3). With three colours the limit is 13. If x and y must be different the numbers change, so the convention has to be stated.

3. Triangles (Ramsey, R(3, 3) = 6). Colour every link among six dots red or blue: there is always a triangle of one colour. One dot has five links, so three of them share a colour, say red; if any link among their three far ends is red it closes a red triangle, and otherwise those three ends form a blue triangle. With five dots, a red pentagon and a blue five-pointed star avoid it (12 of the 1,024 colourings of five dots avoid a one-colour triangle, computer), and every colouring of six dots has at least two one-colour triangles (Goodman, 1959; computer). The game Sim colours the fifteen links of six dots in turn, and whoever completes a triangle of their own colour loses; it cannot end in a draw, and the second player wins with perfect play (Mead, Rosa and Huang, 1974, by computer).

New here: the threshold where avoiding a pattern becomes impossible, met three times in three representations (equally spaced squares, sums, triangles of links), with pigeonhole as the proof of the triangle theorem.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 colours strips of 4 to 9 squares with red and blue counters, checks for equally spaced threes with spacing combs, and finds how long a strip they can fill; plays "colour the strip" against the adult; no reading, counting to 9. Grades 2–3 find the longest good strip and all its colourings, colour the numbers 1 to 4 with no same-colour sum, and colour the links of five dots with no one-colour triangle. Grades 4–5 prove 9 squares and 5 numbers impossible by following forced colours, prove the six-dot theorem by pigeonhole, and play Sim.

Materials

Printed strips of 3 to 9 squares, 25 mm each, numbered, two of each length per pair; red and blue counters, 10 of each per pair (five sets). Spacing combs: card strips with three windows spaced 1, 2, 3 and 4 squares apart that slide along a strip to show one triple at a time, one set of four per pair (print on card and cut; about 15 minutes for five sets). Number cards 1 to 6 for the sums. Boards of five and six dots on a circle about 12 cm across, each link drawn faintly so it can be traced in red or blue pencil; extra boards for Sim. Children check each other's colourings with the combs. The launch colours a non-task strip of five squares together and checks it with the combs.
