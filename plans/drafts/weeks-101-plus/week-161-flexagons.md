# Week 161 (draft plan): Flexagons

**Strand:** symmetry (Patterns and symmetry). **Deep-math field:** combinatorics of folding, state graphs, Möbius bands. **Status:** draft plan, November 2026. No student pages, guide, review card or pilot.

## The deep idea

A hexaflexagon is a strip of equilateral triangles folded flat into a hexagon of six "pats"; pinching three corners together and opening the centre (the pinch flex) turns it inside out and shows a face that was hidden. The trihexaflexagon (Arthur Stone, Princeton, 1939) folds from a strip of 10 triangles, one a glue tab, and shows three faces; the hexahexaflexagon folds from 19 and shows six. The counts are bookkeeping children can check: a face is six triangle sides, so three faces need 18 sides on 9 triangles and six faces need 36 sides on 18. The structure is a state graph. A state is the pair of faces showing (top and bottom), and a flex moves to a neighbouring state. Tuckerman's picture of this graph is a tree of triangles whose corners are the faces and whose edges are the states, a flex moving round one triangle. For the trihexaflexagon it is a single triangle: three states, each face in two of them. For the hexahexaflexagon with Gardner's numbering it is a central triangle 1-2-3 with one more triangle on each side: 1-2-5, 2-3-4 and 3-1-6. It has 9 states; faces 1, 2, 3 lie in three triangles each and faces 4, 5, 6 in one, which is why Gardner reports that the first three turn up three times as often; no state shows two of 4, 5, 6 together; and from the starting state (1 and 2) a flex opens to 3 or 5, never 4. The reason is visible in the folding: the 18-triangle strip is first folded into nine doubled triangles, back to back, so every doubled triangle shows two of the front faces 1, 2, 3 and hides one back face between them. The face hidden between 1 and 2 is the one that sits on the outer triangle of edge 1-2. The Tuckerman traverse (keep flexing the same way until it will not open, then move over one corner) walks round the outside of this tree of triangles and returns after 12 flexes, with the faces on top running 1, 3, 6, 1, 3, 2, 4, 3, 2, 1, 5, 2. Anderson, McLean, Pajoohesh and Smith (2010) proved that with pinch flexes an order-3n hexaflexagon (9n triangles, 3n colour faces) has 6n − 3 "mathematical faces" (states), 3 for the trihexa and 9 for the hexahexa, and that its band has 3(3n − 2) half-twists (their phrase is "a Möbius band"); they also counted faces under McLean's V-flex: 3420 for the hexahexaflexagon. The half-twist counts are 3 for the trihexa, odd, so it is a true one-sided Möbius band, and 12 for the hexahexa, even, so its paper band is two-sided, which is why all of 1, 2, 3 can sit on the front of the strip and 4, 5, 6 on the back. Tukey and Feynman worked out a full theory in 1940 that was never published. Proved: the 6n − 3 count, the half-twist count and the V-flex counts (in the papers cited). Checked here by simulation: the face map, the 9 states, the 12-flex traverse and the hidden-face rule (see Checked facts). Children can discover the hidden faces, draw the face map, and notice that some faces are hard to reach.

## A kid-friendly way in

Fold a trihexaflexagon from a printed strip of 10 triangles (crease lines and numbers printed, glue tab shaded), with one adult per table helping the final fold and tape. Children colour or sticker each face as they find it (three colours on a trihexa), then draw a "face map": a dot for each colour and an arrow for each flex. Pre-folded spares let anyone who struggles with folding start flexing at once. Prep: print trihexa strips on card (two per child) and hexahexa strips for grades 4–5 (fronts 1, 2, 3 repeating; backs 5, 5, 6, 6, 4, 4 repeating, starting behind the first 1 and 2, so that the face map above holds), fold six spares, scissors, glue sticks or tape, stickers in six colours; about 25 minutes including folding spares. Launch (2–3 minutes): hold up a pre-folded trihexaflexagon showing yellow, ask what is on the back, then pinch and open it to show a third colour; show the pinch slowly twice so everyone sees where the fingers go.

## By band

- **K–1:** flex a pre-folded trihexaflexagon (or one folded with an adult), put a sticker of a new colour on each new face, and find out how many colours it has; is any face hiding?
- **2–3:** fold a trihexaflexagon, colour each face, and draw the face map; then a hexahexaflexagon from a pre-folded spiral (an adult does the rolling step): find all six faces and record which faces were easy and which were rare.
- **4–5:** fold the hexahexaflexagon, run the Tuckerman traverse and count flexes until the start returns; draw the triangle map with faces at corners and states as edges; explain from it why faces 4, 5, 6 are rare and why 6 faces need 18 triangles; predict from the unfolded strip which face is hiding between 1 and 2, then flex to check. Notice that a face's picture comes back rotated or scrambled (the triangle orientation question).

## Checked facts

Computed in `check_number_symmetry_2.py`, `check_week_161()`:

- 3 faces × 6 triangles = 18 = 9 triangles × 2 sides (10 with the tab); 6 × 6 = 36 = 18 × 2 (19 with the tab);
- 6n − 3 gives 3 and 9 states for n = 1 and 2, and 3(3n − 2) gives 3 and 12 half-twists;
- a simulation of the paper (standard-library Python in the same function). Each flat state is six pats, each a stack of numbered triangles with a known side up; a pinch flex splits every other pat at its top fold, turns the upper half over and lays the lower half on the neighbouring pat it is hinged to. After every flex the script checks that each pair of neighbouring strip triangles is still in the same pat or in neighbouring pats and that the paper's front and back sides match the hinge (folded or open). The trihexaflexagon (9 triangles, band closed with a half-twist) gives 3 states and 3 faces. Gardner's hexahexaflexagon (pairs of triangles folded back to back, then folded like a trihexa; the band closes with no net flip, matching 12 half-twists) gives 6 faces, each made of a single number, 9 states up to turning and rotating, and 12 flex links. The groups of three states one flex apart are exactly 1-2-3, 1-2-5, 2-3-4 and 3-1-6, so faces 1, 2, 3 lie in three triangles and 4, 5, 6 in one, and no state shows two of 4, 5, 6. The start shows 1 and 2, and one flex from it reaches only 3 or 5. Flexing the same way until it will not open, then shifting one pat, returns to the start after 12 flexes through all 9 states, with tops 1, 3, 6, 1, 3, 2, 4, 3, 2, 1, 5, 2 up to where the cycle starts (Wikipedia's sequence) and faces 1, 2, 3 three times each.

How this was confirmed. The structure (a central triangle of 1, 2, 3 with one outer triangle per side) comes out of the simulation for every choice of back numbering: the outer triangle on edge a-b always brings in the back face hidden inside the doubled triangles whose fronts are a and b. Which back number that is depends on the printed strip. The earlier draft put 4 on edge 1-2, 5 on 2-3 and 6 on 3-1; that is the right shape but the wrong labels for Gardner's strip, because Gardner and the Berkeley handout both say a flex from 1 and 2 reaches 3 or 5, and Wikipedia's traverse then fixes 4 on 2-3 and 6 on 3-1. So: the shape is checked by simulation; the labels by source plus simulation; the simulation's pinch rule and its "same way" rule for the traverse are my model of the paper, confirmed only by reproducing every published count, so the map, the traverse and the fit of a printed strip still need checking on a physical hexahexaflexagon. The 3420 V-flex count and the half-twist formula are cited, not computed.
