# Week 106 (Coloring maps): kid-fit review of draft

| Band | Activity | Page | K–1 bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| K–1 (draft/k-1.pdf, 8 pages) | strong | workable | not met (narrowly) |
| Grades 2–5 (draft/grades-2-5.pdf, 12 pages) | strong | workable | |

This review comes from reading the pages, not from watching children. It does not say how long any problem takes, whether a five-year-old's thick pencil fits the smallest countries, whether whiteboard markers can fill regions well, or how many games a pair plays before the strategy shows. Only a rehearsal or a pilot can settle those.

---

## K–1

**Activity: strong.** The core move is coloring a big country next to one already colored with "your two thick pencils". A child can do it within a minute of the launch, and it needs no reading. The mathematics comes out of things a child can feel. In Problem 5, going round a pond of 5 or 7 with two pencils leaves the last country touching both colors ("the last one doesn't work"). In Problem 6, the 4-petal flower takes 3 pencils and the 5-petal flower takes 4. In the strip game (Problem 7), the Spoiler wins by trapping one square between two different colors, which a six-year-old can find and repeat. The partner enforces the game's rules.

**Page: workable.** Most of it is right for the age: large maps, one or two sentences on most problems, an answer box for a numeral, and eight strips and six ponds so pairs can replay. Three things keep it below strong:
- Every map in Problems 1, 2 and 4–6 is printed once, and colored pencil doesn't erase. The likeliest wrong start leaves no way back.
- Problem 3 asks children to draw their own lines, and the parent volunteer has no way to tell why two colors failed when a line stopped partway.
- Problem 7's rules are a 7-sentence read-aloud.

**K–1 bar: not met, narrowly.** Children can make the core move themselves, and in the games the partner enforces the rules. Two things fall short:
- **Nothing undoes a stuck coloring.** In the Problem 2 Venn map (three circles), the natural start is to color each circle's outer moon with a different pencil. A child who colors two of them in different pencils gets stuck at the lens between those two moons, because it touches both colors. The only copy is then spoiled. That leaves the child with "it doesn't work". The child never gets to "once I pick one color, the rest is decided".
- **No one at the table can explain a failure on a child's own drawing.** In Problem 3 a kindergartner's "line from one side to another" will often stop in the middle or end on another line, making a T. Two colors then fail, and a parent volunteer with no guide cannot say why.

Both are page fixes: print spare copies, and give a picture of an allowed line (fixes 1 and 2 below).

### Patterns found

1. **Hands-on start that turns into record-keeping:** not found. The only recording is a numeral in a box (Problems 4–6) and a circled word (Problems 7–8).
2. **Precision children don't have:**
   - Mild, page 2, Problem 2, Venn map. The center country and the three small lenses are about 1 cm across in the 3.3 in × 1.3 in figure (about 8.5 × 3.3 cm), and a thick pencil from a five-year-old will run over the borders, so neighbors look the same color. The page has a blank bottom third that could hold bigger maps.
   - Same, page 1, Problem 1, bottom map: the small lens countries where the wiggly lines meet the left and right edges.
3. **Procedure instead of choices:** not found. At each country the child decides whether they need a new pencil.
4. **Told, not felt:**
   - Mild, pages 1–2, Problems 1–2. "Two colors always work" is only a surprise if the child expects it to fail, and a five-year-old just colors.
   - The page partly answers this. Problem 3 (draw your own) and Problem 4 (the T and Y maps that need 3) give the contrast, so a child can feel it. Nothing to change beyond fix 2.
5. **Adult runs it:**
   - Page 7, Problem 7. Seven sentences of rules: roles, turns, who goes first, what "stuck" means, both win conditions, swapping, and the question. The game itself is simple enough once it has been demonstrated.
   - Page 8, Problem 8 changes the map and who goes first at the same time. The parent volunteer has to keep track of "Spoiler goes first now".
   - Page 3, Problem 3, as above.

**Most likely delight:** the Spoiler's trap on the 4-square strip (Problem 7). The Spoiler colors the square one away from the Painter's square in the other color, and the square between them is stuck. Children will laugh and want to do it to each other again. Close behind: the gasp when the last petal or pond country "won't work" in Problems 5–6.

**What a child is doing, minute by minute (a kindergartner, Problems 1–7):**
- **Minute 5:** filling big wiggly countries in Problem 1 with two pencils, happily.
- **Minute 15:** drawing their own scribble in Problem 3, possibly with a line that stops, then coloring it. This is where they may get stuck with nothing to try again on.
- **Minute 30:** playing the strip game in Problem 7.

## Grades 2–5

**Activity: strong.** The packet varies what children do:
- color (Problem 1);
- try to break a claim on an erasable whiteboard (Problem 2: "Can you draw a map like this that cannot be colored with two colors?");
- find the fewest colors and predict "a ring of 20 countries? A ring of 25?" (Problems 4–5);
- draw (Problem 6: try for 5 countries that all touch);
- stump a partner (Problem 8: "Draw a map that you think needs five colors. Give it to your partner");
- play a strategy game where the trick changes with the number of colors (Problems 9–10);
- play in a strange world (the donut pages).

The two game problems are the high point. With 3 colors the Spoiler wins by surrounding a square with three different colors. With 4 colors the Painter wins by taking the middle square early. Third graders can find both in a few games, and fifth graders can explain them.

**Page: workable, close to strong.** The opening needs no adult and a second grader can start at once. The problems are substantial, and the later pages give the 4–5 table plenty. Four things hold it back:
- Problem 1 has small countries.
- The game, the problem most children will want, doesn't start until page 8.
- Problem 9 is a 90-word paragraph.
- The donut pages rely on arrow notation and on checking 21 pairs across the wrap with nothing on the page to help.

### Patterns found

1. **Hands-on start that turns into record-keeping:**
   - Mild, pages 4–5, Problems 4–5. These are eight maps of the same kind with "Colors: ___" lines. They earn their place, because even against odd is the contrast and the questions about 20, 25, 100 and 101 countries are the payoff. Still, a third grader coloring every country of eight rings will be tired before the questions. Children could mark each country with a dot of color.
   - Page 11, Problem 12, "Explain why you cannot use fewer colors". The explanation means checking that all 21 pairs of the 7 countries touch, partly across the wrapped edges and across pieces split by the edge, such as the slivers labeled 3 and 6 at the side edges and 5 at the bottom. That is bookkeeping, and a fifth grader may lose track.
2. **Precision children don't have:**
   - Mild, page 1, Problem 1. The four maps are about 6.5 × 5 cm. The map with three circles and a line, and the map with four lines, have many slivers, which become fiddly coloring for 2nd–3rd graders.
   - Page 6, Problem 6. When children freehand "4 countries that all touch", two countries often meet at a single point, which the rules say is not a border. The adult has to rule on it, which is fine.
3. **Procedure instead of choices:** not found.
4. **Told, not felt:**
   - Page 7, Problem 8. That no map ever needs five colors is told, not felt, and children can only fail to find one. Failing as a pair is enjoyable, so this is acceptable.
   - Page 11, Problem 12. That every country touches all six others is hard to see on one wrapped square. Unless the child can see the neighbors, "you cannot use fewer colors" will be announced by the adult.
   - Page 10, Problem 11. The donut rule is felt for the left-right wrap only if someone rolls a board into a tube, and nothing printed lets a child do that with these small boards.
5. **Adult runs it:**
   - Page 8, Problem 9. Nine sentences of rules in one paragraph. Third graders will wait for the adult to demonstrate, which the launch can cover.
   - Page 10, Problem 11. The arrows on the left and right edges point up, and the arrows on the top and bottom point right. A child reads them as "go this way", not "these two edges are the same edge", so the organizer will have to explain the notation.

**Most likely delight:** the Spoiler's three-color trap on the 3-by-3 board (Problem 9), then the switch in Problem 10, where with four colors grabbing the middle square early wins it for the Painter. Close behind:
- stumping a partner with a "five-color" map (Problem 8), and finding out it doesn't need five;
- the donut with 3 stripes needing 3 colors when the same stripes on a flat page need only 2 (Problem 11).

**What a child is doing, minute by minute:**

- **A third grader at the 2–3 table:**
  - Minute 5: coloring Problem 1.
  - Minute 15: on the whiteboard trying to break two colors (Problem 2), or on the stop-partway maps (Problem 3).
  - Minute 30: coloring rings in Problems 4–5. In order, they will probably not reach the game on page 8 within the hour (see fix 3).
- **A fifth grader:**
  - Minute 5: past Problem 1 and into Problem 2.
  - Minute 15: on rings and wheels.
  - Minute 30: drawing in Problems 6–8 or playing Problems 9–10, with the donut pages kept for a return visit or the last minutes.

## Fixes, most important first

1. **K–1, pages 1–2, 4–6 (Problems 1, 2, 4, 5, 6): give a second copy of each map.** Repeat each map smaller below it, or add one spare-maps page after page 6. A stuck coloring then becomes "try again" and not a spoiled problem. Page 2 already has a blank bottom third. The same change helps grades 2–5 Problems 1, 4 and 5, where a slip such as giving opposite countries of the 6-ring the same color turns a 2-color map into 3. Alternatively, have these maps done as quick dot-coloring on the whiteboard.
2. **K–1, page 3, Problem 3: add a small picture that shows the drawing rule.** Use the same kind of picture as the rule picture on page 1: a line from edge to edge with ✓, and a line that stops in the middle against another line with ×. That way the parent volunteer and the child can tell an allowed map from a broken one. Problem 4 then gives the reason the × maps need 3.
3. **Grades 2–5, page order: move the game (Problems 9–10) up to directly after Problem 5,** ahead of the drawing problems (Problems 6–8). The 2–3 table then reaches the most engaging problem within the hour, and the more abstract four-color problems sit later, where the quickest children will find them. Nothing else changes.
4. **K–1, page 2, Problem 2: enlarge the Venn map and the flower-loop map** to use the empty bottom third, so the center country and the lenses are big enough for a thick pencil.
5. **K–1, page 7, Problem 7: shorten the rules.** For example: "Play with a partner. Take turns coloring a square with your two thick pencils. The Painter goes first. If a square touches both colors, the Spoiler wins. If all 4 squares get colored, the Painter wins. Who can win every time?" Keep "swap jobs" for the launch.
6. **Grades 2–5, page 1, Problem 1: make the four maps larger, or use fewer slivers.** The page has room below. In particular, simplify the map with three circles and a line, which has the most tiny countries.
7. **Grades 2–5, page 10, Problem 11: replace the arrow pairs with matching edge marks** a child reads as "same edge", such as the same letter or the same small symbol on the left and right edges and a different one on top and bottom. Also make one board large enough to cut out and roll into a tube. Whether a printed board rolls well enough to show the wrap needs a rehearsal.
8. **Grades 2–5, page 11, Problem 12: let a child see each country's neighbors without wrap bookkeeping.** For example, add a smaller picture of the same board repeated 2 × 2 or 3 × 3, so every country shows all six neighbors around it in ordinary flat adjacency. This stays within the outline's hexagon-tiling construction. Without it the "explain why" is mostly told by the adult. With it, this is a strong finish for the readiest fourth and fifth graders.
9. **Grades 2–5, page 8, Problem 9: shorten the paragraph.** Drop "Take turns." (it is implied), and merge the two win conditions into one sentence. Leave "who goes first" and "swap jobs" to the launch. No mathematical change.

None of the activity verdicts needs an outline change. Both bands' activities are strong, and the fixes are all page changes.
