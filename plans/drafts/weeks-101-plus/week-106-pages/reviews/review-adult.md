## K–1 packet

**What I think the activity is (confidence: high).** Children color maps with the rule that countries sharing a border get different colors. Touching only at a corner is allowed, and the three little squares with ✓/✗ on p.1 make this quick to explain. Problems 1–3 use only two colors, on maps of curves and loops and then on maps the children draw themselves. Problems 4–6 ask for "as few pencils as you can" and a number in the box. Problems 7–8 are a game for two players, a Painter and a Spoiler.

**How I would launch Problem 1.** I would hold up the p.1 key: two squares side by side must be different, two squares touching at a corner can match. Then I would color two or three countries of the top map in front of them with the two thick pencils. After that the children color both maps on p.1 and both on p.2. On p.3 they draw their own lines from side to side (top box) and their own loops (bottom box), then color those too.

**What I think the point or surprise is (confidence: medium-high).**
- Maps made only of side-to-side lines or loops always work with two colors (P1–P3).
- When three countries meet at one spot, you need three colors (P4, the T and Y maps; the cross and the loop maps need only two).
- A ring of countries around a pond needs two colors when it has an even number of countries and three when the number is odd (P5).
- Adding a middle country to the flower adds one more color, so a flower with an odd number of petals needs four (P6).
- In the game, the Spoiler can always win on a row of four squares. On the pond map, when the Spoiler goes first, the Painter can always win.

I worked these out myself. Nothing on the page states them, which is right, but an adult will need the guide to confirm the answers.

**Where I got stuck**
- **p.1, P1 and p.4 onward: "your two thick pencils", then "as few pencils as you can".** On P1–P3 children have exactly two pencils. From P4 on they need a third pencil, and on P6 a fourth. The page doesn't say where the extra pencils come from or how many to hand out. *Slowing.* This is materials, so it goes on the adult card.
- **p.2, P2, both maps (three circles; the four-lobed flower shape):** "Color each map". Is the white area inside the box but outside the shapes a country that should be colored? The p.1 maps fill the whole box, so the question doesn't arise there. Either answer works with two colors, but children will ask. *Minor.*
- **p.1 key:** the "different colors" pair is diagonal hatching beside cross-hatching, and at a glance it looks almost like the "same color" pair. I worked it out in a few seconds. *Minor.*
- **p.1, P1, both maps: "Countries that touch only at a corner".** The key shows square corners, but on these maps the "corners" are the points where two curves cross. I made the link myself. A K–1 child may need it pointed out on the actual map. *Minor.*
- **p.3, P3: "draw lines that go from one side of the box to another side".** Does "another side" mean a different side? Can a line come back to the side it started from? It doesn't matter for the coloring, but a child will ask. *Minor.*
- **p.7, P7: "Take turns coloring one square."** The page doesn't say which color a player uses: either of the two, chosen each turn? one color per player? It also doesn't say whether the Spoiler has to follow the coloring rule. If the Spoiler doesn't, the Spoiler can just color two neighbors the same and win. The 2–5 packet settles this ("color any empty square with one of the 3 colors, keeping the coloring rule"), but the K–1 adult won't see that page. *Slowing, close to blocking*: two children at my table would argue about it straight away.
- **p.7, P7: "A square that touches both colors is stuck."** "Touches" is not the word the rules use ("share a border"). It also isn't clear that this means an *empty* square whose neighbors already show both colors. I could read it as a colored square. *Slowing.*
- **p.7, P7: eight boards and "Swap jobs after each game".** This is clear: each pair plays about four games each way. *No problem.*
- **p.8, P8: "Play the same game on these pond maps."** "Square" from P7 now means a country of the pond map, and "all 4 squares" means the 4 countries. I could follow that. P5 already said to leave the pond white. *Minor.*

## Grades 2–5 packet

**What I think the activity is (confidence: high for P1–P10, medium for P11–P13).** The packet works through one question: how few colors does a map need? It uses the same border/corner rule, stated once at the top of p.1.
- P1–P2: two colors always work for maps made of edge-to-edge lines and loops. Find a map that breaks this, or explain why none does.
- P3: maps where lines stop partway. Some need three colors. Explain why two aren't enough.
- P4: rings around a lake, ending with rings of 20 and 25 countries.
- P5: a ring with a middle country, ending with rings of 100 and 101.
- P6: draw 4 countries that all border each other, then try 5.
- P7: a map that needs four colors but has no four countries that all border each other.
- P8: try to draw a map that needs five colors.
- P9–P10: a Painter/Spoiler game on a 3×3 grid with 3 colors, then with 4.
- P11–P13: the same questions on a "donut" board whose opposite edges are joined.

**How I would launch Problem 1.** I would read the rule paragraph and draw two countries sharing a border and two meeting at a corner point on the whiteboard. Then I'd color the top-left map of P1 partway in front of them with two colors. The children color the four maps. Then they move to their whiteboards for P2 and try to draw a line-and-loop map that defeats two colors.

**What I think the point or surprise is (confidence: medium).**
- Lines and loops always allow two colors.
- Two colors fail exactly where an odd number of countries go round a point or a ring. Odd rings need 3, and odd rings with a middle country need 4.
- Five countries can't all border each other on paper, and no one finds a map that needs five colors.
- A map can need four colors without having four countries that all touch each other (P7).
- In the game, the Spoiler can always win with 3 colors, but the Painter can always win with 4. I checked both by working through every possible game, and for each the answer is the same whoever goes first.
- On the donut board, more colors can be needed. P12's map seems built so that all 7 countries border each other.

**Where I got stuck**
- **p.1, rule paragraph.** Unlike K–1, there is no little picture of "share a border" against "meet at a corner point". I could manage without one, but the K–1 key is better. *Minor.*
- **p.1, P1, bottom-right map (the five-lobed flower shape):** this raises the same question as K–1 p.2. Is the area outside the shape but inside the box a country? *Minor.*
- **p.2, P2: "Draw your hardest map in the box."** If the point is that every such map *can* be colored with two colors, I'm not sure what "hardest" means: most crossings? the one they thought would fail? The page also doesn't say whether to color the map in the box. *Slowing.*
- **p.3, P3, middle-right map.** A short line comes in from the right edge and stops in the middle of a country, touching nothing. Is the land above and below that short line one country or two? I think one country, since you can walk around the end of the line, but nothing on the page says what a "country" is when a line stops partway. This is the point of the problem ("some lines stop partway"), so children will ask an adult. *Slowing.*
- **p.3, P3, top-right map.** Six lines meet at one point, and the bottom-right map has five. They look almost the same at a glance, and I had to count the spokes carefully. That is probably intended, so it isn't a fault. *Minor.*
- **p.7, P8: "Draw a map that you think needs five colors. Give it to your partner … Then swap jobs. Keep your best attempts in the boxes."** I can't tell what goes in the two boxes. Is each one a child's own five-color challenge map? the partner's four-color coloring of it? one box per partner? I also can't tell whether the first drafts go on whiteboards (P2 used a whiteboard) or straight into the boxes. *Slowing.*
- **p.8–9, P9–P10.** These rules are clear: any empty square, any of the colors, keep the coloring rule, and "stuck" is defined. Neighbors are by shared border, so diagonal squares don't count. Children record a game by coloring a grid. *No problem.*
- **p.10, P11: what counts as a country on the donut board.** The text says "a country that runs off the right edge comes back on the left edge". Read literally, the leftmost strip and the rightmost strip in the first map are *the same country* crossing the edge, so the 4-strip map is really 3 countries and the 3-strip map is really 2. The next sentence, "countries can share a border across an edge", suggests the opposite: they are different countries with the board edge as their border. The board edge is drawn with the same line as the inner borders. P12 settles its own map by giving each piece of a country the same number on both sides of the edge, but the P11 maps have no numbers. The answers change with the reading (for the strips: 2 and 3 colors if the edge is a border, 3 and 2 if it isn't). *Blocking*: I could not tell the children what the countries are.
- **p.10, P11: the arrows.** The text says "the arrows mark the edges that are joined". It doesn't say that the double-headed arrows (top and bottom) pair with each other and the single arrows (left and right) pair with each other, or why the arrows point in a direction at all. I guessed. *Minor to slowing* for someone who has never seen this notation.
- **p.10, P11: "donut board".** The word "donut" carries no meaning beyond the stated rule. Some adults will want to show children *why* it's a donut, for example by rolling a sheet into a tube. That is teaching, not a page fault. *Minor, for the adult card.*
- **p.11, P12.** This is clear: pieces with the same number are one country, and children color with as few colors as they can and explain why. To check a child's explanation, an adult has to find every pair of bordering countries across the joined edges. That is real work with no help on the page. *Slowing, for the adult card* (the guide should say that every pair of the 7 countries borders).
- **p.12, P13: "as many countries as you can where every country shares a border with all of the others."** I don't know what "good" looks like or whether a child is done. P12 seems to be a 7-country example already, so I'd wonder whether copying it counts. *Slowing, for the adult card* (state the target and what to say when a child points at P12).

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **Grades 2–5, p.10, P11: say whether the board edge is a border.** Number the pieces of each map as P12 does, so that a strip cut by the edge carries the same number on both sides. Or, if the intent is that every drawn strip and cell is its own country, replace the first sentence with: "On a donut board, the left and right edges are glued together, and so are the top and bottom edges. Each strip or square is its own country, and countries on opposite edges share a border." Use the same convention in P11, P12 and P13.
2. **K–1, p.7, P7: say which color a player uses and that both players follow the rule.** Change "Take turns coloring one square" to "Take turns coloring one empty square with either pencil, following the coloring rule." Change "A square that touches both colors is stuck" to "An empty square that shares a border with both colors is stuck."
3. **Grades 2–5, p.7, P8: say what goes in each box.** For example: "Draw your map in the top box. Your partner draws theirs in the bottom box. Swap papers and color your partner's map with four colors." Or label the two boxes "My map" and "My partner's map".
4. **Grades 2–5, p.3, P3: say what a country is when a line stops partway.** Add to the shared rules on p.1: "A country is a piece of the map you can reach without crossing a line." This settles the middle-right map with the stray line and also the K–1/2–5 "background" question.
5. **Grades 2–5, p.2, P2: make the box task clear.** Replace "Draw your hardest map in the box" with "Draw the map you think is hardest to color in the box, and color it."
6. **K–1, p.2, P2 and grades 2–5, p.1, P1 (flower shapes): say whether the outside counts.** Add "The space outside the shapes is a country too", or the shared-rules definition from fix 4.
7. **Grades 2–5, p.10, P11: name the arrow pairs.** Change "The arrows mark the edges that are joined" to "Edges with the same arrows are joined."
8. **Grades 2–5, p.1, rules: add the K–1 three-square key** (border ✗, corner ✓) beside the rule paragraph.
9. **For the adult card (K–1):** two thick pencils per child for P1–P3, and at least four different colors per child from P4 on (P6 needs four).
10. **For the adult card (grades 2–5, P12–P13):** every pair of the 7 countries in P12 shares a border. Seven is the most possible on a donut board, so in P13 a child who reproduces P12's pattern has reached the maximum, and the interesting work is drawing it fresh or explaining why an 8th country can't fit.
11. **For the adult card (both games):** who wins each game. K–1 P7 and grades 2–5 P9 go to the Spoiler; K–1 P8 and grades 2–5 P10 go to the Painter, with either player going first in the 3×3 games. Also a one-line strategy hint for the winner.
