## K–1

**What the activity is (confidence: high).** Children color the countries in each map, and countries that share a border must get different colors. The opening rule and the three little square pictures (two squares side by side with different colors: tick; same color: cross; squares meeting at a corner with the same color: tick) make the rule clear in seconds. Problems 1–3 use only two pencils on maps made from lines that cross the whole box and from loops. Problems 4–6 ask for the fewest pencils and a number in the box. Problems 7–9 are a two-player game, and Problem 10 asks them to invent maps that need 3 and then 4 pencils.

**Launch of Problem 1.** I would hold up two thick pencils, color one region of the top map, and ask which spaces touch it along a line. Then I'd color one neighbor with the other pencil and point out that two spaces that touch only at a crossing point may share a color. The children then color both maps on page 1 with two pencils.

**Point or surprise (confidence: medium).** Maps made only from across-the-box lines and loops can always be done with two pencils. Once a line stops at another line (P4) or countries go around in a ring with an odd count (P5), or there's a middle country touching every petal (P6), two is no longer enough. The game asks who can force a win. I'm guessing at the point from the order of problems. The page doesn't state it, and that's fine.

**Where I got stuck.**
- **p1, rules: "your two thick pencils" (Problem 1, and again in P2, P3 and the game).** I don't know whether each child has two pencils, or whether partners share one pair in the game. Not a page fault, but I'd need to know before handing out materials. *Minor (adult card).*
- **p3, Problem 3, the ✗ example.** It took me a moment to see what is wrong with the crossed-out picture: the second line starts on the first line instead of at a side of the box. The text ("from one side to another side") covers it, so I could explain it. *Minor.*
- **p3, Problem 3, "In the bottom box, draw loops."** No picture or word says whether loops may overlap each other. If a child draws three separate circles, the coloring is trivial and the problem goes flat. The 2–5 packet says lines and loops "may ... cross each other," but a K–1 adult won't see that. *Slowing.*
- **p4–p6, Problems 4–6, "with as few pencils as you can."** After three pages of "your two thick pencils," I'm not sure the children have more than two. The problem only works if they can reach for a third and a fourth. *Slowing (adult card / one page word).*
- **p7, Problem 7, "Who can win every time?"** I understood this to mean "Is there a player who always wins if they play well?" A K–1 child may hear it as "Who won the most?" That is a teaching matter, not a page fault. *Minor.*
- **p10, Problem 10, "a map that needs 3 pencils."** I read "needs" as "can't be done with fewer." It's natural enough. *Minor.*
- **p11–p12, extra copies.** They are clearly labeled. No problem.

## Grades 2–5

**What the activity is (confidence: high).** The opening paragraph defines a country (one piece that you can cross without crossing a line), counts the space around shapes in a box as a country, gives the share-a-border rule with the same three square pictures, and says that a dot of color is enough. Children color maps with as few colors as possible, record the number, and explain why fewer colors won't work. The packet runs from edge-to-edge lines and loops (always two colors?), through maps with lines that stop partway, rings around a lake, and wheels with a middle country, to a 3×3 Painter/Spoiler game with 3 and then 4 colors. After that, children explain the two-color fact (P8), draw four and five mutual neighbors (P9), look at a wheel that needs 4 colors with no four mutual neighbors (P10), and play a partner challenge to draw a map that needs 5 (P11). The packet ends with maps on a donut board whose opposite edges are glued (P12–P14).

**Launch of Problem 1.** Same as K–1: color one country of the top-left map, show that a neighbor across a line must differ and that a crossing point doesn't count, and remind them that a dot will do. They color all four maps with two colors.

**Point or surprise (confidence: medium-high).** Maps built from full lines and loops always need just two colors (P1, P2, P8). Odd rings need three, and a wheel around an odd ring needs four (P4, P5). A map can need four colors without having four mutual neighbors (P10). Five mutual neighbors seem impossible on a flat page (P9, P11) but possible on a donut, where a map can need seven colors (P12–P14). The pages make this arc readable. P8 and P10 more or less say it.

**Where I got stuck.**
- **p2, Problem 2, "On your whiteboard."** The page assumes each child has a whiteboard. *Minor (adult card).*
- **p2, Problem 2, "Draw the map you think is hardest to color."** "Hardest" is vague. Does it mean the most countries, or the closest to needing three colors? The real question, "Can you draw a map like this that cannot be colored with two colors?", is clear and can fail, so this only affects what goes in the box. *Minor.*
- **p3, Problem 3, middle-right map.** A short line runs in from the right edge and stops inside a country without meeting another line. For a moment I wondered whether that country counts as two countries. The page 1 definition ("you can get from any spot in it to any other spot without crossing a line") settles it, since you can go around the stub. *Minor.*
- **p4, Problem 4, "Each map is a ring of countries around a lake. The lake is not a country."** The page doesn't say whether the space outside the ring is a country. Page 1 counts "the space around the shapes in a box," and these rings are not in boxes, so I think it isn't. But I had to reason that out, and if a child counts the outside, the answers change (the outside would touch every ring country). P5 and P10 raise the same question, though the answer matters less there. *Slowing.*
- **p6–p7, Problems 6 and 7, "Play games with each player going first. Which player can win every time?"** It's one question after two kinds of games. I can't tell whether the expected answer may differ depending on who goes first. The K–1 packet splits this into two answers (P9 has two rows). *Slowing.*
- **p11, Problem 13, "Write a number in each piece of a country, so that pieces joined across a glued edge get the same number."** This is the first time a country can be in several pieces. P12's maps never split a country across an edge in a visible way (each strip glues to itself). The picture that shows numbered pieces is on the next page (P14). I only understood P13 after turning the page. *Slowing.*
- **p12, Problem 14, second picture.** The tiled picture with a thick square and "1" at its corners took me a couple of minutes to match against the left board. Matching 2, 5, 6, 3, 7, 4 inside the square does confirm it. "Color the board" could refer to either picture. I assume the left one. *Slowing (picture) / minor (which picture).*
- **p10, Problem 12, donut board.** It's well defined in words: the A and B edges, "leave on the right, come back on the left," and countries on opposite edges share a border. A paper tube would help, but that is preparation. *Minor (adult card).*

## Located fixes, most important first

1. **2–5 p4, Problem 4:** change "The lake is not a country." to "The lake and the space outside the ring are not countries." (Optionally add the same clause to P5 and P10: "The space outside the map is not a country.")
2. **2–5 p11, Problem 13:** before the request, add one sentence: "A country can cross a glued edge, so on the flat board it can show up as more than one piece." Or move the current P14 ahead of P13, so that children see numbered pieces before they draw them.
3. **2–5 p6–p7, Problems 6 and 7:** replace "Which player can win every time?" with "Who can win every time when the Painter goes first? When the Spoiler goes first?" (keep "Explain how").
4. **K–1 p3, Problem 3:** change "In the bottom box, draw loops." to "In the bottom box, draw loops that cross each other."
5. **K–1 p4, Problem 4:** add "Use more pencils if you need them." before "Color each map with as few pencils as you can." Also, *for the adult card:* give each child (or pair) at least four thick pencils of different colors from Problem 4 on, and have game partners share one pair of two colors in Problems 7–9.
6. **2–5 p12, Problem 14:** change "Color the board" to "Color the left picture." In the second picture, label the thick square "the board" so that the reader knows which square it is.
7. **2–5 p2, Problem 2:** change "the map you think is hardest to color" to "your best try at a map that needs three colors."
8. *For the adult card:* Problem 2 (2–5) needs a whiteboard and marker per child. For the donut board (P12–P14), a paper rectangle rolled into a tube and then bent shows the gluing. K–1 children will need the problems read aloud.
