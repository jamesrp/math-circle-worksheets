Mathematical purpose
A journey is an ordered finite list of directed traversals of actual edges on a fixed graph. The only
shortening move deletes two adjacent traversals of the same edge in opposite directions. The
graph, start, and finish remain fixed. The empty journey means staying at the initial vertex.
Unique reduction. Every journey ends in the same reduced edge path, regardless of deletion
order. Each deletion shortens by two, so the process terminates. A running stack of oriented edges
gives an invariant normal form; the proof is on page 6. “Same endpoints” is enough to identify an
edge only on these simple printed graphs. Parallel edges would need separate road identities.
Tree theorem. Between two vertices of a tree there is a unique simple path, and every journey
between them reduces to it. In particular, every closed tree journey reduces to the empty journey.
A reduced path on a four-edge ring, by contrast, keeps going in a single direction and may survive
for one or more complete circuits.
Order can survive. Let a be H→A→B→H and b be H→C→D→H on the two-ring map. The journeys
ab and ba have distinct reduced paths. The commutator ab a⁻¹ b⁻¹ has zero net count around each
ring but has no adjacent inverse edges, so it does not disappear. Net traversal counts lose
essential order information.
Topological scope. These elementary edge moves describe endpoint-fixed homotopy of graph
edge paths, with immediate inverse-pair insertions allowed when comparing equivalence classes.
The student shortening activity uses deletions. The open regions inside rings are not filled faces:
paths cannot slide across them, cancel separated inverse steps, trade subjourney order, or move
their basepoint. Paths may revisit vertices and overlap themselves.
Entry points and readiness
K-1: play and replay short journeys with a pawn; an adult records ordered steps. Grades 2-3:
construct long tree walks, classify ring results, and contrast order on two rings. Grades 4-5: prove
unique reduction and find the zero-net-count obstruction. Counting to 12 is useful, not fluent
reading or algebra.
Before-use gate: the exact large route tiles, adult-recording method, mats, and pawn workflow
have not been physically tried with the intended group. A K-1 child must be able to replay an
ordered route and remove an immediate reversal without changing the remaining order. Stay with
the concrete demonstration if that is not yet reliable.

Preparation demonstration and a flexible hour

least 30 mm long. Per table: a two-ring mat about 200 mm wide with H marked, 20 spare tiles,
transparent sleeves, and erasable pens. Use the final four-page packet. An adult may record the
route instead of asking the child to transcribe it.

Exact map identities. Tree edges: AB, BC, BD, DE, DF. Ring: AB, BC, CD, DA. Two-ring map: HA,
AB, BH and HC, CD, DH. The graph is never erased. Every recorded step identifies a particular road
and direction; physical tile arrows without a road identity are insufficient when a junction offers
several choices.
Before-use test. Replay the packet’s A→B→C→B→D example with your pawn and actual tiles.
Remove the middle B→C and C→B tiles, then replay A→B→D. Check that the child sees deletion of a
journey excursion rather than removal of a road. Confirm the route remains readable when tiles
are lifted and that the empty result can be recorded as “stay at A.” This practical trial remains
unperformed here.
Launch, 5-7 minutes. First let everyone travel freely. Show four step tiles in order for
A→B→C→B→D. Point to the two adjacent opposite traversals of BC, remove just those tiles, and
replay the surviving route. Use the same labels in input, process, and output, as on student page 1.
Do not teach the stack algorithm before children explore removal choices.
