# Choosing a route through the atlas

Start with a question you would enjoy investigating yourself. Read its prerequisite gates and worked example, lay out the materials, and try the launch without looking at the hints. Then choose the smallest instance that leaves a real decision for the learners.

The atlas is broader than the current K-5 circle. It includes object-based beginnings, fraction and algebra investigations, calculus problems, and some advanced prerequisites. The entry gate, explanation gate and general-proof gate can differ substantially. None of them is an age assignment.

## A first browsing route

These selections are editorial recommendations from the drafted mathematics, not classroom ratings. They show the atlas's range and supply useful candidates for the next detailed student packets.

## A workable hour

Use one family and one main question. An optional continuation is enough. The existing sixty-minute pattern remains useful: ten minutes handling the materials, five for a launch and rule check, twenty for investigation, five for movement/reset, fifteen for another attempt or extension, and five for sharing. Adapt it to the people in the room.

Do not give a learner the entire facilitator card. It includes answers and adult context. Say the launch, supply only the needed objects/diagram, and keep the hint ladder beside you. A child who finds a convincing small example has participated in mathematics even if the general theorem waits.

For a mixed group, keep a common object when that helps: an overlap window can support matching, length comparison, a piecewise graph and an integral formula at different tables. It is also acceptable to run separate questions when a common object would conceal the mathematical prerequisites. Advanced vocabulary alone is not an extension.

## Returning participants

Consult `prior-use-map.md` before counting a family as new. Record the exact board, starting configuration, rule set, function, parameter choice and explanation encountered in `use-log.csv`, or link from the current circle's existing log. Do not mark a family taught merely because a PDF was printed.

A return should change something mathematical. For example: after constructing a large antichain, find a short upper-bound certificate; after predicting an overlap graph, recover possible window lengths from the graph; after solving a fair random walk, change the transition probabilities and examine which proof step changes. More arithmetic alone is not the intended growth.

The six-year target is about 120 sessions along a child's path. This atlas does not assign a calculus card to an elementary child to satisfy that count. Rich families can support several genuinely different sessions, and gaps in the elementary route should prompt new design rather than relabeling advanced work.

## How to extend the collection

Choose either an unvisited taxonomy subarea or an undeveloped seed. Record the precise adult question first. Build a small world that preserves the relevant objects, actions and question, and label the bridge's strength. Keep hypotheses with the theorem. Solve the example, search for a counterexample, and ask another reviewer to do the same before polishing student pages.

Keep source title and page/theorem locator with the record. Separate a source you read from one merely located. Record proposed age/readiness as a design hypothesis, and revise it from actual sessions. Interesting failed bridges belong in the frontier too: they prevent repeated work and identify the prerequisite that a better representation would have to overcome.
