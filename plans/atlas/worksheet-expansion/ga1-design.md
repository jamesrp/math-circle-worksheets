# GA-01–08: design handoff

September 25, 2026. Eight complete investigation designs, **25 proposed student pages and 55 keyed prompts**, with separate solutions, hints, guide-only extensions, exact figure data and prerequisite lines. These are unpiloted designs. This records the design stage; original atlas cards and the accepted ten trials remain unchanged. Production subsequently added the custom renderer and development previews. See `ga1-maker-qa.md` and `review-ga1-pdf.md` for the current PDF paths, checks and review coverage.

The design follows the root README, the random-ten design brief and assessment/review, and the source-supported teaching notes in `plans/lesson-format-source-notes.md`. In particular, *Math Circle by the Bay*, preface pp. viii–x, supports deep mathematical themes, manipulatives, independent attempts, explanations and flexible pace. Rozhkovskaya's reported copying bottleneck supports keeping records purposeful. Those are source lessons; these particular problems, page divisions, timing estimates and constructions are our editorial choices. No classroom performance is claimed.

## GA-01 — Can a box keep a secret?

The atlas's two-turn witness becomes an investigation in which the learner chooses turn words, challenges another pair, then finds the **shortest word restoring one marked direction while changing the full orientation**. A three-turn conjugate works; a case split rules out one and two turns. The later planar comparison isolates the common-center hypothesis instead of suggesting that all plane rotations commute.

The core needs fixed-room-axis tracking, two marked directions and inverse moves. Signed coordinate notation is optional. Matrix multiplication is an honest gate for the guide-only classification of commuting powers; no Lie-algebra activity is claimed. Woit's §6.1 coordinate matrices were inspected to fix signs, but the shortest-word challenge and different-center example are newly derived.

First-pilot candidate with a physical reset demonstration. The principal facilitation risk is treating axes as box-attached rather than room-fixed. Use direction cycles and two distinct marker shapes. Previous Week 3 composition is acknowledged; this uses rigid rotations and hidden orientation rather than relabeling alone.

## GA-02 — Design a fence that defeats a match

The learner first moves linked markers on an exact six-ramp profile, then chooses six heights to try to defeat equality. Only afterward does the half-turn exchange argument appear. Later cases distinguish continuous ramps from isolated posts, jumps from steep slopes, and a circle from an ordinary interval. These changes make the proof a response to the learner's own failed construction.

The new heights are `0,5,1,4,2,3`. There are exactly three antipodal matching pairs in a half-period, at post coordinates `4/7, 8/5, 7/3`; exact fractions are explicitly optional. The core is comparison plus continuity, not interpolation arithmetic. Lebl's intermediate value lemma and its bisection proof were inspected; our circle deduction is fully written.

First-pilot candidate. Printing must supply the duplicated profile and an exact three-post marker separation; an adult should not have to invent a suitable wavy strip. The proof guarantees an exact equality in the mathematical model, while physical measurements only locate it approximately.

## GA-03 — A cover with a tiny budget

A point-naming opponent creates a real allocation choice. The learner distinguishes a five-point purchase from a rule that must work at every numbered position, then adapts the rule to an arbitrary positive budget. The dense rational enumeration makes the payoff less predictable: all rational points can be covered cheaply even though they lie between every pair of distinct real points.

The core requires fractions, geometric sums and genuine infinite-list reasoning. Paper strips are only an entry. Assigned interval cost is carefully distinguished from union length: overlap does not refund the game's cost, although it may reduce the actual union's length. The interval-cover lower bound is explicitly supplied on the last page. Axler's §2A results 2.4 and 2.14, including the Heine–Borel dependency, were inspected. The worksheet does not pretend a drawing proves the lower bound.

Promising with specific gates. The accepted GA-29 Cantor investigation is cross-linked: zero outer measure does not imply countable. The new content is the adversarial enumeration, arbitrary-budget allocation and dense rational case, not another Cantor-stage computation.

## GA-04 — A root takes a journey

The learner chooses target positions and signed routes, including detours and reversals, then asks whether another continuous root choice could evade the ending reversal. The final rule classifies return by net-turn parity for paths already supplied by a continuous running angle. A triples machine grows the same question into an mth-root return criterion.

The actual core is angle halving plus continuity, with complex division available for a formal proof. The finite checkpoints are not passed off as proof of a no-jump trajectory. Lebl's root-continuation example was inspected; the worksheet establishes the unit-circle obstruction and does not claim unrestricted monodromy.

Promising with specific gates. Two large blank dials and an unwrapped signed-angle record are necessary. The Week 3 cycle analogy is noted, but the new obstruction is a continuous global root choice.

## GA-05 — Can a network hide a high point?

The accepted AP-01 trial already contains an averaging path, equal-gap uniqueness and a ferry preserving values. Accordingly the path here is only a reset, which returning learners can skip. The new edge `a–c` changes the solution from `3,6,9` to `9/2,6,15/2`; learners predict changes before solving. They then design their own finite networks in an attempted hidden-maximum challenge. Maximum propagation proves the obstruction, and subtracting two assignments proves uniqueness and a boundary-sensitivity bound.

The actual gates are averages (including halves), simultaneous consistency and reasoning from a greatest value. It is not a path-travel problem. Hunter's continuous maximum/uniqueness principles were inspected as the source connection; the finite graph proof is supplied independently and does not claim a continuum limit.

First-pilot candidate for average-ready learners. Fixed squares and averaging circles must be visually unmistakable. The correct condition is a fixed boundary in every connected component; disconnectedness by itself is not the obstruction. Uniqueness is stated as **at most one** solution, and no existence theorem is smuggled in through that proof.

## GA-06 — What does a real picture miss?

Learners choose complex z values, find every w, and establish an exhaustive two-branch description. Restricting coordinates to real values collapses the picture to one point, generating the central representational surprise. A branch-switching challenge supplies a continuity obstruction. Finally the invertible coordinates `u=w−iz`, `v=w+iz` turn the deformation into `uv=1` and yield a complete nonzero-parameter description.

Complex arithmetic and factorization are real prerequisites. Two Argand diagrams represent two coordinates; an ordinary planar X is not labeled as the four-real-dimensional variety. Lebl's §6.5 regular-point definition and crossing example were inspected. The optional singularity proof uses the disconnected punctured neighborhood versus a punctured disc; it does not infer singularity merely from a vanishing gradient.

Advanced/specialist. The exact parameterization is the satisfying algebraic stop. A complex-manifold interpretation needs the explicitly stated local-disc gate. No existing Week 1–10 activity supplies these prerequisites.

## GA-07 — Build an angle machine

Learners choose tests to fit an odd cubic. Some tests are redundant, making selection a mathematical decision. A rival polynomial passes all original tests but fails elsewhere, so the supplied cosine addition identity and recurrence earn their role as proof. Polynomial composition then models multiplication of angles.

The core needs unit-circle cosine, signed fractions, algebra and the addition identity. NIST DLMF 18.5.1 and §18.9 were inspected. The candidate-test classification, false fit and exact expansions are derived here. Extending the composition identity outside [−1,1] uses a **supplied polynomial-root bound**, not an unjustified real-cosine representation.

Promising with specific gates. The input-coordinate/angle distinction must stay visible. Finite graphing or measuring is not advertised as the complete investigation; the recurrence proof provides its substance.

## GA-08 — Can a rate law choose a future?

**Calculus remains a core prerequisite.** The first page searches actual differentiable formulas rather than using a proxy game. The learner chooses a waiting time, constructs a piecewise departure, and defends the join with one-sided difference quotients. The advanced continuation classifies every nonnegative differentiable solution on [0,∞): the forever-zero function or a finite waiting time followed by `(t−c)²`.

The classification is not a list of verified examples: nonnegative derivative implies monotonicity; the zero set is all time or an initial closed interval; on the positive interval the derivative of `sqrt(y)` is 1. Continuity at the last zero fixes the integration constant. Every resulting candidate is then checked at the join.

Teschl's local-Lipschitz hypothesis and square-root example were inspected. The source uniqueness theorem has an open-domain hypothesis; the half-line example here is solved directly. If comparing to the theorem, the rate can be extended as `2 sqrt(max(y,0))` on R, still non-Lipschitz at zero. The comparison `y′=y` is settled by the product rule. The negative-sign rate `y′=−2sqrt(y)` is a precise counterexample to “non-Lipschitz always means multiple nonnegative forward futures.”

Advanced/specialist. A complete delayed solution and its join check are the satisfying stop; the all-solutions proof is a stronger later destination. The user-requested gate is prominent in the index line, core gate, page gates and rendering notes. No existing worksheet is substituted for this differential-equation work.
