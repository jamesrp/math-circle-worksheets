# Ten random atlas entries: worksheet trial

The organizer's September 25 feedback changes the acceptance test: an interesting adult topic and a correct small example are not enough. The current Week 1 packets are the reference for an investigation that develops through concrete actions, a surprising obstruction or connection, and a worthwhile explanation. In particular, a preliminary activity below the real prerequisites must not be advertised as a satisfying version of the deeper problem. The organizer explicitly identifies calculus as essential to a satisfying GA-08 investigation.

## Sampling

`sample.json` records one uniform sample without replacement from all 90 family records, sorted by ID, using a once-drawn 64-bit seed and Python's seeded sampler. No redraw, prerequisite filter, or subject balancing was applied. The random draw contains no AD-family record; this is retained rather than corrected after seeing the sample.

Draw order: GA-26, GA-29, GA-11, AP-26, AP-23, AP-21, GA-25, AP-07, AP-01, AP-29. The sample and source hashes permit reproduction. These are ten family entries, with instances redesigned where needed; the original atlas files remain unchanged.

## What a strong worksheet must provide

1. A learner can do something meaningful immediately after a short rule demonstration. For an advanced sheet this can be a genuine calculus or algebra choice, rather than an unrelated manipulative warm-up.
2. The learner chooses examples, constructions, moves, parameters or explanations. Filling the adult's calculation table is not the central activity.
3. Experiments lead to a mathematical payoff: an obstruction, optimal construction, complete classification, invariant, surprising representation, or rule change that breaks a conjecture.
4. The first page does not reveal the key trick. Later reference cards, representations and hints are handed out deliberately; facilitator solutions stay separate.
5. Drawings, boards and recording spaces do actual work. The worksheet supplies the materials it depends on, with exact legal moves and unambiguous diagrams.
6. Every printed prompt has a checked facilitator response. Distinguish empirical observation from proof, and a finite model from its continuous or infinite continuation.
7. The prerequisite label describes the satisfying core. A lower-level entry does not erase a calculus, algebra or infinite-reasoning requirement. Extensions have their own gates.
8. Assess the ten outcomes candidly. A random sample is a test of the atlas, not a reason to call every result equally strong. Actual classroom performance remains unknown.

## Print design

Aim for two to four student pages per investigation, handed out one at a time. Preserve generous drawing and working space, readable type, a small number of purposeful prompts, and reusable pieces. There is no requirement that every investigation take exactly one hour or that every learner reach the last page.

Final print products will be a combined student book and a separate facilitator guide in the year-2 `combined/` directory. Plans, mathematical checks and diagram data stay here; builders go in year-2 `source/atlas-random-ten/`; raster previews and assembly intermediates go in `tmp/pdfs/atlas-random-ten/`. These random trials are not assigned to new weekly sessions or recorded as taught.

## Production and review

Three subagents develop disjoint groups. The editor compares their drafts against the Week 1 standard, then other agents review rules, new mathematics and diagram data before production. After rendering, independent page review checks the actual student experience as well as correctness: can the reader identify the task, use the supplied figures, make decisions, and work without encountering an accidental answer? Repair findings before release and retain a concise assessment of each trial's strengths and limits.
