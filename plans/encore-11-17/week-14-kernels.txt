An ear is a triangle with two polygon boundary sides. The triangle-adjacency graph of a triangulated convex n-gon has n-2 vertices and n-3 edges and is connected, hence a tree. For n≥4 it has at least two leaves, giving at least two ears. No upper bound of two is asserted: the central-triangle hexagon has three ears. Deleting an ear and its middle corner leaves a smaller triangulated convex polygon. Ear removal gives a new structural direction beyond counts and flip routes.

Every triangulation of a convex polygon admits a three-coloring of its corners in which every triangle has all three labels. Start with one triangle, then propagate across the adjacency tree; a neighboring triangle has exactly one unassigned opposite corner when reached by peeling/insertion. The coloring is unique up to permuting labels. Choosing the smallest color class covers every triangle and uses at most floor(n/3) corners. One corner need not cover every triangle: the central-triangle hexagon needs two, while a fan needs one. This is a combinatorial corner cover, not a claim about physical visibility in a convex room.

A regular labeled hexagon has 14 triangulations. Geometric rotation symmetry is a new condition; rotating does not identify different diagonal sets. Exactly 6 have half-turn symmetry, 2 have one-third-turn symmetry, and none have one-sixth-turn symmetry. A half-turn invariant triangulation must have one diameter: three diagonals in pairs leave an invariant diagonal, and two diameters would cross. Choose one of 3 diameters and one of 2 triangulations on one quadrilateral; the other is forced. One-third turns allow the two alternating central triangles. This is not merely recounting all hexagon fillings.
