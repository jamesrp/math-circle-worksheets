Week 11: Chip firing: escapes, avalanches and lost histories

Mathematical kernels

1. Without a sink, conservation can prevent stabilization or produce cycles at the stable capacity, although another configuration with the same total stabilizes. On a closed three-vertex triangle, every degree is 2 and capacity is 3. Four chips cannot stabilize. With three chips, (3,0,0) stabilizes to (1,1,1), but (2,1,0) follows ABC back to itself: (0,2,1), (1,0,2), (2,1,0). A sink in another disconnected component would not rescue this graph. This changes an assumption rather than repeating sink-board order tests.

2. A one-chip avalanche on the four-cycle S-A-B-C-S is a local perturbation, distinct from repeated addition orbits. Every stable state has entries 0 or 1. From (1,1,1), adding at A makes A,B,C fire once and ends (1,1,0); adding at B makes B fire twice and A,C once, ending (1,0,1). A single added chip can thus trigger more firings than vertices. From a stable state, adding at an empty vertex makes no firing. Cataloguing firing patterns from stable states discovers threshold propagation, not just final addition-state cycles.

3. Finishes erase history, even when the exact number of firings is known. On the sink triangle, to finish at (1,1) after exactly four firings the only starts are (0,6),(3,3),(6,0), and there are eight legal words in total. For counts (a,b) with a+b=4, the start must be (1+2a-b,1+2b-a); nonnegativity leaves a=1,2,3, and legality further limits order. Four words occur from (3,3), two from each other start. Reverse moves require enough chips at both recipients, including the sink; all starts begin with an empty sink. This is inverse firing-history reconstruction, not inversion of the base one-chip addition map.
