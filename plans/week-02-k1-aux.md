## Prerequisites and access

No independent reading, written arithmetic, or formal proof. An adult reads the numbered task and demonstrates. Lamps require matching a target picture, recognizing the two ends of a road, and adding or erasing a dot. The one-light walks add route following. Room work starts with drawing a closed shape and identifying an enclosed region; counting to about seven is useful but can be done aloud with the parent. Mark one dot per region before counting so writing numerals is unnecessary. Recognizing corners is introduced by finger tracing.

Children may point or dictate while the adult draws. Imperfectly straight strokes are fine when the intended wall is clear. Keep shapes large. Follow mathematical readiness rather than grade labels; the older children may also enjoy invention pages if wanted.

## A flexible hour

| Time | Action | Adult support |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 min | Draw and erase freely with the materials. | Let children try the action before introducing a rule. |
| 5-10 | Whole-group lamp launch. | Show OFF/OFF and ON/OFF road moves; children help predict and change both ends. |
| 10-20 | Square targets and partner puzzles, pp. 1-2. | One successful attempt per task may be enough. |
| 20-30 | Single-light walks, p. 3; or switch sooner. | Show that this task starts with one ON lamp deliberately. |
| 30-35 | Movement break. | Stand, stretch, move safely, then settle with the next drawing. |
| 35-50 | One-wall houses and two-wall rooms, pp. 10-11. | Draw one wall, put a dot in each room, then compare. Use p. 9 first if closed shapes need practice. |
| 50-55 | Share one discovery per child. | Invite a demonstration or picture, not a speech or proof. |
| 55-60 | Tidy. | Save one interesting invention and note actual use. |

These blocks sum to 60 minutes. They are pacing guidance, not a demand to complete the default five-page selection. A child who spends the drawing block making four interesting one-wall houses has done worthwhile work.

## Launch and management scripts

**Common launch:** “A dot means ON. An empty lamp is OFF. Choose a road. Change BOTH lamps at its ends: erase a dot, or add one. Leave the other lamps alone.” Demonstrate first with two empty endpoints, then with one ON and one OFF. Finishing both endpoints completes the move. No whole-group written record is needed.

**K-1 trio roles:** one child chooses the road, one changes one end, one changes the other end. Rotate the three roles every turn. During partner work, the parent partners with the third child, or all three cooperate using these roles. Nobody is assigned an idle spectator or checker role.

**Room launch:** “This square is one room. Draw one straight wall from the outside edge to the outside edge. Put one dot inside each room.” Trace the boundaries with a finger. A valid full wall crosses the interior, ends on the outside boundary, and is not drawn along an existing wall. Later, show dot/X neighbor marking separately, after identifying rooms is secure.

If lamps become a test of erasing stamina, use a tablet, have the adult record, or switch to drawing. If a state becomes confused, reset without blame. If a child wants to decorate or invent, integrate a concrete question: “Where are its corners?” or “Can a friend find every room?” Stop at success rather than adding another obstacle.

## Checked mathematical substance

For lamps, every printed target intended to be possible can be reached with the printed roads. The one-light walking solutions maintain exactly one ON lamp after every complete move. Each target is reset to its stated starting picture; P8 intentionally changes the start from one ON to all OFF. P11 is impossible even if the child temporarily makes extra ON lamps, because each separate island preserves the parity of its number of ON lamps. Adding road 2-5 in P12 allows the single light to move by roads 1-2 and 2-5. Invention problems are guaranteed solvable because every move is its own inverse.

For rooms, one full interior wall makes two regions. Two distinct full walls make three or four regions. Three distinct full walls can make four, five, six or seven. The printed P20 maps have four, six and seven, in order. Full-line arrangements admit two marks with different marks across every shared wall. The special T map has three mutually adjacent rooms, so needs three marks. A diagonal through opposite square corners yields two triangles; a wall between opposite sides away from corners yields two quadrilaterals; a cut across a corner yields a triangle and a pentagon. The guide's optional upper bound for three full walls is adult background, not an expectation for K-1.

## Sources, classroom evidence, and limits

Read locally: Natasha Rozhkovskaya (name verified in the EPUB metadata), [*Math Circles for Elementary School Students*](../external-resources/msri-math-circle-books/Math%20Circles%20for%20Elementary%20School%20Students.epub), Lesson 7, “At the lesson,” item 2 (EPUB `OEBPS/part0017.xhtml`), and Lesson 8, “At the lesson,” item 2 (`OEBPS/part0018.xhtml`). Lesson 7 reports children's energetic work making many polygons with K'nex, including a giant rectangle. Giving children freedom to invent and compare is a lesson drawn from that account; substituting drawing and adding the room investigations are new adaptations, not activities or outcomes claimed for the source. Lesson 8 reports slow number writing and different coloring speeds splitting the group. The minimal writing burden and flexible page selection here are our design response to that account.

Local evidence: the organizer's first-year experience as summarized in [README](../README.md) emphasizes concrete work and time to learn by doing. The [Week 1 classroom review](week-01-classroom-review.md) supports explicit next actions and multiple concrete examples before abstraction. The [prior-year materials](../lowell-math-circle-year-1/lowell-math-circle/) remain reference material; these particular lamp and room pages are not claimed as previously tested activities.

The idea that larger maps, invention, partner play, and a drawing theme will improve engagement is a **design hypothesis**. All new pages must be distinguished from observed classroom success.

## Use log and six-year progression

After the meeting, enter only the pages, graph targets, invented boards, and room maps children actually tried in the [session use log](fall-k-5-year-a-use-log.md). Record what they did, found unclear, wanted to continue, and understood unaided. Distinguish those observations from an explanation of why they happened. Retain unused pages as reserve rather than marking them taught.

These activities relate to later work on graph routes, connectedness, reversible operations, parity, polygon geometry, planar regions, and coloring. Returning children should meet a new question or representation: e.g. compare route choices, construct a target for a friend, investigate how one wall changes a town, or explain a dot/X coloring. Reusing the same targets alone does not constitute progression.
