Week 53: Connect every place for the least cost

Mathematical kernels

1. Let a finite connected undirected graph describe the available whole links between fixed places, with a strictly positive purchase cost on each link. Purchase a subset that allows travel between every pair of places, paying for each purchased link once. Every minimum-total-cost connected purchase is a spanning tree: any cycle permits removal of a positive-cost link without disconnecting the network. A tree on n places has n−1 links, but using n−1 links alone guarantees neither connection nor minimum cost. The quantity optimized is the total price of the purchased network, not the length of a particular journey or the number of walk steps. Extra junctions or unprinted links are not available.

2. The cut exchange principle explains a safe cheap choice. If already chosen links form a forest contained in some cheapest spanning tree, choose a cheapest available link joining two different components. A cut separating one of those components respects all chosen links. Some cheapest tree containing the chosen forest can be made to contain the new link: add it, find the resulting loop, remove an at-least-as-expensive link crossing that cut. Thus repeatedly choosing the cheapest link that joins separate components (Kruskal) is optimal. Students should have time to invent approaches before any algorithm is named or prescribed. A local claim such as “buy the cheapest n−1 links” can fail by buying a loop and omitting a place; a four-place example AB=1, BC=2, AC=3, CD=4, AD=5 has unique optimum {AB,BC,CD}, cost 7.

3. Exchange certificates support substantial upper work. Adding an unused link to a tree creates one loop; replacing a more expensive loop link lowers total cost while preserving connection. An edge strictly more expensive than every other edge of a cycle occurs in no cheapest tree. A uniquely cheapest link across a cut occurs in every cheapest tree; a tied cheapest link is safe for some compatible optimum, not automatically compulsory. Distinct prices guarantee a unique cheapest tree; ties may or may not yield several. For a fixed candidate tree, it is cheapest exactly when every unused link costs at least as much as the largest price on the tree path between its endpoints. The reverse direction can be proved by replacing links of any competing tree until it becomes the candidate without increasing cost. Small actual networks make these arguments checkable with removable links; no abstract matroid vocabulary is needed.

Suggested emphasis by level

K–1 supported entry only: Adult reads and helps pay small dot prices; children select links and trace whether every pictured place is reachable; compare tangible coin piles on a three/four-place map. No independent symbolic greedy proof or separate shallow K–1 packet is implied.

Grades 2–3: Select removable links on small maps, pay small whole-number prices, and optimize the total while retaining all places; read labels/price numerals or dots and add/count totals around 10–20, with adult recording if needed.

Grades 4–5 (ready third graders): Competing designs, profitable and blocked exchanges, tied optima, and cut/loop certificates; follow a path in one unchanged chosen network, retain the purchase record, and compare integer prices. General optimality/uniqueness arguments are readiness-dependent continuations.

Materials

Five kits for two K–1 pairs, two 2–3 pairs, and the upper trio: each has 30 same-value counters about 12–15 mm across, ten removable purchased-link markers, one pawn, a dry-erase sleeve or tracing sheet, marker, and blank network-record copy. Whole-group demonstration adds another 15 counters, four link markers and a large three-place map. Price dots on young-entry maps are countable; price numerals can replace them later. Chosen paths have one clear on/off marker or trace; coins may be placed in a payment tray and counted after a candidate is complete, avoiding a simultaneous running ledger. Returning a link refunds its exact marked price. The available-link price sum on a fully manipulable map must be at most 30, unless a larger coin supply is explicitly supplied. Use approximately three to seven places and five to ten available links; if a later map has more links, its kit needs that many purchase markers. Printed lines are permissions and prices, not physical string lengths. Only endpoints marked as places are junctions, including at crossings. Buying part of a link is not legal.

Keep labels and price marks close to their links, leave room to mark selected links, and provide a large enough working map for the pawn/fingers and removable markers. Do not rely on drawing scale to encode cost. The whole group first handles the counters and markers, then sees one legal purchase and a trace along bought links; this demonstrates payment and connection without revealing an optimal strategy. If the recording convention changes from dot-price cards to numbered links, include a small non-task input → purchased links/payment → connected record visual before first use. Adults read/record and check payment while children choose links. Allow 10–15 minutes to print/organize reusable pair kits; later reset should take under ten minutes. Rich optimization should not be displaced by a full unweighted tree catalog, tree-walk code, or source-map transcription. The coordinator will give combined-PDF output routing separately. Physical rehearsal and classroom piloting remain unperformed.
