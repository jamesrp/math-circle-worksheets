Week 54: Partitions and reversible rebuilding

Mathematical kernels

1. A partition of a nonnegative integer n is an unordered collection of positive whole-number parts adding to n; rearranging identical parts creates no new partition. For n=0 the empty collection is the sole partition. A left-aligned diagram of rows in nonincreasing length records a partition without counting rearrangements. Exchanging rows and columns is an involution that preserves n and exchanges the largest part with the number of parts. Consequently partitions of n into at most k parts are equinumerous with partitions of n whose largest part is at most k. For example (4,2,1) becomes (3,2,1,1), and applying the exchange again restores (4,2,1). This is a physical row/column correspondence, with no need for formulas for the unrestricted partition function. The complete partition counts for n=1,...,8 are 1,2,3,5,7,11,15,22; the writer should choose manageable totals and may pool a catalog rather than require every child to copy it.

2. For each n, partitions with only odd parts and partitions with no repeated part have equal counts. The reversible correspondence is concrete: repeatedly join two equal parts q,q into one part 2q until every size is different; in the other direction repeatedly split each even part into two equal halves until every part is odd. Total size stays fixed. Every positive part has a unique form 2^j u with u odd. For each odd u, its multiplicity is expressed as a sum of distinct powers of two, so the final parts are precisely 2^j u for the powers used. This proves independence of merge order, distinctness, and both inverse identities. It is the d=2 case of Glaisher's bijection, proving Euler's odd/distinct partition theorem. Example: (3,3,1,1,1) becomes (6,2,1); splitting 6 and 2 restores the three original sizes with their multiplicities. For n=5,...,10 the odd/distinct counts are 3,4,5,6,8,10. Experiments suggest equal counts; reversible rebuilding explains the equality for every n. Do not confuse "only odd" with "odd AND distinct", or compare ordered compositions. Binary notation is optional adult language; repeated physical pairing and halving carry the proof.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1: no standalone packet recommended; an adult-supported entry can rearrange a small fixed number of unit pieces into groups without claiming independent access to the bijection. Grades 2–3: build and distinguish unordered partitions, then explore odd-only and different-size collections through tangible pairing and halving. Grades 4–5: classify small catalogs, use the row/column correspondence, and investigate whether rebuilding always reverses and whether different legal merge orders change the result.

Materials

For each pair: 24 identical unit squares or snap cubes, preferably about 10–15 mm across; a roughly 42×30 cm table work area; two trays or paper lanes labeled before and after; pencil, eraser and blank paper or whiteboard. Three active pairs require 72 units, with the upper table's third child rotating builder/checker or partnering with the mathematician. Grouping strips and joining/splitting happen beside the worksheet, so printed pictures are records rather than exact-fit mats. Keep all working totals at most 24 unless the writer explicitly increases the kit; use the same total before and after and never discard a "leftover" unit. Parts are unlabeled and positive: color, position and order are not additional distinctions. Introduce the row/column record and rebuilding action with a small worked visual before their first use (input, one meaningful intermediate action, output), using an instance outside the main enumeration. Children should handle pieces before the brief common legal-attempt demonstration. Adult reading/recording is compatible with children making mathematical choices; binary numeral conversion is not a prerequisite. Physical fit, rule retention and classroom pacing remain untested.
