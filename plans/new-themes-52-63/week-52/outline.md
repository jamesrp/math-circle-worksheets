Week 52: Hinged frames and braces

Mathematical kernels

1. A nondegenerate triangle made from three fixed-length bars with freely turning pin joints cannot change shape continuously in the plane: fixing one side places the third vertex at the intersection of two circles, a discrete set. A four-sided frame can shear. Adding a fixed-length diagonal to a square divides it into two fixed triangles and prevents this planar shape change. The scientific comparison is between turning joints and changing bar lengths; sliding the whole frame or turning it on the table is not a shape change. This is the genuine concrete K–5 entry.

2. For a complete rectangular grid with m rows and n columns of square cells, all side bars fixed in length, planar freely turning joints, no missing bars or holes, and braces that are diagonals of individual cells, form a second graph with m row vertices and n column vertices. A brace in row i, column j gives the link Ri–Cj. The grid is infinitesimally rigid exactly when this bracing graph is connected (Bolker–Crapo). In the initial square grid a first-order displacement has one sideways slip per row and one vertical slip per column; a brace equates the corresponding signed turns. Connectedness forces all turns to be one whole-frame rotation. k connected components leave k−1 nontrivial first-order modes. This statement concerns ideal bars and first-order motion, not the apparent stiffness of cardboard.

3. In this complete planar grid setting, connectedness also prevents finite continuous shape change; disconnected components have an actual flex by turning all row/column bar directions in one component together. This finite claim is justified separately, not inferred from an arbitrary first-order flex. The fewest braces are m+n−1: a connected graph on m+n vertices needs that many links, and a tree in the row/column graph supplies them. Counts alone and touching every row and column are insufficient. A 2-by-2 grid needs three braces; the two opposite braced cells leave two components. A 2-by-3 grid needs four. In a 3-by-3 grid, bracing all four cells of the upper-left 2-by-2 block plus the bottom-right cell uses five braces and touches every row/column but still leaves two components. Rich continuations concern whether a brace helps, is redundant, or is essential, and why a design works beyond a hand test. Full case enumeration is optional adult evidence, not the activity's destination.

Suggested emphasis by level

K–1: Handle preassembled triangles and quadrilaterals, compare shape-changing frames, and make a frame hold its shape with a removable diagonal; adult reads, no arithmetic beyond counting a few bars.

Grades 2–3: Concrete brace placement and testing on small complete grids; count small brace sets and distinguish where a brace acts; the row/column graph is an optional next representation for children who can retain both labels.

Grades 4–5 (ready third graders): Use the physical model to investigate minimum braces, redundant constraints, and connected components; a row/column-link graph needs an explicit non-task visual bridge before use; proof uses propagation of fixed turns and joining separate groups, without matrices or calculus.

Materials

Five pair/trio kits for the fixed tables. Two K–1 kits each have a preassembled equilateral triangle (three side bars/three joints), a separate square (four side bars/four joints), and one removable rigid diagonal. Two 2–3 kits each have a preassembled 2-by-2-cell grid (12 side bars/nine joints) and four removable diagonals. One 4–5 kit has a preassembled 2-by-3-cell grid (17 side bars/12 joints) and six removable diagonals; the three children rotate builder, tester and recorder. A spare 2-by-3 kit lets the upper table compare two designs simultaneously. A separate square and diagonal serve the common demonstration. Total: 76 side bars, 23 diagonal bars, 60 pin joints, plus a few spare pins. Removable diagonals use existing corner joints, not new joints at crossings. An optional 3-by-3 model is an additional 24 side bars, 16 joints and nine diagonals; diagrams can support its reasoning if this extra kit is unavailable.

Use stiff prepunched strips or a suitable reusable hinged-bar kit. Proposed hole-center distances: sides 60 mm, square diagonals 60√2 mm (about 84.85 mm); width about 12–15 mm, with at least 8 mm beyond each hole. These are hole-center dimensions, not total strip lengths. Holes must permit rotation without appreciable sliding. Braces are rigid bars, not string, elastic, tape that locks an angle, or strips attached at arbitrary positions. Frames stay flat on the table; lifting a joint introduces a different three-dimensional problem. Adult assembles, cuts/punches, and adds/removes pins as needed; children choose brace locations and gently test. Model size is about 180 by 120 mm for the 2-by-3 grid, with strip ends beyond it; use separate table space rather than promising it will fit a printed outline. Printed diagrams are recording maps unless explicitly declared full size. Use square cells with equal x/y scale.

Provide pencils, two colors of markers, a few removable row/column label tabs, and blank recording maps. Keep the main frame and its brace record recoverable. No compulsory joint-by-joint construction on student pages. The whole group first handles a triangle and square; the adult shows a legal gentle planar push and how to attach one diagonal, without supplying the general criterion. There is genuine young entry; do not duplicate it merely to fill standard grade-band page quotas. The coordinator will give output routing separately. First fabrication may take 45–60 minutes; reusable preassembled kits should need about 10–15 minutes to check/reset before later sessions. Pin friction, strip bending, diagonal fit, and practical setup time remain physically untested and require a rehearsal before classroom use.
