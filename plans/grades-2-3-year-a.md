# Grades 2–3 math circle: a first 20-session plan

Draft for discussion, September 10, 2026. Ten fall meetings and ten possible summer meetings, about one hour each. This proposes the experiences and their sequence; individual puzzles, handouts, and complete facilitator guides come after review.

The [aligned K–5 fall plan](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/fall-k-5-year-a.md) places K–1 and 4–5 versions alongside these ten fall activities, with shared materials and level-specific guidance.

Build the year around what worked: shape subtraction, ciphers, pattern blocks, strategy games, logic puzzles, and billiards. Introduce new mathematics through similar kinds of play. Fibonacci numbers, triangular numbers, and binomial coefficients were less engaging for most of last year's group, so they are not main topics here. Counting questions can remain optional extensions for interested children.

## What the sources suggest

I reviewed the text of all eleven Lowell fall handouts, the shape-operation TeX files, selected source diagrams, relevant JRMF guides, selected Math for Love and Early Family Math activities, and introductory/teaching passages from the three MSRI books. This is a targeted first review, not a complete reading of the collection. The saved fall handouts also do not tell us everything taught during the full school year.

- **Children learning from each other is part of the mathematics.** *Math Circle by the Bay*, preface pp. vii–x (PDF pp. 8–11), emphasizes interaction, connected themes, manipulatives, revisiting explanations, and making mistakes without embarrassment. Proposed application: pairs compare approaches and explain a move or construction at every meeting.
- **The physical version matters.** Rozhkovskaya's *Math Circles for Elementary School Students*, Lesson 12, “At the lesson,” describes difficulty with imagined mirror arrangements and a later session using actual mirrors. Lesson 2 notes that some capable participants were not fluent readers. Proposed application: build, move, or draw first; read prompts aloud and provide visual alternatives to text ciphers.
- **Leave the discovery to the children.** JRMF's leader guides repeatedly advise short introductions, exploration, and questions about the choices a child has already made. Proposed application: demonstrate the rules, then circulate; keep solution strategies in facilitator notes.
- **Accessible starts need not exhaust the activity.** JRMF's beginner guides provide gentler entry points, and Math for Love's remote-curriculum introduction encourages play with materials outside formal lessons. Proposed application: offer a small starting challenge and a deeper branch using the same kit.

The 60-minute structure and sequence below are my recommendations, informed especially by the organizer's experience. They are not a prescribed curriculum from any source.

## A meeting rhythm to try

| Minutes | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0–10 | Arrive, handle the materials, build or play freely; offer an optional invitation. |
| 10–15 | Demonstrate one challenge and clarify its rules. |
| 15–35 | Children explore, usually in pairs. The adult asks questions and offers small hints. |
| 35–40 | Brief stretch or show-and-tell; introduce a new question if useful. |
| 40–55 | Continue, try an extension, or invent a related puzzle. |
| 55–60 | Share one discovery or question and tidy up. |

Keep this flexible when children are absorbed. The extension is an alternative path, not an assignment everyone must reach. Most sessions require little reading and counting only small quantities. Work in pairs without requiring partners to move at the same speed; switch who handles the pieces. An assistant, when available, can help children interpret rules without taking over the solution.

## Fall: familiar pleasures, new problems

| Week | Main activity | What children actually do and explore |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Pattern blocks: same outline, different pieces** | After free building, make two copies of a new silhouette using different block combinations. Then try JRMF's stronger rule: the two copies cannot use any of the same block colors. Explore how different parts can make the same whole. |
| 2 | **Shape subtraction and missing drawings** | Combine and remove line segments in new pictures. Solve a missing-picture puzzle, then make one for a partner. Move from carrying out an operation to finding an unknown and checking the result. |
| 3 | **Make a secret alphabet** | Encode and exchange short messages using a one-to-one symbol key. Discover what repeated symbols reveal. Children who prefer pictures can encode a sequence of objects or actions with a shared picture key. |
| 4 | **Break a cipher** | Use a movable alphabet strip or wheel to investigate shift ciphers. Decode new messages with a partial clue, then write a message a partner can solve. Discuss which clues actually helped. |
| 5 | **Skyscrapers** | Build a small city with cube towers. Each row and column uses each height once; edge clues say how many towers are visible. Get down to table height to check! Distinguish a placement that is possible from one that must be true. |
| 6 | **Crack the color code** | One child hides three red/blue counters; a partner guesses. Feedback gives only the number of positions correct. Keep guesses visible and choose the next experiment from the evidence. This is deduction through play, with no required enumeration of all codes. |
| 7 | **A new take-away game** | Take turns removing 1, 3, or 4 counters; taking the last wins. Play short games, then set up positions a partner cannot escape. This changes the available moves from last year's consecutive-number versions. |
| 8 | **Rook's Move** | Share one token on a grid. Move it right or down any positive distance; reaching the goal corner wins. Compare starting squares and investigate how to leave an opponent an awkward position. Connect spatial strategy to last week's game. |
| 9 | **Billiards: build a table for a target** | Launch at 45 degrees from the bottom-left corner and trace reflections. Instead of another list of tables to calculate, choose or design a rectangle that sends the ball to a specified corner. Test and trade designs. |
| 10 | **Puzzle workshop and fall finale** | Choose shape operations, codes, or games. Make a new challenge, test it with a partner, revise an ambiguous rule or unhelpful clue, and share it. This is also the space to continue an unfinished favorite. |

### Entry points, extensions, and preparation

**1. Pattern blocks.** Use blocks and silhouettes matched to their actual size. Start with any two constructions of one outline; add the disjoint-color rule only when useful. Extend by banning a favorite piece or finding a construction with fewer pieces. Source: JRMF [Changing Colors][changing], pp. 3–6 and task bank pp. 7–14. Use new outlines, not the Lowell hexagon and large-triangle tasks. Preparation: moderate initially, then reusable.

**2. Shape operations.** Use drawings, tracing paper or transparent overlays, and erasable markers. Start with disjoint strokes, then introduce overlaps and ask whether a missing picture must be unique. The proposed convention is to count coincident strokes once: overlay to add; copy the combined drawing and erase specified segments to subtract. Sources: the organizer's [shape-operation files][shapes] and Lowell Handout 11, problem 11.3. New drawings and inverse questions are our adaptation. Preparation: moderate.

**3–4. Ciphers.** Use new keys, messages, and clues. Start with a complete key in week 3 and partial information in week 4. Reading and alphabet fluency matter here; offer picture keys or a reading partner. Extend by comparing repeated-letter patterns or composing two shifts. Source: [Rozhkovskaya][elementary], Lesson 2, “Secret code,” problems 2.2–2.8 and its teaching notes. Confirm which cipher types were used last year before choosing the exact challenges. Preparation: moderate, with decoding checked in advance.

**5. Skyscrapers.** Start with one row of heights 1, 2, 3, then a 3×3 grid. A full grid uses nine towers and eighteen snap cubes per pair. Extend with a new 3×3 puzzle or by making clues for a completed city; 4×4 is optional. Source: JRMF [Skyscrapers Teaching Guide][skyscrapers], pp. 1–2, 5–8, 13–14. Preparation: moderate; grids and towers are reusable.

**6. Color code.** Use two colors of counters, a small screen, and rows for recording guesses. Begin with two positions if needed; let children learn the feedback rule before imposing a guess limit. Extend by changing just one position between guesses and explaining what the change in feedback tells us. Source: JRMF [Crack the Code][code], pp. 2–4, adapted to physical pieces and gentler starting rules. Preparation: low.

**7. Take-away.** Use about twenty counters per pair and a visible card showing the legal moves. Start with small piles and compare two candidate moves. Extend by marking winning/losing positions with colored counters or designing another move set. Source: JRMF [Countdown Teaching Guide][countdown], pp. 1–4 for the teaching approach and arbitrary move sets, and p. 8, challenge 4 for the 1, 3, 4 game. The small-pile progression is our adaptation. Preparation: low. No requirement to find a general formula.

**8. Rook's Move.** Use a hand-drawn 4×4 grid and one token; chess knowledge is unnecessary. Start close to the goal, then move farther away. Extend by changing the starting square or trying King's Move. Source: JRMF [Rook's Move][rook], pp. 3–8. Preparation: low.

**9. Billiards.** Use large graph paper, a straightedge, and a token to follow the path. Begin with short paths on rectangles not assigned in the saved handouts, such as width 3/height 6 and its transpose; a 3-by-9 table offers another endpoint. Then let children choose the target. Sources: [JRMF Billiards Geometry][billiards], PDF p. 2; Lowell Handouts 10–11. The inverse design task is our adaptation. Preparation: low. Save unfolding for summer.

**10. Workshop.** Reuse two or three kits from the quarter. Offer concrete starting tasks: make a missing-shape puzzle, a cipher with one useful clue, or a game position with a winning move. Children explain how they checked their puzzle; an adult can scribe. Source inspiration: [Rozhkovskaya][elementary], M.4, “Make your own problem”; the workshop format is our proposal. Preparation: low.

## Summer: a fresh entry point and a wider range of experiments

Summer must work for children who missed fall. Reintroduce each activity's rules; returning children can take a new challenge or help explain a technique without revealing the solution.

| Week | Main activity | What children actually do and explore |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 11 | **Pentomino puzzle makers** | Handle the pieces, cover small silhouettes with two pieces, then build and trace a silhouette for a partner. Explore turning, flipping, fitting, and different solutions to the same outline. |
| 12 | **Mirrors and folded shapes** | Explore a mirror, then predict what a cut in folded paper will make. Reverse the question: how should we fold and cut to produce a target? Return to symmetry through new tools and constructions. |
| 13 | **Secret pictures and robot instructions** | Follow arrow instructions on a grid to reveal a picture. Partners encode their own drawings and debug instructions that produce the wrong image. Explore precise instructions and reversing a process. |
| 14 | **A rolling cube has a memory** | Roll a marked cube along short grid routes. Compare “right, then up” with “up, then right”: same destination, different orientation. Create a route that reaches a target square with a chosen face on top. |
| 15 | **Map coloring** | Color small invented maps so neighbors differ. Try fewer colors, explain why a particular map needs at least three, then design maps for one another. Investigate local constraints and convincing explanations. |
| 16 | **Frogs and toads swap places** | Move two groups of counters past one another along a row with one empty space. Start small, undo dead ends, and find a repeatable method. Explore planning, useful intermediate arrangements, and simplifying a problem. |
| 17 | **Subtract sticks, change the triangles** | Build a triangle arrangement with sticks, then remove sticks to meet a target. Pay attention to large triangles as well as small ones. Invent a removal challenge and verify that every remaining stick belongs to a triangle. |
| 18 | **Can this board be tiled?** | Cover small boards with dominoes. Remove squares and sort boards into “we can tile it,” “still investigating,” and “we can explain why not.” Introduce checkerboard coloring when it helps explain an obstruction. |
| 19 | **Billiards without bouncing** | Trace a short billiard route, then place reflected copies of the table beside it so the route becomes straight. Fold or trace it back to the original table. Use the construction to explain a new target-table design. |
| 20 | **A small math festival made by the children** | In pairs, create a new challenge using a favorite summer kit, solve it, give it to another pair, and improve it. Share a method or a surprising result. This can also extend a topic that deserves another meeting. |

### Entry points, extensions, and preparation

**11. Pentominoes.** Use a set per pair and small silhouettes. Begin with two-piece puzzles; offer more pieces or another solution as extensions. Finding all twelve pentominoes is optional for an interested child, not the session's goal. Source: JRMF [Pentominoes Beginner Guide][pentominoes], pp. 3–4, task bank pp. 8–13. Preparation: moderate once, then reusable.

**12. Mirrors and folds.** Use small mirrors, paper, and scissors. Start with one fold; extend to two perpendicular folds or a target shape with specified symmetry. Source: Early Family Math [Cutting Symmetric Shapes][folding], p. 1; [Rozhkovskaya][elementary], Lesson 12, especially problems 12.4–12.8 and teaching notes. Two-mirror experiments are an optional alternative if the children prefer them to cutting. Preparation: low once materials are available.

**13. Picture instructions.** Use large square grids and arrow cards; reading words is unnecessary. Start with a short route using horizontal and vertical steps. Extend by finding an error or writing instructions to retrace a route. Source: [Rozhkovskaya][elementary], Lesson 2, problem 2.1, and M.3, “More coded pictures.” Use original drawings. Preparation: moderate.

**14. Rolling cubes.** Use a large, distinctly marked cube and a grid whose squares match its footprint. Start with two-move comparisons and physically reset the cube each time. Extend with longer routes or a route back to the same square that changes the orientation. Source: Math for Love [“It all depends on the roll of the dice”][rolling], Puzzle 1, p. 1; short-route entry tasks are our adaptation. This is spatial experimentation, not a lesson on counting paths or formal group theory. Preparation: low.

**15. Maps.** Use small maps, erasable colors, or movable colored counters within regions. Two regions are neighbors only if they share a length of border. Begin with a few regions; extend by designing a map needing three or four colors. Source: JRMF [Map Coloring Teaching Guide][maps], pp. 1–4 and task bank pp. 7–10. Use small fictional maps rather than requiring geographic knowledge. Preparation: low to moderate.

**16. Frogs and toads.** Use two colors of counters and five marked spaces to start with two of each animal. Slide one space or jump over one occupied space into the empty space. Allow backward moves initially; make forward-only movement an extension. Source: JRMF [Frogs and Toads][frogs], pp. 3–6 and 9. The counting formula on p. 8 is an optional private extension for a child who wants it. Preparation: low.

**17. Sticks.** Use craft sticks and a simple starting diagram. Begin by freely building and identifying triangles, then remove sticks. Extend by designing a puzzle with two different solutions. Source: JRMF [Toothpick Triangles][sticks], pp. 3–7. This connects to shape subtraction through a new physical operation and a geometric constraint. Preparation: low.

**18. Dominoes.** Use plain two-square pieces, 4×4 boards, removable square covers, and optional checkerboard overlays. Start with whole rectangles. Compare removing opposite corners with removing two adjacent corners. Extend by designing and explaining an impossible board. Source: [A Decade of the Berkeley Math Circle][decade], Session 5, §4.4, problem 4(b), printed p. 96 (PDF p. 116); using smaller boards and constructive controls is our adaptation. Preparation: moderate once.

**19. Unfolded billiards.** Use a prechecked short route, paper table copies, tracing paper, and a straightedge. Re-teach reflections for newcomers. Start by unfolding a single bounce; extend to the whole route or a larger table with the same proportions. The physical unfolding sequence is our proposed extension of [JRMF Billiards Geometry][billiards] and the Lowell billiards work, not a quoted activity from that guide. Preparation: moderate. This is the most speculative session; fall experience should determine how far to take it.

**20. Festival.** Reuse two or three summer kits and allow spoken or drawn explanations. Offer puzzle starters so inventing something is approachable. Each pair should supply a solution or a reason the challenge is impossible; another pair tests the instructions. Source inspiration: [Rozhkovskaya][elementary], M.4. Preparation: low; no public event or extra staffing is assumed.

## What to preserve when we develop the activities

- **Novelty:** reuse successful themes, but log the actual diagrams, rule sets, cipher types, messages, and questions used. The Lowell handouts already contain the 1–3 and 1–5 take-away games, animal seating puzzles, a beehive sum puzzle, Fibonacci tilings, grid-path counting, and several families of billiard tables. Reopening their source books can reproduce an old problem accidentally. Check any additional spring/summer history before finalizing instances.
- **Mathematical care:** in the proposed 1, 3, 4 game, small losing piles are 0, 2, 7, 9, 14, 16; a few examples do not prove a general pattern. In color-code play, feedback counts correct positions only. In shape addition, overlapping strokes make “add, then subtract” potentially lose information. For dominoes, equal black/white counts are necessary, not sufficient for an arbitrary board to be tileable.
- **Preparation:** reuse blocks, counters, snap cubes, grid boards, dry-erase sleeves, and markers. Add mirrors, tracing paper, and craft sticks for the relevant weeks. During production, check physical scale, cipher decoding, puzzle solvability, and any claim of uniqueness. No special room, projector, or purchased curriculum is assumed.
- **Pacing:** weeks 10 and 20 are real making-and-explaining sessions, and also provide breathing room. If an investigation needs another week, use that flexibility or defer a new topic. Finishing every source challenge is not the success criterion.
- **Learning from this year:** record a brief note after each meeting: what held attention, where children needed help, what they invented, and what they wanted to try next. This becomes evidence for Year B and later K–1/4–5 adaptations. Year A is only half of a possible two-year rotation within the 2–3 band.

## Decisions for discussion

The main recommendation is to keep the fall close to the demonstrated favorites, then use summer to try more new formats. The largest choices are whether two consecutive cipher sessions feel right, whether the existing group has already tried the proposed new games, and whether the speculative unfolding session should instead become another logic or shape workshop. Group size, adult help, and available manipulatives will affect preparation and grouping, but need not hold up reviewing the mathematical menu.
