# Weeks 13–25: independent introduction audit

I visually inspected 95 student pages across all 52 current student PDFs in this range: 39 base band packets and 13 separate companions. Every PDF's opening page was rendered and viewed. Companion band starts and new-convention pages were also inspected; the coverage table and JSON identify the exact pages. This is an introduction audit, not a full mathematical verification, full-page layout audit, or classroom trial.

Currentness was checked against `lowell-math-circle-year-2/WEEKS-11-51.md` and `BONUS-AND-RETURN-VISITS.md`. The inspected files are the direct current week-folder links, not archived versions or editable-package reference duplicates. The index identifies Week 15's base core as piloted at home; this audit does not extend that claim to its companion or to other weeks.

## Prioritized finding

**Week 22, K–1, Grades 2–3 and Grades 4–5, student page 1: the examples define one region but do not show the actual two-group task.** The E–J key shows a point, a segment and a filled triangle separately. The task then asks children to split the labels, draw both regions and mark where they meet. A child can understand each shape and still wonder whether to divide the sheet into regions, join all four dots, reuse a dot in both groups, or make the groups touch at a labeled dot. The shared point need not itself be one of the labels, and that is not illustrated by the key.

This is a **moderate, localized concern**, strongest for K–1 and for children new to the activity. It closely matches the human distinction in the Week 78 review between a clear shape definition and an unclear task. It is not a proposal to simplify the Radon investigation or remove the upper-band material.

The facilitator guide partially addresses the gap: PDF page 3 (printed page 2) asks the adult to divide a rectangle's corners into two opposite-side pairs, draw them and ask whether they share a point. That is a useful failed attempt, followed by an invitation for a child to regroup. However, neither the student opening nor this launch explicitly ensures that one successful split is completed and its common point pointed out before independent work.

**Minimal fix:** finish one non-task split in two colors or solid/dashed lines, retain the two group memberships, and mark one common point on both regions. A crossing-segment example is enough to show the operation; a point-inside-triangle example can remain a later discovery. This can be a small printed worked visual or an explicit completed step in the shared launch; no extra student printable is needed. The normal definition-only demonstration is not quite enough, but this brief task demonstration should be.

## Demo dependencies checked and not treated as defects

**Week 23, all three base bands, page 1, and bonus page 1:** the bare student opening has no worked card traversal. I initially considered this a concern, then checked the actual launch. The base adult guide PDF page 2 (printed page 1) instructs a one-bar comparison with 3 and 1, then already ordered and equal cards, an untouched third lane, and one two-bar run. This is a sufficient ordinary physical demonstration of a small fixed operation. The student rules and endpoint dots remain available afterward. I do not recommend adding an example mechanically or labeling these packets unclear. The bonus guide page 1 likewise supplies a non-task dotted-endpoint comparison with all cards advancing together. This judgment assumes those specified launches occur; the PDFs are not self-teaching manuals.

**Week 19, all base bands, page 1:** “fits inside” could be mistaken for physical card size, but its definition is precise and the adult guide page 2 demonstrates A inside AB, AB with AC as a legal overlap, and the empty card. Children can then retain the mathematical choices. No packet change needed on this evidence.

**Week 20 bonus, page 1:** “choose fairly” does not explain the randomizer on the student page. The adult guide page 1 specifies two or three indistinguishable-back slips, replacement, and a short token walk including the zero-score neighbor. With those supplied materials this is sufficient; no need to burden the student page with a new mechanism.

**Week 21 bonus, page 1:** the small unfolding visual is compressed. The concrete task can nevertheless begin by placing string in a legal two-contact route, and the guide page 1 explicitly uses that route to match contacts and lengths to the visual. Treat reflection as a supported continuation, not a prerequisite to beginning the problem. No essential opening omission found.

## Representative strengths

- **Week 14 return visit, page 1:** ear peeling shows the original figure, the erased corner/sides, and the result. “Keep its third side” has a visible meaning, and the example does not supply the target catalog. Page 5 similarly demonstrates a half-turn before the symmetry search.
- **Week 17 base, all bands, page 1; return visit, page 1:** the machine examples include an input, intermediate states and an output. Children can check what an arrow loop means without an adult operating the whole investigation.
- **Week 20 K–1, page 1:** the copied piles and two sharing mats make the zero-valued square a full participant while retaining both original square piles. **Grades 4–5, page 1** explicitly checks two equations on the same unchanged board, avoiding sequential-update confusion.
- **Week 24 K–1, page 1:** all nine comparisons appear as concrete dot-card pairs; the group pools one collection of winners. This reduces bookkeeping without losing the nontransitive question.
- **Week 25 K–1, pages 1–2:** the two-counter picture becomes row/column counts before children reconstruct a picture. The convention is explained without solving the first task.
- **Weeks 13, 14 and 16 base openings:** concise rules and large usable diagrams already leave substantial mathematical choices to children. Additional solved examples are not needed simply to satisfy a template.
