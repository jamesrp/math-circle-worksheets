Week 36 bonus: Making threes

Mathematical kernels

1. In a two-player avoid-a-three game, players alternately claim an unused tile; the first to own all three tiles of an allowed triple loses. The FIRST player can claim a center tile c, then mirror each second-player choice through c in the nine-point affine plane. The other eight points form four pairs. The response cannot be previously claimed; the first player cannot own a pair opposite about c because its other point is second-owned. Any first-player triple not containing c reflects to a triple already owned by the second player. Thus the first player safely responds, and the second player must lose by their fourth choice. This is a competitive strategy and symmetry explanation, beyond the base one-player greedy avoidance task. Independently solve the finite game and check the copying strategy.

2. Coloring all nine tiles with two colors must produce a monochromatic allowed triple, because a triple-free color class has at most four points. Three colors can avoid monochromatic triples; verify a concrete coloring independently. This is a Ramsey-style two-versus-three coloring design, not a repeat of listing cap sets.

3. Three attributes (each with three values) create 27 tiles with the same all-same/all-different rule in each attribute. Any pair still has one completion. Layers and projections offer concrete ways to construct large triple-free collections and diagnose why simply stacking two nine-tile caps can fail. Do not require a full 27-card maximum theorem; select a finite achievable design goal with exact checked witness. This is a changed dimension/representation, not different numbers on an old puzzle.
