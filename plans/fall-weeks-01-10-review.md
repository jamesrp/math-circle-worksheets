**Corrections and clarity issues, in priority order**

1. **Week 2: the tree theorem needs a parity qualification.** [Extra task 3](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-02/week-02-extra-grades-6-7.tex:16) asks children to prove that an edge must be pressed exactly when one side has an odd number of targets. Repeated presses are legal, so that is false literally. For the empty target, pressing any edge twice succeeds even though both sides have an even target count. The same issue occurs in the [facilitator's used/omitted wording](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-02/week-02-facilitator.tex:49).

   Replace the assertion with “An edge is pressed an **odd number of times** exactly when one side has an odd number of targets.” Then conclude that the solution using each edge at most once is unique and shortest. Alternatively, restrict the assertion explicitly to such reduced solutions. The intended result is correct.

2. **Week 4: one instruction naturally asks for the wrong shift.** [Upper task 1](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-04/week-04-grades-4-5.tex:8) says, “Input 9 became output 1. Find the shift from 0 to 11.” The intended shift is +4; “from 0 to 11” instead sounds like a movement with answer +11. Use “Find the shift +k, where k is between 0 and 11.”

3. **Week 9: qualify the density theorem under the worksheet's stopping rule.** The [extra page](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-09/week-09-extra-grades-6-7.tex:12) says an irrational-slope square billiard is dense, immediately after changing the starting point in task 3. Its rule box says to stop at a corner. With that convention, the unrestricted statement needs a qualification: launch from (0, 2−√2) with slope √2. The unfolded line reaches (1,2), which folds to the bottom-right corner, where the path stops.

   The origin-start √2 example is correct. A precise replacement is: “The √2-slope path launched from (0,0) above is dense: it comes arbitrarily close to every point.” Align the [facilitator wording](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-09/week-09-facilitator.tex:48). This is a convention/hypothesis issue, not an error in the core corner calculation. [Masur–Tabachnikov, §1.4, manuscript pp. 4–5](https://math.uchicago.edu/~masur/handbook1.pdf) discusses density in the torus model and permits continuation through vertices for integrable polygons; the worksheet uses a different stopping convention.

4. **Week 4: bound the six-picture step sizes.** The [K–1 extra prompt](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-04/week-04-k-1.tex:22) asks which step sizes visit every card but does not restrict the range. The [facilitator](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-04/week-04-facilitator.tex:19) answers 1 and 5, although 7, 11, 13, and other integers coprime to 6 also work. Add “among steps 1 through 5,” as the [plan already does](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/week-04-redesign.md:33), or explicitly discuss equivalent steps.

5. **Week 3: increase or clarify the counter supply.** [K–1 task 4](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-03/week-03-k-1.tex:24) uses three counters per child. The three youngest children need nine for simultaneous individual work, but the [preparation list](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-03/week-03-facilitator.tex:8) supplies seven for the whole group. Supply nine for KK1 plus counters for the older children—13 if each older child retains one—or explicitly allow the supplied slips to serve as tracking counters. Update the plan and README together.

6. **Week 5: delay asking children to eliminate a bottom row.** The [upper worksheet](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-05/week-05-grades-4-5.tex:15) asks for “Possible bottom rows; how I ruled one out” before the next prompt introduces the necessary interaction with row 3. Both 4123 and 4213 satisfy the bottom clues. Eliminating one requires the subsequent row-3 and row-2 analysis. First record both possibilities, then use columns for bottom row / row 3 / possible row 2. The facilitator's actual case proof is correct.

7. **Small copyediting fixes.** The extra packets are described as “1 pages” in the [Week 5](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-05/README.md:8), [Week 6](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-06/README.md:8), and [Week 7](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-07/README.md:8) READMEs. **Depth, week by week**

**Week 1 — a strong benchmark, especially in the middle investigation.**

The K–1 exchange shop produces a genuine invariant—unchanged shape—and a reversible local move. The middle progression is especially successful: equal area fails to guarantee tileability; an up/down imbalance proves impossibility; a packing attains a lower bound; purple preserves the obstruction by replacement; pink changes its premises. This is serious mathematical reasoning with a very small arithmetic burden. The middle optimum of three green fillers and the different optimum of five total blocks are correctly distinguished.

The upper investigation connects physical tilings to a state graph, shortest paths, and parity. Six tilings, six graph edges, and a four-flip extreme distance are correct. One facilitation distinction deserves emphasis: the [six supplied cards](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-01/week-01-grades-4-5.tex:15) are not by themselves a proof of completeness. The [ribbon argument](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-01/week-01-facilitator.tex:64) supplies that missing fact. If the session stops before the ribbon argument, it can still establish excellent graph results, but describe completeness as supplied or still awaiting explanation. The plan already warns against confusing six examples with an exhaustive classification.

All four extensions develop real new questions. X1 strengthens a global balance obstruction to a local shortage; X2 shows why equal scalar scores need not mean zero distance; X3 distinguishes states from routes; X4 distinguishes two random rules and stationarity from time-step convergence. The matching minimum of four fillers, 20 larger-board tilings, nine-flip extreme distance, and 42 shortest routes check out. The connection to lozenge flips and distance is direct, with appropriate restrictions on regions and tiles; see [Saldanha–Tomei, §3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9801111).

For returners, X2's opening enumeration of three R's and three L's has a close prior counterpart in Lowell Handout 5, problems 5.5–5.6: three cookies of each type and three east/three north steps. If a child recognizes it, reuse that knowledge and move to distance or route counting. This does not make the extension repetitive as a whole; its new mathematical work is substantial.

**Week 2 — one of the most complete examples of the intended design.**

K–1 has an accessible undo operation and an impossible singleton. Middle work pairs the parity obstruction with constructions of all even targets, so it addresses necessity and sufficiency. Upper work reduces repeated presses, shows why a first-edge choice forces the rest, finds the two complementary solutions, and optimizes between them. These are actual instances of binary linear algebra and graph incidence, without requiring the terminology.

The disconnected extra is a good counterexample to an overbroad conjecture; the tree cut rule is a coherent next theorem. Correct the parity wording above. An optional small improvement is to ask explicitly why, after choosing the first edge, the [upper table leaves no further choices](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-02/week-02-grades-4-5.tex:16). The facilitator already explains it; making the child articulate it protects the exhaustiveness claim from becoming mere table filling.

**Week 3 — a well-chosen extremal problem with a manageable search space.**

K–1 compares a three-cycle, a swap with a fixed point, and a noninvertible key. Middle work uses equality patterns and loop structure, rather than merely encoding more messages. Upper work asks whether five symbols can take longer than five turns to reset, then proves the maximum of six using cycle partitions. The noncommuting swaps make a valuable contrast with the following week's shifts. The eight-symbol extension correctly reaches a maximum of 15.

The principal optional improvement is to protect the classification step. [Upper page 2](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-03/week-03-grades-4-5.tex:16) supplies all seven partitions while asking the child to list the possibilities. That is useful support, but can turn the investigation into seven return-time calculations. Cover the supplied rows initially, or ask the child to organize them by largest loop and explain why none is missing. This is an improvement to already substantive work, not a demand for a longer proof.

**Week 4 — correct mathematics, but the general proof needs a better bridge.**

The younger versions are strong: missed positions, separate loops, incompatible code clues, and the distinction between an invertible rule and a rule whose orbit visits every position. The upper classification and the shuffle extension faithfully connect to cyclic groups and multiplicative order. The out-shuffle treatment correctly distinguishes card labels from positions and handles the final fixed position separately.

The main gap is [upper task 6](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-04/week-04-grades-4-5.tex:24). Reducing 12 divides 8t to 3 divides 2t is concrete, but the general conclusion requires: when n′ and k′ are coprime, n′ divides k′t only if n′ divides t. The [facilitator](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-04/week-04-facilitator.tex:27) names prime factors or Euclid's lemma without developing either, while readiness mentions counting to 18 and introducing gcd through examples.

Add an adult-supported divisibility argument, explicitly identify that lemma as additional readiness, or separate a proved finite-ring result from a general conjecture. The target formula is right; the issue is whether the child can explain the step that makes it general. The whole 12-row table should remain optional once a child sees the structural argument.

**Week 5 — excellent lower and middle versions; make the upper conceptual payoff explicit.**

K–1's six skylines, opposite viewpoints, and impossible pair of views offer classification and obstruction with objects. The middle activity is particularly strong: two completions, a second clue that selects one, and a row/column swap showing why one clue cannot suffice. This pairs an optimal construction with a general lower bound.

The upper five-clue uniqueness proof is correct and substantial. However, the [plan promises](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/week-05-redesign.md:13) deleting each clue and discovering a smaller three-clue set. A small optional continuation would complete the central comparison: give the five alternate cities as certificates to check, then ask the child to test left clues 4, 3, 2 on rows 1, 2, 3. Those clues force the same target and demonstrate that minimal need not mean minimum. The [facilitator has all of this](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/lowell-math-circle-year-2/source/week-05/week-05-facilitator.tex:42). **Week 8 — another particularly successful proof sequence.**

**Implications for the whole sequence**

The strongest feature is the repeated distinction between finding an example and explaining why it is best, unique, exhaustive, or unavoidable. The connections across weeks are mathematical: toggle parity returns in graph repair; general permutations specialize to shifts; uniqueness becomes efficient identification; restricted subtraction becomes Nim; modular repetition becomes unfolded geometry. The extras generally change the question rather than merely enlarge the numbers.

The main recurring risk is confusing a destination in the adult notes with a result children have established. In Weeks 4 and 9 the missing bridge is divisibility; in Week 5 it is a concrete minimal-versus-minimum comparison; in Week 6 it is symmetry and the final indistinguishability argument; in Week 10 it is constructing before generalizing. Preserve the distinction in the use log: “tried,” “conjectured,” “verified these cases,” “proved,” and “given as a theorem” describe different mathematical experiences.

The detailed timetables also diverge from the shared-hour description. The [shared guide](/Users/jamespfeiffer/math-circle/plans/fall-k-5-year-a-activities.md:11) promises ten minutes of exploration and a 35–40-minute movement/reset break. Weeks 2–4 schedule mathematical work through that interval; Weeks 5–7 use a different flow with no explicit reset. Flexible timing is appropriate, but identify one default rhythm so a volunteer following only the weekly packet does not accidentally omit the break. This is operational consistency, not an argument for rigid timing.

For the hour with seven children and two adults, identify one satisfying stopping point per group, plus an optional proof continuation. Week 6 especially needs adult discussion; more table entries will not substitute for it. Weeks 1, 2, 7, and 8 already provide particularly good models of questions a child can test independently and then explain to an adult.

These judgments are consistent with the actual source accounts: *Math Circle by the Bay*, preface viii–x, advocates themes of substantial depth, manipulatives, extra challenges, repeated explanation, and flexible pace; Rozhkovskaya's Lesson 3 describes trying before displaying a table, Lesson 7 emphasizes legal play and construction, and Lesson 8 records copying becoming a pacing problem. The particular scaffolds proposed here are review recommendations, not instructions quoted from those books. Nothing in this review assumes every optional theorem must fit into one meeting.
