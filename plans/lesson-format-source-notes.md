# Running a mixed-level hour: notes from the books

Research notes, September 18, 2026, for the [fall K–5 plan][fall]. Current roster: K, K, 1; 3, 3, 3; one fifth grader. All seven children share a room with two adults. These notes distinguish the books' accounts from our proposed adaptation.

## What the Eugene passage actually says

[Math Circle by the Bay][bay], preface, printed p. viii (PDF p. 9), describes **two elementary groups**: generally grades 1–2 and 3–4, or **2–3 and 4–5 in Eugene**. It reports weekly sessions of 50–55 minutes, with 25–30 children and two instructors per group. Eugene has additional undergraduate assistants, bringing staffing to approximately one instructor per six children and permitting more individual attention.

This passage does not describe a K–1 group or specify whether Eugene's groups share a room. It does not explain an adult rotation between groups. These are accounts in the book, not a check of how the programs operate today.

The three-level passage is in [Natasha Rozhkovskaya's Math Circles for Elementary School Students][elementary], **“Introduction: Manhattan 2011”** (EPUB section `OEBPS/part0027.xhtml`). The Kansas State University program has three levels roughly divided by age; its Level I is analogous to BMC-Elementary. The introduction does not give the three grade boundaries or say that they meet together. Thus it does not establish K–1 / 2–3 / 4–5 as the book's grouping scheme.

## What they do during a lesson

### Choose an idea that can grow into serious mathematics

On **printed p. viii (PDF p. 9), “How we select our topics,”** *Math Circle by the Bay* describes choosing themes with deep mathematical context that remain interesting from first grade through undergraduate, graduate, and professional mathematics. The authors explicitly connect this to a developing mathematical tradition, rather than treating an entertaining puzzle as an isolated trick. This is a particularly direct source for the organizer's September 19 request to deepen Weeks 2–10.

**Our adaptation:** begin with a small, playable instance and give it a mathematical destination: a complete classification, an invariant, an optimal construction with a lower bound, or a strategy that works against every reply. Put the undergraduate and research connections in the facilitator notes; children's pages should ask concrete questions that actually instantiate those ideas. A topic name or a paper citation alone does not make an activity deeper. The revised [Weeks 2–10 rationale](fall-weeks-02-10-mathematical-redesign.md) records this design standard and the source trail for each week.

The same book, printed pp. ix–x (PDF pp. 10–11), cautions against a uniform pace and supports manipulatives, extra challenges, and repeated opportunities to explain. Thus the deeper questions are invitations, not an expectation that every child finish a proof or every printed page during the hour. Rozhkovskaya's Lesson 8, “At the lesson,” item 3 (`part0018.xhtml`), also explicitly traces a children's investigation of new arithmetic operations to the contemporary MSRI program on tropical geometry. That is another documented example of a research connection influencing activity design; our particular replacement activities are not taken from that lesson.

### Math Circle by the Bay

The preface's **“How we teach,” printed p. ix (PDF p. 10)**, describes changing the pace through warmups, discussion, independent solving, children explaining at the board, and occasional hands-on projects. Manipulatives such as cubes, coins, and sticks are always available. Warmups also accommodate late arrivals. The instructors keep extra challenges for children who are ahead.

Explaining is treated as something children learn through practice: the instructors ask them to repeat or improve an explanation, even after they have solved the problem. Mistakes and correction are normal parts of the process.

On **printed p. x (PDF p. 11)**, the authors say they often use the same material with both age groups, changing pace and depth. They also caution that classes differ too much to prescribe how long a topic will take. This supports our common weekly themes, but not a fixed minute-by-minute formula or a requirement that every group finish together.

### Rozhkovskaya's elementary classes

**“Introduction: Berkeley 2009”** (`part0010.xhtml`) describes grades 1–3 split into two sessions with identical lesson plans, at different times. Alongside the author, two additional instructors helped at every lesson; parents could stay if they actively helped children and distributed materials. The stated aim was individual access to adult help. These were not three simultaneous age-level lessons.

**Lesson 3, “At the lesson,” item 1** (`part0013.xhtml`) gives a particularly useful teaching sequence: children first try a logic problem for a few minutes, then explain their solutions, then the instructor draws a table to make the reasoning visible. The same passage describes five-year-olds working with parents alongside children with much more mathematical experience. Additional problems were prepared for quick finishers at every lesson, and generally were not discussed with the whole class.

**Lesson 7, “At the lesson,” items 1–2** (`part0017.xhtml`) supplies two practical observations. During a two-player game, unexpected results apparently arose from missed legal moves: checking that children can play the rules matters before interpreting their strategy. In a separate activity, open construction of polygons with K'nex was highly engaging, including making a giant rectangle. Physical exploration can be a substantial mathematical part of the session.

**Lesson 8, “At the lesson,” item 2** (`part0018.xhtml`) records a pacing problem: writing and coloring tables took very different amounts of time for different children. Instructors reduced some of the copying work for the next table. Our inference: offer a meaningful next question when a child is ready, and avoid using copying or extra arithmetic merely to keep everyone occupied for the same duration.

The introduction of [A Decade of the Berkeley Math Circle][decade], printed pp. ix–xi (PDF pp. 11–13), concerns a primarily middle/high-school program and describes two-hour sessions. It is a less direct model for the logistics of this elementary group; the two elementary books above are the principal sources for the revised hour.

## What we propose for this room

The books support different levels of challenge, active adult help, time for independent attempts, and opportunities to explain. **The staffing, seating, triplet roles, and timetable below are our proposals**, not arrangements documented in these sources.

- Keep KK1 and 333 as triplets; put the fifth grader beside the third graders so shared problems and conversation remain possible.
- Give one adult primary responsibility for KK1. Have the other alternate between 333 and the fifth grader, leaving a clear next attempt and returning predictably.
- Give children in a triplet enough materials to try things themselves. Use rotating roles for exchanges and games; preserve two opposing sides in games whose strategy depends on that rule.
- Make adult discussion and play part of the fifth grader's planned experience. Extra challenges alone do not solve the lack of a peer or an opponent.
- Use a short common launch and brief closing exchange. Discuss deeper extensions locally when they are not useful to the whole room.
- If a third parent joins, assign them to a group, with particular value as a game opponent and mathematical companion for the fifth grader.

Try the arrangement, then record where children waited, which rules needed adult help, and whether each child had time to manipulate, think, and explain. Use those observations to change the next week's staffing and tasks. The operational timetable is in [“A shared hour” in the fall plan][fall].
