## Facilitator opening: mathematical destination

**Growth and differences.** If a nested shape S_n has a_n unit tiles, its removable next layer has a_{n+1}−a_n tiles. This is the forward difference, and it is signed when total counts decrease. For side-n squares, a_n=n² and a_{n+1}−a_n=2n+1; the odd layer decomposes into a row of n+1 and column of n without double-counting their shared corner. For n-row staircases of lengths 1,…,n, a_n=n(n+1)/2 and the next layer has n+1 tiles. For n × (n+1) rectangles the difference is 2n+2. Second differences are respectively 2,1,2. These conclusions follow from specified building laws, not guessing a continuation from four pictures.

**Accumulation and differences undo one another when the initial value is included.** Define A_0=b and A_n=b+u_1+…+u_n. Then A_n−A_{n−1}=u_n by cancellation. Knowing all increments but not b leaves a constant ambiguity: starting with five extra tiles adds five to every total. In a cascade, stage one includes the current input before its new total is copied into stage two: B_n−B_{n−1}=A_n. Under the forward convention, ΔB_n=B_{n+1}−B_n=A_{n+1}; the difference row begins at A_1, not another leading zero. With all-one inputs and zero starts, the first stage gives n, the second n(n+1)/2, and the third n(n+1)(n+2)/6. Repeated differences recover the preceding stage with this alignment.

**Constant higher differences require a rule over the relevant range.** A polynomial of degree d has constant d-th forward difference and zero (d+1)-st difference; for a n^d term the d-th difference is a·d!. The binomial-basis identity Δ binom(n,k)=binom(n,k−1) is a discrete counterpart to reducing degree. The general algebraic theorem is adult background or an advanced return visit. Concrete children can prove the staircase and square facts by moving layers, and prove accumulation cancellation with objects. A finite list alone never determines an arbitrary next value: two fully specified building rules can agree for the first four terms and differ on the fifth.

Experiments here produce shapes and total rows; conjectures concern regular layers; explanations use a tile partition, cancellation or a specified recurrence. Grades K–1 may use nested shapes only; grades 2–3 can invert increments and totals; grades 4–5 can operate a two-stage machine, classify constant differences and separate evidence from a specified law. The third-stage and three-dimensional continuations are optional; no formal derivative, limit, or polynomial notation is an entry prerequisite.

## Kernel-only brief for the later writer

1. A specified shape-growth rule yields a recoverable layer and a forward difference. Different shapes can share a difference feature without having equal totals.
2. Accumulation and differences are inverse operations once an initial value is specified. Repeated accumulation gives higher-dimensional counting sequences and repeated differences recover their inputs.
3. A finite sample supports a conjecture, but only a stated rule justifies a unique continuation. Constant differences at every future stage are an additional assumption.

Suggested emphasis: K–1, optional square/staircase growth with removable layers; 2–3, reconstructing totals from supplied layer cards and comparing different starts; 4–5, two-stage accumulation, difference rows, and nonunique continuation. No lower-band arithmetic worksheet is required. Give a child a route based on retained rules and number comparison rather than age alone.

## Materials and preparation

Per pair for the concrete route: seventy-two identical 20 mm square tiles or square-ended snap cubes usable flat; six stage cards numbered 0–5; two shallow trays or marked regions labeled old shape and new layer; a 120 × 120 mm board for side-six squares and a 120 × 140 mm board for 6 × 7 rectangles. Provide paper outlines for stages 1–4 at 20 mm spacing, plus unnumbered extra workspace. A staircase through step five uses fifteen tiles; side-five square uses twenty-five; step-five 5 × 6 rectangle uses thirty. Seventy-two pieces permit the square and rectangle to be displayed simultaneously, with reserve layers. Do not require six complete square snapshots concurrently (they would need ninety-one tiles through side six). Photographing children or raw classroom logs is not part of this task; the adult can sketch a single saved configuration.

For the accumulation route provide two 100 × 80 mm receiving trays per pair, a small input tray, forty 15–20 mm counters, and large reusable total cards 0–40 (45 × 32 mm) written as needed on blank cards rather than pre-cutting forty-one cards for every pair. For each move children transfer the input counters into the first total tray, leave them there, then copy its new total using number cards into the second stage's addition slot. At most one tray contains a high-count second-stage display; quantities above twenty use a number card, after the child understands what it stands for. Cards are a deliberate replacement for huge physical counts, not extra concurrent logs. Include a prominent persistent initial-value card beside each tray; do not reset it invisibly.

Print Letter, 100%, equal scaling for all tile diagrams. A compact growth picture may use 5 mm unit squares because children build on the separate actual-size boards; working boards use 20 mm cells. Layer cards for P3 have a pictured strip or L-shape on their front, with a numeral on the back for optional adult use; dimensions match 20 mm pieces. Supply five odd L-layer outlines of sizes 1,3,5,7,9 and five even rectangle-layer outlines of sizes 2,4,6,8,10. The even layer at step n grows the (n−1) × n rectangle into n × (n+1): a new top row of n+1 tiles owns the corner, and a right column beside the old rows has n−1 tiles. At n=1 the second part is empty. Children use individual loose tiles within the outlines and may rearrange them for a different starting patch. These are movable outlined region cards, not forty transcription boxes. For late 3-D work one shared kit of sixty-four cubes suffices for side-four cubes; distinguish the old corner cube and its three disjoint shell parts by position, color or removable covers. Do not commit a requirement to buy hundreds of cubes.

At the four-child tables use two concrete kits; the three-child upper table uses one machine, with rotating feeder, accumulator and checker. One anchored adult can read, check one persistent stage state, and record outputs on a whiteboard. Preparation with existing tiles: about 15–25 minutes for boards and reusable cards. Before production, rehearse the exact cascade: adding a new input, retaining the first total, copying that total, and retaining the second total. Confirm zero input is a real move and leaves stage one unchanged while stage two may still increase. If adults must run all stages, stay with shapes and a single accumulator.

## Common launch and the first two convention visuals

Let children handle tiles for two minutes. Build a 2 × 2 square. Ask a child to make it a 3 × 3 square while leaving the old square together; use a temporary different-color layer. Say: “We are studying what gets added and what is there altogether.” Do not announce the odd-number law. The launch demonstrates a legal growth action and its one record.

Page 1 shared guidance includes a **non-task** example using a fixed-width two-row strip, not squares or staircases: **input**, a 2 × 2 rectangle of four tiles; **middle**, the same four tiles shaded gray and one new two-tile column hatched; **output**, a six-tile rectangle with total `6`, beside a detached new column labeled `added 2`. Below, matching labels give `old total 4 → added 2 → new total 6`. This explicitly bridges physical growth to counts before number rows appear.

Before P4, print a second non-task example of one accumulator: **input**, total tray holding three counters and incoming card `2`; **middle**, the two counters entering the tray, old three still visible; **output**, tray total five and saved total card `5`. Labels are `old total 3`, `add 2`, `new total 5`. No second-stage rule is introduced yet. The cascade gets its own visual immediately before P5, after children have used one machine.

## Solutions and adult proof map

**P1.** Side-n square totals for n=0,…,6 are 0,1,4,9,16,25,36. New layers are 1,3,5,7,9,11. A layer from n to n+1 is an L: one strip n+1 tiles long and one n tiles long. Thus it has 2n+1 tiles. A child can detach the L, straighten it, and compare successive layers. The count is derived from the building law; an adult may record total and layer rows only when comparison has a purpose. Optional hint: put old square in a corner of the bigger square. Estimate 10–20 minutes.

**P2.** Staircase totals are 0,1,3,6,10,15; increments 1,2,3,4,5. Rectangle totals are 0,2,6,12,20,30; increments 2,4,6,8,10. The same numerical layer rule does not make both if the starts are as shown. Each rectangle can be cut along its staircase boundary into two reflected n-step staircases, so its total and added count are twice the staircase's. Two copies of the staircase make an n × (n+1) rectangle, establishing `2·(1+…+n)=n(n+1)` with objects. This is a pairing proof, not an imposed multiplication exercise. Optional hint: use a second staircase. Estimate 12–25 minutes.

**P3.** Empty start plus odd L-layers makes squares with totals 1,4,9,16,25; empty start plus even paired strips makes n × (n+1) rectangles with totals 2,6,12,20,30. The same added pieces can surround a fixed starter patch or grow beside it, giving counts exactly four larger: 5,8,13,20,29 or 6,10,16,24,34. The placement need not form a square; a connected attachment is possible, but connectedness is not part of the printed task. Preserve the starter and attach layers without overlap. For any two starts b and c, identical increments produce totals differing by the fixed amount c−b. Optional prompt: can the new pieces tell us how many were there before? Estimate 10–20 minutes.

**P4.** Any four inputs in {0,…,5} summing to ten work: e.g. `1,2,3,4` and `5,0,5,0`, or different orders of one multiset. The running total is the sum of all inputs so far; induction proves this because each move adds exactly the new card. The final result of one accumulator does not depend on order. Zero is a legal move, and its total does not change. A child may find ten using objects while another seeks many different input rows. No exhaustive four-card catalog is required. Estimate 10–20 minutes.

**P5.** For five inputs u_1,…,u_5, the last second-stage total is `5u_1+4u_2+3u_3+2u_4+u_5`: the first input is copied at every move, the last only once. For multiset {2,2,1,0,0}, the maximum is 21, attained by `2,2,1,0,0`, and minimum is 9, attained by `0,0,1,2,2`. Moving a larger card earlier increases the last total: swapping adjacent a<b changes the total by b−a in favor of b then a. Repeated swaps sort the cards, giving a complete extremal proof without enumerating thirty distinct arrangements. The supplied sample order `2,0,1,0,2` gives first totals 2,2,3,3,5 and second totals 2,4,7,10,15. In contrast to P4, order now matters. Optional physical explanation: trace one card's contribution through how many later copies remain. Estimate 15–30 minutes; this may be the entire upper-table visit.

**P6.** Inputs are 2,0,1,0,2, the consecutive changes. Starting at four with the same inputs gives totals 4,6,6,7,7,9. Cancellation proves reconstruction: the old total is included in the new total, so removing it leaves exactly the input. Different initial totals can use the same cards, but cannot produce the same entire listed total strip including the initial value. If only the five inputs are supplied, the starting value is undetermined. An optional signed example `start 5 → 7 → 4` uses inputs +2 then −3, represented by placing/removing counters. It is adult scaffolding rather than a new printed bookkeeping burden. Estimate 10–20 minutes.

**P7.** Rule A: build the side-n square at every step. Rule B: build that square and, from step five onward, add a one-tile-wide tail of n−4 extra tiles. Both give the four supplied square pictures; fifth counts are 25 and 26. The two rules are precise for every positive whole-number step. They may be aesthetically unequal, but no simplicity assumption was made. For adults or algebra-ready children, `n²+binom(n−1,4)` gives another integer nonnegative rule that agrees for n=1,…,4 and gives 26 at n=5. More generally a polynomial multiplied by `(n−1)(n−2)(n−3)(n−4)` can change later values while preserving the sample. The printed task needs no polynomial interpolation. A constant observed second-difference row of the four values does not compel a future one unless that rule is stipulated. Estimate 10–20 minutes; oral invention with an adult sketch is acceptable.

**P8.** Totals 1,8,27,64; new layers from side n−1 to n are 1,7,19,37. Decompose the shell into one n × n face, one n × (n−1) face, and one (n−1) × (n−1) face after assigning all shared edges to the first available face. The sum is `n²+n(n−1)+(n−1)²=3n²−3n+1`. The face partition requires mutually disjoint tile/cube pieces, not three full faces that double-count shared edges and corners. Successive shell increases are 6,12,18, and the next difference is 6. Children may show the shell physically; the algebraic degree argument is optional. Estimate 15–30 minutes, separate encore.

**P9.** With one input each time, stage one gives 1,2,3,4; stage two 1,3,6,10; stage three 1,4,10,20. A stack whose horizontal layers are staircases of sizes 1,3,6,… is a tetrahedral staircase of cubes: at stage n, include all triples of nonnegative whole numbers (i,j,k) with i+j+k≤n−1. It has `binom(n+2,3)=n(n+1)(n+2)/6` cubes. An elementary recurrence proof counts layers by k: the k-th horizontal layer is a staircase, so stage n's total is the sum of the first n triangular numbers. The displayed finite counts are verified by enumeration or objects; the general closed formula is optional. If third-stage totals are C_n, then C_n−C_{n−1}=B_n and B_n−B_{n−1}=A_n, all starts zero. In forward-difference rows omit the extra zero: ΔC_n=B_{n+1}, then Δ²C_n=A_{n+2}, then Δ³C_n=1. Estimate 20–35 minutes, after successful two-stage play.
