## Theorem-first facilitator overview

**An alternating switch balances its two exits within one counter.** An arrow card means the **next** exit: follow it, then flip it to the other exit. The card is not reset between counters. After n visits to a junction, the two exit counts differ by at most 1, irrespective of the starting arrow. In any consecutive even number of visits, they are equal. This local statement counts visits to that junction, not the total number of counters introduced elsewhere.

**A complete binary tree of depth d sends every consecutive block of 2^d counters once to each leaf.** Edges lead only down the tree; every internal vertex has a persistent alternating left/right card. A block of 2^d counters sends 2^(d−1) to either child, then 2^(d−2) to each grandchild, and so on. At depth three, every block of eight reaches all eight leaves once. Starting arrows change the order but not these block counts. The entire seven-card configuration returns after eight counters, because each card receives an even number of visits. This is a finite induction, not an empirical claim based on a few trials. It is false for general uneven trees or if switches reset between counters.

**Feedback on a finite absorbing road still stops and encodes distance.** Use vertices 0,1,…,m, absorbing endpoints 0 and m, and alternating persistent next-exit switches at the interior vertices. Insert each counter at s, let it finish, then insert the next. Every counter eventually reaches an endpoint. If S is the number of interior cards currently pointing to the right, the current counter's position plus S is invariant during a walk: moving left decreases position by 1 but increases S by 1; moving right does the reverse. Over n complete counters, with R finishing on the right,

`m R + S_final = n s + S_initial`.

Since 0 ≤ S ≤ m−1, |R − ns/m| ≤ (m−1)/m < 1. Consequently **every consecutive block of m counters sends exactly s to the right**. A five-edge road entered at 1 sends one of every five to the right; entered at 2, it sends two. This exact elementary specialization is derived here, not quoted from a source theorem. Card orientations may differ after a block even when their total S returns; the balance result does not depend on every card returning.

**Balance does not give random independence.** These machines are deterministic once their starting cards and input positions are fixed. Repeated outputs are correlated and often periodic. A fair coin at each departure can produce repeated identical exits, whereas one alternating switch cannot. A random walk on the m-edge absorbing path has right-hitting probability s/m, agreeing with the rotor frequency; the mechanisms do not have the same distribution of output words. The random-walk comparison requires a separate probability-readiness gate and is an optional adult-supported extension.

Children first operate switches and try to program orders. Trials are experiments; a predicted universal block balance is a conjecture. Pairing alternating visits and passing equal bundles down levels explains the tree theorem. The feedback score gives a later explanation of distance-based frequency. Approximate grades 2–3 can control and program depth-one/two machines; grades 4–5 can reason through depth three and return to feedback. K–1 may choose a one-switch station with an adult, but no forced multi-level packet is planned.

## Kernel-only brief for later worksheet production

1. Persistent alternating junctions give local discrepancy at most one. A complete depth-three binary tree routes each block of eight equally to its eight leaves, for every starting configuration; it returns to that configuration after eight inputs. A deterministic order can be programmed within constraints, so equal output totals do not mean arbitrary output sequences.
2. An absorbing path with persistent left/right rotors terminates; the position-plus-right-card score proves that every block of m counters started at s has s right exits. This gives a concrete deterministic analogue of hitting probability while preserving the distinction between balance and independent randomness.

Suggested emphasis: K–1, optional one-switch physical station; grades 2–3, one/two-level order puzzles and physically checked balance; grades 4–5, three-level programming and a bundle proof, followed on another visit by absorbing-road feedback and its score. Arithmetic requirements are counting to eight/twelve for the core, and simple addition/subtraction or adult recording for the score. No formula reading is needed for physical play.

Materials: reusable one-switch, depth-two and depth-three boards; reversible left/right marker cards whose visible side indicates next exit; counters, fixed exit cups/spots, separate initial-state cards and one arrival strip. Each counter finishes before the next starts; only the encountered card changes. The later writer may select pages while retaining these constraints.

## Common launch and convention visual

Let children flip a spare switch and move a counter on the one-switch board first. Then demonstrate one arrival: the visible card points right, so the counter goes right; turn the card to left, leave it there, and introduce a second counter without resetting. Let a child finish the second arrival. Say: “The card tells the next counter where to go. Follow it, then turn it over. Only that card changes.” Give pairs time to repeat this action before distributing deeper boards.

Shared student rules, once at the start of each packet: **“Send one counter at a time. At a junction, follow the arrow, then flip that card. Keep the cards as they are for the next counter. An exit keeps its counters.”** A problem explicitly allows choosing or resetting a starting configuration; changing cards mid-run is not allowed.

Before first use, a three-panel worked visual has matching node J and exit X/Y labels: **Input:** one counter at J, card next-right; **Move:** counter on J→Y, J's card being flipped; **Output:** one counter at Y, J's card next-left. This is one visit to a non-task machine, so it teaches the convention without enumerating a target tree or proving fairness. A small fourth marker shows the next counter waiting at ENTRY, not a reset arrow.

Before the feedback road's first problem, show a separate three-panel non-target road 0–1–2–3 with entry 2 and cards at 1 next-right, 2 next-left: **Input:** counter at 2; **Intermediate:** after 2→1, card 2 next-right; **Output:** after 1→2→3, counter in RIGHT, both visited cards next-left. The path is `2,1,2,3`. This explicitly shows a repeated visit to the same junction and the changed card governing it. It uses a three-edge road, not either target road below.

## First-visit solutions, proofs and adult facilitation

**P1.** Seven inputs give (4,3) or (3,4), according to the first arrow; twelve give (6,6). No run can leave a difference of two. Pair consecutive visits: each pair uses one exit each, and an unpaired last visit adds at most one. The child can physically pair exit counters, so no algebra is required. The starting arrow affects the extra counter when n is odd, not the balance bound. A repeated experiment is useful; don't turn each run into a tally of visits. Suggested 8–12 minutes.

**P2.** A,C,B is possible: root next-left; left child next-left; right child next-left. The first counter goes A, second C, third B. Their exits are followed by flips; the fourth is D. A,B,C is impossible because the first two inputs at the root must go to different halves; A and B share the left half. The final unused/right-child state after the third counter is observable and need not be copied repeatedly. The impossible order is a genuine local obstruction, not a question of trying all eight starting states. Suggested 10–15 minutes. Hint: after two outputs, point to the root's two departures; do not prescribe this on the page.

**P3.** A,E,C,G is achieved by all seven cards next-left. More precisely the root, both depth-one cards, and all four depth-two cards must point left when first encountered, so this target determines the all-left configuration. The complete resulting eight-word is A,E,C,G,B,F,D,H. A,B is impossible because the first two counters must enter opposite halves at the root. A different four-letter target may be possible only if odd/even arrivals at the root lie in opposite halves and each subtree's arrivals respect its own alternation. The adult can help a child reverse-engineer a target from the leaves; systematic enumeration is not required. Suggested 10–20 minutes. Let partners jointly test the program; a child need not operate seven cards from memory.

**P4.** No leaf stays empty. For any eight consecutive inputs, the root's alternating exits send four to each half. Those four arrivals to each child are consecutive visits at that child (other-half counters do not change its card), so it sends two to each quarter. Each quarter switch receives two arrivals and sends one to each leaf. Starting card directions and the number of earlier inputs do not matter. Every card receives 8,4 or 2 visits, all even, so the entire initial state of the chosen block is restored. This last fact also explains an eight-period output sequence on the unchanged complete tree. Children can explain with bundles of eight, four, two and one counters or with a drawing; the words “induction” and “period” are optional.

Experiments with several configurations make the question credible, but only the splitting explanation establishes every configuration and every block. For a selected later block, remove old counters from exit cups while retaining all seven arrow cards. Resetting the cards at that moment would test a different experiment, though still an eight-block theorem. Suggested 10–20 minutes as a continuation. A readiness-dependent extension asks about depth four: sixteen leaves and blocks of sixteen; use a drawing or whiteboard, not an unprepared larger manipulative kit.

### Encore solutions and explanations

**P1.** For the all-left start, the exit word is L,R,L,R,L,R,L,R. The first path is 2,1,0; the second is 2,3,2,1,2,3,4, then the all-left state is restored. The second path is useful for adult checking, not a solution printed before the task. Other starting states change the order; every consecutive four outputs contains two L and two R, so eight inputs give four each. Do not infer that arbitrary road states return after a block: on this same road, entering at 1 with next-arrow word LLR gives output LLRL after four counters and changes the card word to RLL. Its one-right-per-four balance holds immediately, despite the changed state; no burn-in is required.

No counter stays forever. If a walk on this finite road never ended, some interior vertex would be visited infinitely often. Its alternating card would send infinitely many visits to both neighbors. Repeating this argument along the road forces a visit to an absorbing endpoint, a contradiction. For children, start with the physical claim that any junction repeatedly visited must eventually send the chip towards each side; the full “infinitely many” argument is optional upper readiness conversation. A cap on an experimental walk is not a proof of nontermination; if a run seems stuck, inspect missed flips first. Suggested 15–25 minutes.

**P2.** Entrance 1 works and is the only entrance with one right exit in every five. Entrances 1,2,3,4 give respectively 1,2,3,4 right exits per five counters, for all starting arrows. For all-left starts, the first five output words are LLLLR, LLRLR, LRLRR and LRRRR. These are exact checker data for adults; do not print them on the problem page.

Introduce the score only after feedback play. Count one point for each road step from LEFT to the traveling counter; each interior next-right card contributes one point. When the counter goes left, its old next-left card flips to next-right, so the lost position point is replaced by a card point. A right move gains a position point and loses a card point. Nothing else changes. If a chip finishes left its position score is 0; right it is 5. Adding a new chip at entrance s adds s points. After five chips the total added position score is 5s, and the card-score change is between −4 and 4. Thus 5(R−s) equals the initial card score minus the final card score. It is a multiple of 5 with absolute value at most 4, so it is zero and R=s. This proves any starting state, not just pooled trials. General m gives the theorem above.

Whiteboard recording is legitimate: the child can choose entrance/configuration, move counters and check each local score cancellation while the adult draws a single score row. If the adult performs every move and every check, leave the proof for a later visit. The task can be satisfied experimentally at first, with the universal “can it spoil it?” claim marked a conjecture until explained. Suggested 15–30 minutes, readily a separate visit.

**P3.** A fresh coin machine can send six counters to X in a row, or even just two in a row. The alternating one-switch machine cannot send two consecutive counters to the same exit. Its two possible six-words are XYXYXY and YXYXYX. The coin's possible words are all 64 binary length-six words; each has probability 1/64 under independent fair tosses. Thus identical 3–3 totals in some runs do not make the output mechanisms equivalent. One switch's exact alternation is a correlation across inputs. Children need only exhibit XX; counting 64 is optional.

Optional upper probability conversation after P2: for a fair random walk on 0…5, let h(j) be the chance to reach RIGHT before LEFT. Boundary values are h(0)=0, h(5)=1, and a fair first step makes h(j) the average of h(j−1),h(j+1). The successive gaps are therefore equal, giving h(j)=j/5. The finite absorption property and these boundary equations give a unique solution. This explains why the rotor's long-run frequency matches the random hitting chance; it does not imply random independence or equiprobable rotor output words. Do not ask elementary children to enumerate infinitely many random-walk paths or import a theorem without its assumptions.

1. **Persistent state:** resetting after each chip destroys the investigation. Next-exit cards and the launch explicitly show the surviving card state. Initial-state cards apply only before a fresh trial. Define the card convention once and keep it consistent through tree and road.
2. **Root count versus local visits:** a switch only balances the arrivals it receives. Uneven trees do not have equal leaf frequencies. The planned theorem is restricted to complete binary trees with downward edges and equal depths. General-graph balance or independence is not claimed.
3. **Feedback error:** on a revisit use the current card, not the original picture. The worked 0–1–2–3 road includes a revisit before any target task. Children work with one traveling counter; exit piles represent completed counters.
4. **Age fit:** seven cards and returning paths can tax executive function despite little reading. Deeper machines are earned by independent operation of the smaller one, not age labels. Use a partner referee and adult arrival-strip scribing; don't require simultaneous per-node counts, steps, and outcome records.
5. **Proof load:** the tree has a direct bundle proof accessible through objects. The road score is a later representation with its own concrete check and can take another session. The optional probability comparison needs additional readiness; it is not a gate to enjoying deterministic roads.
6. **No repeated Week 11 theorem:** a generic abelian-order page would overlap chip firing and does not belong in this first visit. The novel core is persistent routing, constrained order programming, block balance and feedback-based distance ratios.
