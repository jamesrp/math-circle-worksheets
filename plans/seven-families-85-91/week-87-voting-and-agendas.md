## Facilitator opening: mathematical destination

**Sincere individual rankings can produce a majority cycle.** With three one-vote ranking cards `A first, B second, C third`; `B first, C second, A third`; `C first, A second, B third`, pairwise voting gives A over B, B over C, and C over A, each by two votes to one. Every card is a consistent ranking; their majority comparisons do not form one consistent ranking. Here a vote means choosing the higher-ranked of the two options currently on offer, with the ranking fixed throughout.

**The same votes can produce any knockout winner by changing the agenda.** Two options compete first, the loser is removed, and the first winner competes with the option that waited. Waiting A produces A; waiting B produces B; waiting C produces C. This claim depends on the three specified ranking cards and sincere voting in each contest. No ranking is changed and no voter lies. A candidate that beats both others in pairwise votes wins every such agenda. This is not a claim that all voting rules are equivalent or that every profile has an agenda-dependent outcome.

**Five nearest-location voters have a strict majority winner at their middle favorite.** On one common line, voter i favors the location with smaller distance from its fixed favorite p_i; equal distances are recorded as a tie/abstention. Suppose p1≤p2≤p3≤p4≤p5, with repeated favorites allowed, and the favorite p3 is an available choice. Against any q<p3, voters at p3,p4,p5 strictly prefer p3. Against q>p3, voters at p1,p2,p3 strictly prefer p3. Thus p3 gets at least three strict votes against every different location. Equal-distance ties among other voters do not weaken that result. Favorite 0 is a real voter, not a missing input. It is the **third favorite with multiplicity**, not the arithmetic mean or the midpoint of the line, that matters.

**The assumptions matter.** With four favorites 1,3,7,9 and candidate locations 0 through 10, every integer location 3 through 7 never loses a pairwise contest, but no location beats every other. With three distinct candidate locations on a line, the middle candidate cannot be the worst of all three for a nearest-distance voter. The cycle profile puts every candidate last on one card, so no ordering of A,B,C on a common line realizes all those rankings by distances. General single-peaked preferences lead to Black's median theorem; this family proves the narrower distance model with objects and keeps the stronger abstraction for adults.

Children should discover that agenda order can be mathematical data, then see why a concrete preference structure restores a guaranteed winner. Experiments are physically voting and moving markers; the cycle counts are a complete finite explanation; the general five-voter result needs the three-marker side argument. Grades 2–3 readiness can handle fixed ranking cards and two-stage knockout brackets. Grades 4–5 readiness can build new profiles, argue the median rule and investigate its limits. An adult-assisted K–1 vote trial may be enjoyable, but preserving imagined preferences across pairings is an untested executive-function demand; no separate K–1 packet is planned.

## Kernel-only brief for later worksheet workflow

1. Fixed transitive rankings ABC/BCA/CAB yield the majority cycle A>B>C>A. Each option can win the same sincere two-round knockout by receiving the bye. A Condorcet winner, when present, wins every three-option knockout agenda.
2. On an ordered line, five voters with distance preferences have a strict Condorcet winner at the third favorite, provided that location is available. For favorites 0,2,4,7,10 and candidates 0,…,10, the winner is 4. Against 0, the votes are 3 for 4, 1 for 0 and 1 tied; against 8, the vote is 3–2 for 4.
3. Optional encore: four favorites 1,3,7,9 give the weak winner interval [3,7] on the continuous line, and integers 3,4,5,6,7 on the printed board. The three-card cycle cannot arise from distances to three fixed distinct candidates on one common line.

Suggested emphasis: K–1, optional orally read one-pair trial only, with readiness assessed; 2–3, supplied imaginary ranking cards, sincere votes and agenda choices; 4–5, profile design and two separate visits for agenda/median mathematics, followed by even-voter and model-limit investigations. The theme need not fill a whole-group K–5 hour by itself.

## Shared launch and worked conventions

After movement time, let children handle letter tiles and ranking cards for a minute. The adult asks which of two offered tiles is higher on **one** card, physically puts that voter token beside it, and then lets a child choose for another card. Announce that these are imaginary voters whose cards stay fixed; children are investigating a voting rule, not having their own favorite voted down. Two-minute launch is enough. Preserve the ordinary, neutral meanings of A,B,C; no politics or real classroom election is needed.

Page 1 shared rules:

> Each card is one voter. Its top choice is first, then second, then third. In a vote between two options, each voter chooses the one higher on its card. Keep the cards unchanged. The option with more votes wins.

Worked visual before Problem 1, using non-task D,E,F cards: V1=`D,F,E`, V2=`F,E,D`, V3=`E,D,F`. **Input:** the three ranking cards beside the offered pair D/E. **Intermediate:** mark only D and E rows; V1's token goes to D, V2/V3 tokens to E. **Output:** tally mat D has one token, E has two; caption `E wins this vote`. Match voter IDs throughout. Use compact 20 × 40 mm card replicas and three horizontally adjacent panels in a 190 × 55 mm visual, rather than fitting nine full-size working cards on the task page. The example teaches that the favorite F on V2 is unavailable and that E wins that voter's current ballot. It does not show a three-way cycle.

The knockout convention appears on page 2 when needed, with its own worked visual. The line-distance convention appears on page 4 after children have a reason to ask how an outcome depends on preferences, or at the start of a separate line visit. Re-launch the physical action then; do not assume that a familiar ranking card has automatically taught distance comparisons.

## Page-by-page student plan

### Page 1 — fixed rankings; Problem 1

After rules/example:

> **Problem 1:** Hold each vote: A against B, B against C, and C against A. Is there an option that wins against both others?

Print compact but readable replicas of the three actual 45 × 80 mm cards ABC/BCA/CAB or provide the cut cards in an apparatus strip; if replicas are 32 × 60 mm, the separate full-size cards are the working objects. Show three matchup spaces at 42 × 35 mm, labelled `A / B`, `B / C`, `C / A`; leave them blank for a final winning tile or adult-scribed result. One 165 × 40 mm answer/drawing area. The actual vote is performed on the separate tally mat, cleared between comparisons; these three supplied spaces retain results after clearing. No child is asked to copy an entire pairwise table. Eight or more minutes is plausible because children change pairings, check each ballot and challenge the apparent winner.

### Page 2 — agendas; Problem 2

New shared convention:

> Choose one option to wait. Vote between the other two. Their winner faces the waiting option. The winner of that vote wins the knockout. Use the same ranking cards for both votes.

Worked non-task bracket using the D/E/F profile from page 1: **Input:** F in waiting slot, D/E in first-contest slots. **Intermediate:** E advances after the demonstrated 2–1 vote; final input slots show E/F. **Output:** V1/V2 choose F and V3 chooses E, so F is final winner 2–1. The first-round losing D tile is moved to a visible `out` area. The worked strip is 175 × 45 mm; full 175 × 100 mm working mat is separate. This demonstrates the procedure without exposing the A/B/C agenda answers.

> **Problem 2:** Choose an agenda that makes A win the knockout. Can you make B win? Can you make C win? Keep the three voter cards from Problem 1.

One compact clean bracket locator below the example, with the separate full reusable mat beside the page. Three short agenda snapshots: target A/B/C, an empty waiting-option box and final-winner box; they are optional recoverable final records, not a distance/tally transcript. Give 165 × 35 mm workspace. Reset by returning **all** A/B/C option tiles to the input bank, leaving every voter card unchanged. The loser of one attempt may enter the next attempt; eliminated options are not globally erased from the family.

### Page 3 — design voting profiles; Problem 3

> **Problem 3:** Make three different ranking cards so that the same option wins every knockout agenda. Make another set where changing the agenda changes the winner.

Print three empty first/second/third card grids and a second three-grid reference row, each compact 28 × 54 mm, with six real 45 × 80 mm blank cards supplied separately. The shared rule that a ranking contains one copy of every option is stated once above these grids: `Each card must use A, B and C once.` This is essential legality, not a solving method. Both constructed profiles can remain visible without transcription. If the table uses only three erasable cards, the adult preserves the first three card words on its whiteboard before children rebuild. Space below for winners/brief argument. No demand to find every possible profile.

### Page 4 — nearest-location votes; Problem 4

New rules shared for Problems 4–7:

> Each favorite marker is one voter. Keep the favorite markers still during a vote. Each voter chooses the nearer of the two proposed locations. Equal distances go in Tie. More votes wins; equal vote counts give a tied vote.

Worked non-task visual, one voter V at favorite 2, proposals X at 0 and Y at 5. **Input:** V's favorite on the upper rail, X/Y on the lower rail. **Intermediate:** same tick line with two unit-step traces, V→X labelled `2 steps`, V→Y labelled `3 steps`. **Output:** V's vote token in X's well, V/X/Y markers still at the same locations. A second **small** tie visual is essential to the convention: V at 3, X at 1, Y at 5 → two 2-step traces → V token in Tie. Each worked visual is a 185 × 35 mm compact strip with three panels; the separate full working board carries the manipulatives. Do not show the five-voter winner in either example.

> **Problem 4:** Use the five favorites shown. Find a location that wins a vote against every other location on this line.

Matching compact 0–10 reference line, 185 × 45 mm: V1 at 0, V2 at 2, V3 at 4, V4 at 7, V5 at 10. On the separate large apparatus board all five favorite markers stay visible, two movable proposals go below, and the tally mat stays beside it. A blank 165 × 35 mm answer region and no printed comparison checklist. Candidate locations are exactly the printed integer ticks for this task; proposals cannot be at midpoints. Voter favorites remain unchanged while children test different opponents. The adult can retain a claimed candidate on X and change only Y, preventing a sequence of unrelated votes from being mistaken for an all-opponents test.

### Page 5 — general middle-favorite question; Problem 5

> **Problem 5:** Put five favorite markers anywhere on the line. Two voters may share a favorite. Does the third favorite from the left always beat every different location? Show why, or find a counterexample.

Clean compact 0–10 reference line, with the separate full working board and five parking lanes ready for repeated favorites. A compact supplied second line with favorites 1,4,4,4,9 is available to make repetition a concrete case; mark all three V IDs at 4 on separate small rows. This is one investigation rather than five separately directed tally problems. Third favorite counts all five markers, including repetitions; adult can help line them up in order without relocating their favorite positions. 165 × 65 mm workspace for a drawn three-voter coalition or adult-scribed explanation. The printed task ranges over integer favorite/proposal ticks; adult proof also covers arbitrary real locations on a line, provided the median favorite is an allowed proposal.

### Page 6 — even-voter encore; Problem 6

> **Problem 6:** Now there are four voters with the favorites shown. Find every location on the line that never loses a vote against another location. Is there a location that beats every other one?

0–10 board with V1=1,V2=3,V3=7,V4=9, four corresponding vote tokens, all ten possible different opponents available. V5 is removed only because this task explicitly has four voters. Candidate locations are integer ticks. A 165 × 55 mm workspace can hold a marked interval or the list of weak winners; no new term such as “weak Condorcet set” is required on the student page. Put a small identical clean board below if children want to circle their answer without altering the reusable working board. This page tests the boundary of the five-voter guarantee; do not turn a tied 2–2 result into a coin-flip elimination.

### Page 7 — model-limit encore; Problem 7

> **Problem 7:** Could all three ranking cards from Problem 1 come from nearest-location choices on one line? Put A, B and C at different locations and choose the voters' favorites, or explain why it cannot be done.

Three visibly supplied ABC/BCA/CAB cards, a 190 × 65 mm unnumbered line for placing three candidate tiles, and three favorite tokens. For this problem locations may be **anywhere on the line**, not only integer ticks; labels A/B/C denote proposals rather than favorite markers. Essential guidance: `A ranking puts nearer locations higher; equal distances tie and do not make a strict ranking.` This clarifies that the cards are strict rankings and ties cannot implement the requested order. No child has to calculate all six candidate orders by distance; leave 165 × 70 mm for rearrangements or a drawing explaining the middle-candidate obstruction. A line has no circular wraparound.

## Keyed facilitator solutions, hints and proofs

**Problem 1.** A/B: V1,V3 choose A, V2 chooses B → A 2–1. B/C: V1,V2 choose B, V3 chooses C → B 2–1. C/A: V2,V3 choose C, V1 chooses A → C 2–1. No option wins both contests. This is a complete exact result, not an experimental frequency; holding another poll with the same cards gives the same ballots. Held hint: cover the unavailable letter on a card, then let the child choose between the two remaining rows. Do not “count first places” for a pairwise election when a favorite is absent.

**Problem 2.** Waiting A: first B/C → B, then B/A → A. Waiting B: first C/A → C, then C/B → B. Waiting C: first A/B → A, then A/C → C. All three winners are attainable, and these are every possible bye choice. Ordering the two first-round input slots does not change that election, so there are three substantively different agendas, not six. The adult may introduce the word agenda only after the child has moved the waiting tile; no need to teach it as vocabulary first. If a ranking changes or an option stays eliminated across resets, return to the physical fixed data rather than debating the wrong game.

**Problem 3.** A profile with three different cards and fixed winner A is ABC, ACB, BAC. A beats B by 2–1 and C by 3–0. If A waits, it beats the first winner; if A plays first, it wins then beats the waiting option. Thus it wins every agenda. A new agenda-dependent profile is ACB, CBA, BAC; A beats C, C beats B, B beats A, each 2–1. All three waiting choices can again win, with the reversed cycle orientation. Any child's valid examples are accepted by testing the three pair votes and three bye choices. Optional explanation: a candidate beating each of the others cannot be eliminated. With three candidates, a strict majority tournament without such a candidate must be a three-cycle, so its bye can determine the winner. Do not generalize that short dichotomy to larger candidate sets.

**Problem 4.** The answer is 4. Against location q=0, V1 prefers 0, V2 is tied, V3/V4/V5 prefer 4: 3 votes for 4, 1 for 0, 1 tie. Against q=8, V1/V2/V3 prefer 4 and V4/V5 prefer 8: 3–2. Complete checked counts in the format `(votes for 4, votes for q, ties)` are q0=(3,1,1), q1=(3,2,0), q2=(3,2,0), q3=(3,2,0), q5=(3,2,0), q6=(3,2,0), q7=(3,2,0), q8=(3,2,0), q9=(3,2,0), q10=(3,1,1). These were recomputed by the planning checker. The third favorite, 4, is not the line midpoint 5 or the mean 23/5. For q<4, favorites 4,7,10 each strictly prefer 4; for q>4, favorites 0,2,4 each strictly prefer 4. This verifies every real q as well, though only integer q are on the student board. Any different proposed global winner loses directly to 4, proving uniqueness. Delay hint until games suggest a stable location: leave the tested candidate fixed and try a challenger on each side.

**Problem 5.** Yes. Sort all five markers including repeated positions, p1≤…≤p5, and let m=p3. If challenger q<m, the three markers p3,p4,p5 lie at or right of m. Their distance to m is p_i−m, whereas their distance to q is p_i−q, larger by m−q>0. If q>m, markers p1,p2,p3 lie at or left of m and strictly prefer m by the same subtraction. Equal-distance ties from the other two voters cannot prevent at least three votes for m. This proves the claim, not merely the two supplied examples. For favorites 1,4,4,4,9 the three voters at 4 themselves form the strict coalition against either side. A count proof can be drawn as three counters behind m while the challenger slides; no algebra is a child prerequisite. The middle favorite must be available as an option: if candidates exclude it, this statement does not claim a strict winner. General single-peaked preferences need a common order and unique peaks; their proof is an optional adult abstraction, not inferred from arbitrary ranking cards.

**Problem 6.** Every integer 3,4,5,6,7 never loses; none beats every other location. For x in [3,7], a challenger q<x is farther than x from favorites 7 and 9, so at least two voters strictly support x. A challenger q>x is farther than x from favorites 1 and 3, again giving at least two strict votes. At most the other two can oppose, so x cannot lose. Two different candidates inside [3,7] split the voters 2–2: favorites 1/3 support the smaller, favorites 7/9 the larger. Thus none is a strict universal winner. For a printed x<3, challenge at 3: favorites 3,7,9 strictly prefer 3, and it wins at least 3–1. For x>7, challenge at 7: favorites 1,3,7 strictly prefer 7. Therefore no other integer is a weak winner. The interval proof holds for arbitrary real x on this particular continuous line too, but students are asked for printed ticks. “Four is even” alone is not an argument that every pair vote ties: many outside-versus-inside votes are strict. Let physical examples precede the interval proof.

**Problem 7.** Impossible. In any placement of three distinct candidate locations, one candidate M is between the other two L and R. A favorite at or left of M is closer to M than to R; a favorite at or right of M is closer to M than to L. Thus M cannot be strictly worse than both endpoints for any nearest-distance voter. But every possible middle candidate is last in one of the supplied cards: A is last on BCA, B on CAB, C on ABC. Whichever is put in the middle contradicts that card. The proof needs no enumeration of six orders and no arithmetic beyond “the farther endpoint is beyond the middle.” If a favorite coincides with M, M is first, not last; if distances tie, the requested strict ranking still is not obtained. Held hint: let the child cover an endpoint and move a favorite through all parts of the line while looking at the middle candidate. No claim is made about voters on a circle, two-dimensional locations, arbitrary utility functions or actual human preferences.

## Review and production gates

- Fixed preferences and current proposal state must remain visible. Empty favorite position 0 still has V1 and gets one vote. A tie token does not disappear, and it is not counted as one vote for each candidate. Page 4's middle location wins by at least three strict votes even if an opponent pair has a tied voter.
- Mathematical risk: “a majority cycle proves Arrow's theorem” is not claimed; the profile only proves this finite cycle. Sincere ballots in the current pair and strategic anticipation across rounds are different models. The median proof needs a common line, distance preferences, fixed favorites and an available median choice. More voters alone does not fix arbitrary cycles.
- Operational risk: children may vote their personal first choice instead of reading an imaginary card, or move a favorite marker when changing a proposal.
