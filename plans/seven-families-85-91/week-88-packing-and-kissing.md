## Facilitator opening: mathematical destination

**Planar equal-disk kissing theorem.** At most six closed disks of radius r > 0 can have disjoint interiors and each touch a central disk of the same radius externally. Six are attainable, with their centers at the vertices of a regular hexagon at distance 2r from the central center. Any two outer centers are at least 2r apart, so consecutive center rays are separated by at least 60 degrees; their angles total 360 degrees. With six, every angle is exactly 60 degrees. Thus every consecutive pair of outer disks also touches. This theorem concerns one central disk and exact circles in a plane. It does not establish the best packing in a tray or an infinite-plane density.

**Centers translate physical constraints into distances.** Two externally tangent disks of radii R and r have center distance R + r; disjoint interiors require center distance at least that sum. For m congruent outer disks of radius r touching a central disk of radius R, the minimum neighboring angle is 2 arcsin(r/(R+r)). Hence the exact maximum is floor(pi/arcsin(r/(R+r))), with equality attained by an equally spaced ring. Our two changed-size kits have maxima nine (R=40 mm, r=20 mm) and four (R=20 mm, r=40 mm). The formula and trigonometry are adult background; children can give measured constructions and explain the equal-size case with equilateral triangles. With mixed outer sizes, this single-angle formula no longer applies. For the supplied 40/80 mm disks, requiring both sizes among the neighbors gives maxima eight around the large center and five around the small center; P7 supplies a separate, optional adult angle proof. Child entry remains checked construction and improvement.

**Packing construction, not optimality.** Eleven diameter-40 mm disks fit in a 160 × 110 mm rectangle by rows of 4, 3, 4. Adjacent rows are 20√3 mm apart, so their total height is 40 + 40√3 ≈ 109.282 mm. Eight fit in two square-grid rows. Finding eleven is an exact construction; this plan supplies no proof that eleven is globally optimal. The late task asks which demonstrated arrangement fits a shorter tray, not for a theorem about all packings.

Children first experiment with touch and overlap, conjecture a maximum, then explain an upper bound in the equal-size case. Grades K–1 have an optional concrete touch-and-fit route; grades 2–3 can rearrange rings and trays; grades 4–5 can translate to centers, prove the six-disk bound, and scrutinize which conclusions experiments support. The unequal-size and tray investigations are separate continuations. A three-dimensional demonstration, if later selected, would establish a construction of twelve spheres only; no elementary upper-bound proof or Kepler-density claim belongs here.

## Kernel-only brief for later worksheet production

1. Nonoverlap becomes a lower bound on center distance. Around an equal central disk, a 60-degree minimum separation yields an exact local maximum of six and rigidity at equality.
2. Staggering changes finite packing efficiency. Exact configurations can be verified by distance and boundary checks without conflating a good construction with a proved global optimum.
3. Changing a radius changes the center circle and required angular separation. Congruence of the outer pieces matters to the simple ring theorem.

Suggested emphasis: K–1, optional externally touching rings and container fits after one hearing; 2–3, contrast local kissing and packing into a tray; 4–5, center-distance certificates, exact equal-size upper bound and qualified size comparisons. No K–1 proof variant is required.

## Common launch and first visual bridge

After two minutes handling the pieces, place one central disk on an unmarked mat. Put one equal disk tangent, one overlapping, and one separated. Children identify the legal touching piece; a partner checks every later placement. Say: “The outside pieces all have to touch this one. They may touch each other, but they cannot cover each other.” Stop before surrounding it.

On the proof page, before first using centers, print a non-task visual at equal x/y scale: **input** two tangent circles with radii 15 mm and 25 mm; **middle** their centers joined through the touching point, with the two segments labeled 15 and 25; **output** the single center segment labeled 40 mm. Keep this three-panel bridge above P3. It teaches center distance without giving the equal-disk ring theorem. Angle language is demonstrated at the table with a right-angle corner and one full turn, if needed.

## Facilitator solutions, reasoning and optional prompts

**P1.** Six fit at center coordinates `(40 cos(kπ/3), 40 sin(kπ/3))`, k=0,…,5, measured relative to the central center. Any neighboring center distance is 40 mm. Seven cannot fit by P3. Five can be evenly spaced with 72-degree gaps (outside neighbors separated); or delete one disk from the hexagon, giving four 60-degree gaps and one 120-degree gap. These are two different arrangements even after rotation. Hands-on success can stop at a good construction; the adult does not need to announce optimality yet. Hint if stuck: allow touching between outside disks. Estimate 10–20 minutes.

**P2.** A checked 11-disk construction has centers x=20,60,100,140 at y=20 and y=20+40√3; middle-row centers x=40,80,120 at y=20+20√3. Same-row distances are 40; nearest cross-row distances are √(20²+(20√3)²)=40. Every center is at least 20 mm from all four boundaries. The bounding height is ≈109.282 mm. Children need not calculate the square root: pieces certify a practical construction after the precision rehearsal. Other counts above eight meet the printed task. Do not mark a child's ten-disk solution wrong for failing to find eleven. Optional prompt: move a whole row rather than rebuilding individual placements. Estimate 10–25 minutes.

**P3.** Let O be the central center and A,B consecutive outer centers. OA=OB=40 mm. Nonoverlap gives AB≥40 mm. At AB=40, OAB is equilateral, so angle AOB is 60 degrees. Shorter angular separation gives a shorter chord and overlap; hence each circular gap is at least 60 degrees. Seven gaps need at least 420 degrees, impossible in one turn. An adult can supply a congruent equilateral triangle wedge and show that making its vertex angle smaller makes its opposite side shorter. This last monotonicity can be explained by rigid radii and a visual chord; it must not be replaced by the false assertion that all three center distances are equal for any nonoverlapping pair. A wedge arrangement plus the one-turn constraint is a concrete proof. Estimate 10–20 minutes after substantial ring work.

**P4.** The six angle gaps total 360 and are each at least 60; if one were larger, another would be smaller. Thus all equal 60 and every neighboring center distance is 40. In the ideal model every neighboring outer pair touches. Slight physical gaps reflect cutting error, not a counterexample. This is rigidity at the sharp bound. Ask only as needed: could one larger gap leave enough room for the other five? Estimate 8–15 minutes.

**P5.** Central radius 40 with outer radius 20: all outer centers lie on radius 60. Required angle is 2 arcsin(1/3)≈38.942 degrees. Nine equally spaced disks have angle 40 and fit; ten would need 389.42 degrees. Central radius 20 with outer radius 40: center radius 60 again, but minimum angle is 2 arcsin(2/3)≈83.621 degrees. Four at right angles fit; five cannot. The common center radius but different outer diameters makes a useful contrast. Students may report checked lower bounds; a mathematically trained adult may explain the upper bounds if trigonometry or a suitable geometric comparison is wanted. Do not label an experimental eight-small-disk ring “proved best.” Estimate 15–25 minutes.

**P6.** Every straight row of four disks in a 160 mm tray must have centers exactly at x=20,60,100,140: the three between-center distances are at least 40 and their available span is only 120. Every middle-row center lies from x=20 to 140, hence is at most 20 mm horizontally from some center in each four-disk row. To keep the center distance at least 40, the vertical gap to each four-disk row is at least √(40²−20²)=20√3. The tray must therefore have height at least 20+20√3+20√3+20≈109.282 mm. The 108 mm tray is impossible under the printed row restriction. The P2 construction attains the bound. Keep the distinction explicit: this proves the best height for this row arrangement, not for all finite disk packings. Children can make the supporting equilateral-center triangle and compare its altitude with the tray's available 34 mm row gap; the exact radical calculation is adult background. Estimate 10–20 minutes; optional.

**P7.** Many legal mixed rings are acceptable. A safe starter is four 80 mm outer disks around the 40 mm center at 90-degree intervals (outer-center radius 60 mm), then replace one by a 40 mm disk touching the center along the same ray (new center radius 40 mm). Its distances to the two adjacent large-disk centers are √(60²+40²)≈72.11 mm, greater than the required 60 mm. This makes a checked mixed ring with three large neighbors and one small neighbor. Another construction uses the 80 mm center: begin with six 40 mm outer disks at 60-degree intervals, then replace one with an 80 mm disk along the same ray; its distances to its neighbors become √(80²+60²−2·80·60·cos60°)=√5200≈72.11 mm, again greater than 60. Children can improve either starter experimentally. Use the geometry condition `distance between centers ≥ sum of their radii` to validate a claimed ring. Optional adult question: which pair consumes the largest angular gap? Variable radii require different angles for different pairs. Estimate 15–30 minutes, possibly another visit.

**Optional adult completion of P7's maximum question.** Interpret “using both sizes” as both sizes among the outside disks, irrespective of the middle disk. Around the radius-40 center, place one radius-40 neighbor at angle 0 and seven radius-20 neighbors at 50,90,130,170,210,250,290 degrees; large and small center radii are 80 and 60. This eight-neighbor construction fits the inventory. Small-small gaps need at least α=2 arcsin(1/3)≈38.942 degrees, and a large-small gap needs β=arccos(2/3)≈48.190 degrees. A large-large gap needs 60 degrees, also at least β. In any mixed nine-neighbor ring, the two gaps incident to a selected large neighbor are at least β, and the other seven are at least α. Their required total is 2β+7α≈368.976 degrees, impossible; more neighbors only increase it. Thus eight is exact. Around the radius-20 center, one radius-40 neighbor at 0 and four radius-20 neighbors at 80,140,200,260 degrees attain five. Small-small gaps need 60 degrees; gaps incident to a large neighbor need at least arccos(1/3)≈70.529 degrees. Six neighbors would require at least 2·70.529+4·60≈381.058 degrees. Thus five is exact. All-pair distances of both witnesses are checked independently. This is adult background for a ready continuation, not an expectation that children know inverse trigonometry or finish a proof during their construction visit.
