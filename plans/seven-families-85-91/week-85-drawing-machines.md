## Theorem-first facilitator overview

**The mechanism is a dilation of scale 2 about its fixed anchor.** The ideal planar machine has a long bar O–X–Q with X its midpoint, a second long bar Q–Y–P with Y its midpoint, and short bars X–T and T–Y. All four sides of X–Q–Y–T have the same length L. On the simple, non-crossed parallelogram branch, O, T and P are collinear, in that order, and OP = 2 OT. Equivalently, with O as the origin and directed half-bars u and v, X = u, Q = 2u, Y = 2u + v, T = u + v and P = 2u + 2v. This proves the identity for every permitted pose, not merely the tested drawings. Crossing or collapsing the linkage is outside this operating assumption.

**Corresponding lengths double, angles remain equal, and area quadruples.** For two tracer positions T1 and T2, P2 − P1 = 2(T2 − T1). The same statement gives straightness, parallelism, unchanged orientation and twice every segment length. A square of side a becomes one of side 2a; four translated a-by-a squares tile it. Triangles and the supplied L-shapes can be dissected into square or half-square units, giving the same factor 4. This is a claim about the ideal planar dilation, not a claim that a rough physical tracing has exact measured lengths or areas.

**The machine can shrink and changing the anchor changes the copy's position.** Interchanging tracer and pencil gives factor 1/2 and area factor 1/4. For a fixed physical tracer point T and two anchor positions O and O′, P = 2T − O and P′ = 2T − O′, so P′ − P = −(O′ − O). The copy shifts opposite the anchor displacement; its shape and size are unchanged. These are distinct return-visit directions: area, inversion, and the center of a dilation.

Children first make and compare copies; their measurements are experiments. A suggestion that the line-up and scale are universal is a conjecture until the frame argument explains it. The approximate grades 2–3 route reaches substantial drawing and inverse-control tasks. The approximate grades 4–5 route can explain the doubling with directed strips or similar triangles and investigate area and centers. A young child may enjoy a supported prebuilt station, but no independent K–1 packet is proposed: fine motor control and the mechanism's recovery procedure are real prerequisites.

## Kernel-only brief for later worksheet production

1. A pantograph built with the explicit midpoint topology above realizes P = O + 2(T − O) on the intended branch. Its dilation law follows from adding the two directed half-bars, or from parallel sides and similar triangles. The working region is restricted away from collapsed poses and mechanical collisions.
2. A dilation of factor 2 doubles lengths and multiplies area by 4. Swapping input and output realizes the inverse. The anchor is the center: moving it while holding the input picture fixed translates the output by the opposite displacement.

Suggested emphasis: K–1, optional supported tracing only after a successful hardware trial; grades 2–3, control a real copying machine and solve an inverse drawing task; grades 4–5, explain the linkage identity, then compare area and the effect of changing the anchor. These are prerequisites, not assignments by age.

Materials: two rigid long bars with hole centers 160 mm apart and midpoint holes at 80 mm, two rigid short bars with hole centers 80 mm apart; a fixed pivot at O; pivots at X, Q and Y; a freely rotating coaxial tracer hinge at T; one coaxial output pen mount at P; a tabletop board and 100%-scale joined print mats. Exact mounting, spacers and branch clearance must be rehearsed before production. The later writer may choose a different problem sequence while retaining these kernels and constraints.

## Common launch and the first convention visual

Allow about three minutes to move an assembled linkage gently with the pens lifted. Then fix O and demonstrate one legal short tracing action, with children close enough to see the pencil move. Say: “This point stays here. You move the tracer; the other pencil makes the copy. Stop and ask if the bars get stuck.” Give each child a brief turn. Keep the scale theorem for their attempts.

The first page's unnumbered shared rule is: **“Keep O fixed. Move the tracer T; the pencil P makes the copy. Stop if the bars get stuck.”** The same names and colors appear on the physical machine and every mat. Mounting instructions, pair routine and safety belong in the guide, not the student rules.

Before Problem 1, place a small three-panel visual across a 160 × 36 mm strip: **Input:** an assembled open linkage, O as a filled anchor symbol, T as a blue hollow circle, P as a red pencil point; the tracer touches a small gray dot. **Move:** a hand symbol moves T along a tiny curved stroke; O remains on the same anchor symbol, and P is shown midway through its own stroke. **Copy:** the two short strokes are visible with the same T and P color labels. No distances, ratios or grids are printed in this convention example. It teaches which point is controlled and which mark is produced on a non-task stroke. Full-page input shapes below it are different. The adult should show the actual action too; the illustration alone is not a motor-control lesson.

## First-visit solutions and adult notes

**P1.** In the ideal machine, the copies are a straight segment 60 mm long; an elbow with lengths 40 and 30 mm and the same corner angle; and a 40 × 30 mm rectangle with the same orientation. Their positions relative to O also double. Physical copies will be approximate. If a child sees rotation, mirroring or inconsistent scale, check a wrong branch, displaced anchor or off-axis tip before treating it as mathematical evidence. Let children repeat favorite paths rather than rushing into measurements. An adult may ask which point drew the larger mark after comparing overlays. Suggested 10–15 minutes.

**P2.** No permitted ideal pose is a counterexample: P = 2T relative to O. The line O–T–P and ratio 2 remain, as do parallelism and shape scaling for complete paths. Experiments suggest these facts; a successful finite set of poses does not prove them universally. Hints in escalating order: use a loose straightedge; stretch a string from O to P; compare the two portions with the same short paper strip. Do not print these as directions on the page. A child can spend the whole remaining first visit here. Suggested 8–15 minutes.

**P3.** The house's tracer vertices are (70, −10), (90, −10), (90, 10), (80, 17.5), (70, 10). The flag uses half of each listed coordinate. These are all safe ideal positions. Children need not write coordinates: guiding P onto the target while controlling T creates the input path directly. Correctly recognizing that the tracer path is a half-size house/flag is the intended conclusion. A child who simply drags P has not done the inverse-control task; the partner checks that the hand remains on T. If precision consumes the mathematics, accept approximate tracing and compare its shape. Suggested 8–15 minutes.

**P4.** Let a directed strip u run from O to X. Because X is the midpoint, another u takes X to Q. Let v run from Q to Y. The second long bar contributes two copies of v. The short bars and parallelogram branch make X to T the same directed v, and T to Y the same directed u. Thus reaching T uses u then v; reaching P uses u,u,v,v. Doubling both displacements gives the same endpoint as doubling the entire O-to-T displacement. Consequently P lies along the O-to-T line at twice its length. This is a proof without requiring written vector notation.

Alternatively, triangles OXT and OQP have OX = OQ/2, XT = QP/2 and parallel corresponding directions, so are similar with factor 2; their remaining sides are OT and OP. The assumptions matter: X must be a true midpoint, all listed joints must be attached as specified, and XQYT must be the simple parallelogram. Equal-sided crossed configurations or singular collapsed poses are excluded by the operating setup. Let children first identify the actual four-sided frame; the adult can place duplicated paper strips on a whiteboard. Do not insist on a formal similarity theorem if it is unfamiliar. Suggested 10–20 minutes, or a separate return conversation.

**Encore P1 solution.** Copies have areas 16, 12 and 8 square units, so the factor is four, not two. Subdivide a doubled square into a 2-by-2 array of original-size squares. Each original unit, including a half-square, becomes four congruent units of its former area; the L-shape and triangle inherit the factor. These finite dissections explain these three shapes. The general polygon statement follows by triangulation/dissection; it is an optional facilitator extension, not a demanded general proof. The physical fill may be inaccurate because of drawing errors; the exact ideal shapes on grids permit the argument. Suggested 15–25 minutes.

**Encore P2 solution.** The copies are 30 × 20 mm and 40 × 20 mm, respectively, with unchanged angles and orientation. Swapping points solves T = O + (P − O)/2. The apparent larger hand movement and smaller copied path now have a concrete purpose. Doubling once then shrinking once restores lengths and areas in the ideal model; the physical overlay may not return exactly because of slop. This is an inverse transformation. Suggested 10–15 minutes.

**Encore P3 solution.** Relative to the fixed physical input, the second copy translates 30 mm left: P′ = 2T − O′ = (2T − O) − (30,0). It keeps side lengths, angles, orientation and area. The two copies cannot coincide while both the input and distinct anchors are fixed. If the input is also shifted, translating it 15 mm right compensates: 2(T + (15,0)) − (O + (30,0)) = 2T − O. Let children first see two stable overlays and propose a displacement; adult arithmetic or a ruler can check. This is a genuine center/composition investigation. Suggested 15–25 minutes. It is upper readiness work, not a compulsory final page.

## Risk gates and verification required before production

1. **Correct geometry:** exact midpoint centers and an open parallelogram branch are part of the theorem. Do not claim every arbitrary equal-bar assembly is a 2:1 pantograph. The crossed branch, full extension and collapse require adult resetting.
2. **Motor and hardware demand:** an untested sleeve or pen offset can dominate the hour. Complete the explicit physical trial before presenting printable hardware as ready. Preassembly is assumed; child assembly is a later engineering activity with a separate risk/parts plan.
3. **Reading and executive function:** color-matched points and one hand-controlled point externalize the rule. A partner checks the anchor. Adults read and preserve overlays; children need no live measurement table. If adults must continually operate both points, return to a simpler station.
4. **Measurement versus proof:** exact digital coordinates verify an ideal mechanism, not accuracy of hand-made traces. Keep experimentation, the universal frame proof and unrehearsed physical accuracy distinct.
5. **Representation load:** introduce u/v strips only in the adult proof conversation. The student page's physical half-bars and labeled joints are sufficient. Half-square area units receive their own visual before the triangle; coordinates remain builder/adult data.
6. **Page fit:** later production must render and inspect all pages and joined mats, verify 100% registration, target containment, 80/160 mm hole spacing and the 10 mm unit system, then inspect a clean extracted-package rebuild. None of those PDF or ZIP checks has been performed because production was not requested.

Use log fields for later visits: date; page/problem and exact insert used; child-selected route; hardware version; observed control and recovery; what children conjectured/explained; unfinished questions. Record observations separately from hypotheses. Do not mark this family piloted merely because an adult has rehearsed the machine.
