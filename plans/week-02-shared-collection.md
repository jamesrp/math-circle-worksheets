## Common launch, stable adult coverage, flexible work

Current roster: **10 children, KK1 / 3333 / 445; three adults, two mathematicians and one non-mathematician parent.** These are planning facts reported September 28, not reconstructed attendance at a prior meeting.

The initial adult/table assignments support classroom management. They do not restrict the pages available to a child. Keep adults and seats stable while pages travel. Give one or two choices at a time; do not create ten simultaneous independent lesson plans or mandatory rotating stations. If the parent needs mathematical support, the organizer first asks the other mathematician to cover the two older tables, then visits briefly. The parent can keep children working on a familiar board or drawing task meanwhile.

Suggested timing:

| Minutes | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Handle counters, or draw and erase freely on paper/tablets. |
| 5–10 | Whole-group demonstration: start all OFF, change both ends of one road, then repeat to undo. Also show an ON/OFF move so “change” does not become “always turn on.” Let a child replay. |
| 10–30 | Select a useful route. Use solo attempts, partner puzzles, or rotating three-child move roles. |
| 30–35 | Stand, stretch, and reset. |
| 35–50 | Continue a worthwhile investigation, change difficulty, or choose a drawing task. |
| 50–55 | Share one attempt, invention, or discovery. |
| 55–60 | Tidy. |

After a child can make and undo a legal move, the adult can skip familiar examples. Reading speed and writing pace must not decide access to mathematical ideas. A child can point, act, or dictate while the adult records. A successful move, an invented puzzle, a useful comparison, or an explanation with a drawing can be a complete session outcome.

## Mathematical substance retained

The shared collection preserves the earlier investigations:

- A pair move toggles both endpoints; repeating a road twice cancels. Paths leave only their endpoints ON when starting all OFF.
- On a connected graph, exactly even targets are reachable. On a disconnected graph, every component must have an even target count. Pairing targets and connecting each pair proves sufficiency.
- The four-ring has exactly eight reachable states: all OFF, all six pairs, and all four ON. The twelve recording spaces do not give away the answer; an explicit “saved” mark distinguishes a deliberately recorded all-OFF state from an unused box.
- A cycle has exactly two reduced solutions for each reachable target, and they are complementary. Choosing the first edge forces the other decisions. On the five-ring one solution has at most two presses; on the six-ring complementary reduced solutions can tie. Different unreduced or reordered lists can tie on either ring, and Problem 24 accepts “same.”
- Every edge of a tree is forced by parity on one side of its cut. The unique reduced solution is shortest. This argument is given in full in the guide.
- Distinct full walls in a square create regions: two walls yield three or four rooms; three walls yield four, five, six, or seven. Full-line arrangements admit two room marks, while the T map's three mutually adjacent rooms require three.

“Not yet” records an unsuccessful search; it is not an impossibility proof. The guide provides the complete arguments but does not require children to write them. Setup by hand is explicitly distinguished from a legal move from all OFF. All large working lamp boards are blank so printed dots never prevent erasing.

The shared four-ring uses the same orientation throughout: 1 top, 2 right, 3 bottom, 4 left. This rotates the former auxiliary square drawing without changing its edges or solution lists. The island diagrams retain their own clearly pictured labels in the adult key.

## Source findings and our adaptations

Relevant local passages were read during this revision:

- **Math Circle by the Bay**, preface, printed pp. viii–x, especially p. x (PDF page 11), describes using the same material across ages at different pace and depth, with younger groups sometimes proceeding as quickly. It supports flexible entry; it does not establish our particular staffing arrangement.
- **Natasha Rozhkovskaya, Math Circles for Elementary School Students**, Lesson 3, “At the lesson,” item 1 (`OEBPS/part0013.xhtml`), reports children trying and explaining before a table was introduced, and reserve questions prepared for mixed experience. The order of action, recording, and explanation here is an adaptation.
- The same book, **Lesson 7**, “At the lesson,” items 1–2 (`part0017.xhtml`), describes missed legal moves affecting a game investigation, and sustained engagement in open polygon construction with K’nex. Our explicit pair-move launch responds to the former; paper polygon invention is our adaptation of the latter, not an experimentally established equivalent.
- **Lesson 8**, “At the lesson,” item 2 (`part0018.xhtml`), describes writing and coloring pace dividing the group. Adult recording and readiness-based page selection are our responses to that observation.
- **Lowell year 1, Handout 11, Problem 11.3**, provides continuity through invented split/recombined partner pictures; **Handout 9, Problem 9.1**, uses door toggles. Availability of these handouts does not establish which children used them.

Actual local classroom observations remain in the [Week 1 classroom review](week-01-classroom-review.md). The organizer's September 28 feedback concerns the printed presentation and the proposed shared format. It is not a classroom trial of these new pages.
