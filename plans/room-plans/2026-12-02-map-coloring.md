## Why this is more than "color some maps"

James's worry (November 20) was that map coloring is only "color some islands" and "find one that needs four". The hour is built from five different kinds of experience, so the children are not doing the same thing five times:

1. **A trick that always works.** Scribble any lines from edge to edge, or loops that close up, as wild as you like: two colors always do it. Children try to break it and can't, then find exactly what breaks it (a line that stops partway).
2. **An odd-and-even rule.** A ring of countries round a lake needs 2 colors if the ring is even and 3 if it is odd; put a country in the middle and it needs one more. The room acts this out at minute 28 as a human ring.
3. **A drawing challenge that keeps failing.** Four countries that all touch each other: easy. Five: nobody can. "Draw a map that needs five colors" is a theorem children can attack all afternoon (the four color theorem, proved in 1976 with a computer).
4. **A game with a sneaky strategy.** Painter and Spoiler take turns coloring a tic-tac-toe map. It only needs 2 colors, yet with 3 colors the Spoiler can always win. With 4 the Painter always can. That is the finale, on the floor.
5. **A world where the answer changes.** On a donut-shaped world (edges wrap like an old video game) seven countries can all touch each other, so some maps need 7 colors. For the readiest 2–5 children, late in the packet.

## For adults

The rule: countries that share a border get different colors; countries that meet only at a corner point may match.

- **Scribble maps (K–1 Problems 1–3, 2–5 Problems 1, 2 and 8).** Lines that run edge to edge and loops that close up always make a two-color map. Why, in children's words: crossing any one line switches you from one side of it to the other, so color each country by whether you crossed an even or odd number of lines to get there. A line that stops partway breaks it: a "T" or "Y" of lines makes three countries that all touch (2–5 Problem 3, K–1 Problem 4). K–1 Problem 10 asks them to draw maps that need 3 and 4 pencils: a "Y" and a flower with 5 petals are the quick answers.
- **Rings (K–1 Problems 5–6, 2–5 Problems 4–5).** A ring round a lake: even needs 2, odd needs 3, because colors going round must alternate. A middle country touches the whole ring, so add one: middle with an even ring needs 3, with an odd ring needs 4. So a ring of 20 needs 2, a ring of 25 needs 3; a middle with a ring of 100 needs 3, with 101 needs 4.
- **All-touching countries (2–5 Problems 9–10).** Four countries that all touch: a center with three around it. Five is impossible on a flat page. Problem 10's map (a middle with a ring of 5) needs four colors but has no four countries that all touch: any four of them include two ring countries that are not next to each other. Needing four colors is not the same as having four all-touching countries.
- **Needs five (2–5 Problem 11).** Every flat map can be colored with four colors (the four color theorem; Appel and Haken 1976, with a computer). Nobody will win; let them try, and tell them adults know it is impossible only after a good attempt. Real example: the US map needs four because Nevada has a ring of 5 neighbors.
- **Painter and Spoiler (K–1 Problems 7–9, 2–5 Problems 6–7, finale).** Players take turns coloring a country with a color that does not clash; the Spoiler wins if some empty country gets stuck (its neighbors already use every color). Checked by computer over every line of play:
  - K–1, a strip of 4 squares with 2 colors: the Spoiler wins when the Painter goes first (Problem 7), the Painter wins when the Spoiler goes first (Problem 8). A ring of 4 round a pond (Problem 9) is the same: whoever goes second can always win. Spoiler's trick: color the square two away from the Painter's in the other color; the square between is stuck.
  - Tic-tac-toe map with 3 colors: the Spoiler wins whoever goes first. The idea: get three different colors around one empty square, often the middle. With 4 colors the Painter wins whoever goes first.
- **Donut world (2–5 Problems 12–14).** Leaving one edge brings you back on the opposite edge. Problem 12's boards need 2 (four stripes), 3 (three stripes), 2 (4-by-4 grid) and 3 (3-by-3 grid): on the donut, three stripes wrap into an odd ring, though flat they need only 2 (checked). Problem 13 asks for 5, then 6, all-touching countries; 7 is the most. Problem 14's 7-country map has every country touching all six others (21 pairs, checked), so it needs 7 colors. A tube (left and right joined only) gives nothing new; the top-and-bottom join is what makes the donut.

## Opener: paint the island (8 minutes)

A soft logic puzzle on the tables as children arrive. An adult reads the clues aloud at any table that wants it. The rule is the session's rule, so the opener is also the first explanation of "shares a border".

**The puzzle (most of the room).** Six countries, each red, blue or yellow; countries that share a border differ, countries that touch only at a corner may match.

1. Ant Land is red.
2. Ant Land and Fox Land are different colors.
3. Cat Land and Eel Land are the same color.
4. Exactly two countries are blue.

**Answer:** Ant red, Bear blue, Cat yellow, Dog blue, Eel yellow, Fox yellow. Eel and Fox are both yellow and touch only at a corner, which is allowed. It is the only answer and every clue is needed (checked, [paint-the-island_check.py](openers/paint-the-island_check.py)).

**Hint ladder** (one step, then walk away):

1. "Which countries touch Ant Land? Can they be red?" (Bear, Cat and Dog are not red.)
2. "Cat and Dog touch. What colors are left for them?" (One is blue, one is yellow.)
3. "Fox touches Dog and isn't red. So Fox matches which country?" (Cat. Then Cat, Eel and Fox share a color; with only two blues, that color is yellow.)

**Easier (younger children).** Four countries in a 2-by-2 square. Ant Land is red; Bear Land is blue; Dog Land is not red. *Answer:* Ant red, Bear blue, Cat blue, Dog yellow.

**Harder (anyone done in two minutes).** The same island with no country's color given: Ant Land and Fox Land are different colors; exactly one country is red; exactly two are blue. *Answer:* the same as the main puzzle.

**Early finishers.** "Cross out clue 4. How many answers now? Write a new clue that makes it one again." (Two answers: the main one, and the one with blue and yellow swapped everywhere except Ant. "Bear Land is blue" fixes it, and so do others.)

**At the whiteboard (minute 6).** Solvers call out each country's color while an adult colors the big copy. Then: "Could we paint this island with only two colors?" Let the room argue, then point at Ant, Cat and Dog: all three touch each other, so no. That question, how few colors a map needs, is today's.

## If it goes sideways

- A table finishes early: hand out its early-finisher card.
- A child says "two colors don't work" on a scribble map: "Show me the two countries that clash." Usually one was miscolored; if a line stops partway, that's the discovery 2–5 Problem 3 and K–1 Problem 4 want.
- K–1 coloring is slow: point to the page's rule, a quick scribble in each country is enough, or have partners split the map; the question is whether two pencils are enough, not finishing. For pacing, the four maps of K–1 Problems 1–2 have about 30 countries in all, two or three minutes of scribbling for a pair.
- K–1 lines in Problem 3 come out wobbly or stop short: that is fine. A line that stops in the middle of a country changes nothing (it does not split anything), and a wobbly line is still a line. Only a line that stops exactly on another line makes a "T" that needs a third pencil, which is Problem 4's surprise arriving early: say so. For a child who can't steer a line to the edge, say "keep going until you bump the side", let them do the loops box first (any closed scribble works), or let a partner hold the page.
- The human ring gets silly: "Pencils down" signal works here too (sheets on heads), then go straight to the middle-country step.
- Finale: with 3 colors the Spoilers can always win whoever goes first, and with 4 the Painters always can (checked). Do not say so; ask afterwards "Which team would you rather be with three colors? With four?"
- No floor space: play the finale on the whiteboard with markers in a 3-by-3 grid.
- Jay and Rose: if they have seen map coloring, make them referees in the finale and ring counters at 0:28, who may say "try again" but not why.
