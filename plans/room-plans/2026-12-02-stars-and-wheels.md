## Why this follows bridges

Bridges asked whether one walk could cross every bridge, and in some towns a counter was left behind however you walked. Today the walk has no choices at all: every move is the same hop around a ring of dots. Sometimes one start reaches every dot and draws a star; sometimes the pencil is back early and has to start again at a bare dot. It is the same kind of question (can one trip reach everything, and if not, how many trips?), and again the answer is a number the children can learn to find before they draw. It also closes a loop with November 4's shuffle machines: a hop is a machine that moves every cube the same number of places, and its loops are today's starts, all the same length.

For adults: number the n dots 0 to n − 1. Hopping k from any dot comes back after n ÷ g hops, where g is the biggest number that goes into both n and k (gcd(n, k)). So it takes exactly g starts, each a copy of the first turned one dot along, and one start reaches every dot exactly when g = 1. On a prime number of dots every hop does; on any other number some hop fails. Hop k and hop n − k draw the same lines the other way round (on 12 dots, hop 8 is hop 4 backwards). Why, in the children's terms: the first start lands only on the g-times table, so the dots in between each need their own start; and every start is the first one turned, so all starts have the same number of dots and share the n dots out in equal piles. The code wheel is the same ring with 26 places: a setting is a hop, two settings in a row add (going round past Z), and a setting and its undo add to 26. K–1's picture ring is a ring of 6, where a hop and the hop that undoes it add to 6.

What the children should find: K–1, which hops land on every dot on rings of 4 to 7, and starting again at a bare dot. Grades 2–5, a guess for the number of starts with a reason ("hop 3 on 12 only lands on 0, 3, 6 and 9"), and that the popular guess "starts = hop" fails for hop 5 on 12; readier children go on to the rule, test it on big rings without drawing, and say why. On the wheel, everyone decodes and writes codes for a partner; readier children crack codes with no setting given and find the setting that undoes another. The opener and the finale are this ring made of people: seats counted clockwise are dots, and a note passed 2 seats along is a hop of 2.

## Before the children arrive

- **The ring of chairs**, in open floor with room around it for a sitting circle of about 12 (8 to 10 feet across) for the finale. Five chairs in a circle, facing in. The star sheet on the floor in front of one chair: that is the star seat. The animal signs on the floor beside the chairs. A sixth chair to one side, for the midpoint.
- **The launch board**, propped by the chairs: a ring of 5 dots as big as the board allows, the top dot filled in, a clockwise arrow inside. The filled dot is the star seat. One counter: the note.
- On every table: opener half-sheets and pencils, so the first child in starts on the puzzle; the table card, first side out; that level's pages face down; coloured pencils and rulers; the counters. The early-finisher card stays with the host. The 2–5 hosts keep scissors, fasteners and wheel pages until the wheel.
- The yarn, scissors and timer by the chairs.
- **Hosts.** One adult per table, all hour. The organizer also leads the launch, the floor moves and the time. A spare adult floats: the timer, late arrivals, and a 2–5 table while it cuts wheels.
- **Table cards.** Fold a sheet into a tent and write big. The first side faces the children until the midpoint; then the host turns the card round. A child who walks up mid-round reads the card (or the host reads it) and joins a pair.
  - K–1, first side (draw a ring of 5 dots with an arrow): "Hop the counter. Say *one* on the next dot. Does it land on every dot before it is back? If a dot has no line, start again there."
  - K–1, second side (draw the ring of six pictures): "A hop moves every picture along the ring. What does the secret hop do? Which hop changes it back?"
  - 2–5, first side: "Draw hop 3: a straight line to the dot 3 places clockwise. Keep hopping until you are back. A dot with no line? Start again there. How many starts? Can you tell before you draw?"
  - 2–5, second side: "Code wheel. Set to D: inner A next to outer D. CAT goes into code as FDW. To decode, find the code letter on the outer wheel. Crack the codes, then make one for your partner."
- **Early-finisher cards**, one per table:
  - K–1: "Make a ring of 8 counters on the table. Which hops land on every counter before you are back?" (1, 3, 5 and 7.)
  - 2–5: "Draw 12 dots like a clock. Which hops from 1 to 11 reach every dot without starting again?" (1, 5, 7 and 11; hop 7 is hop 5 the other way.) "Harder: find a ring and a hop that make 7 starts, each one a five-pointed star." (35 dots with hop 14 or 21. Hops 7 and 28 give 7 pentagons.)

## Opener: the round table (8 minutes)

A logic puzzle on the tables as children arrive, then acted out on the five chairs. No clue but the first places anyone on its own: the other three only pin the seating down together. The seats are counted clockwise, the way the hops are, and the answer's note passed 2 seats along is the launch's hop of 2, so the room goes straight from the puzzle into the launch. An adult reads the clues aloud at any table that wants it.

**The puzzle (most of the room).** Five animals sat around a round table. Write each animal's name in its seat: Bear, Fox, Owl, Rabbit, Turtle. Clockwise is the way the arrow goes; to go 2 seats clockwise, call the next seat *one* and the seat after it *two*.

1. Rabbit sat in the star seat.
2. Fox sat 2 seats clockwise from Owl.
3. Bear sat 2 seats clockwise from Fox.
4. Owl sat right next to Turtle.

**Answer:** clockwise from the star seat, Rabbit, Bear, Owl, Turtle, Fox. It is the only answer, and every clue is needed (checked by computer, [round-table_check.py](openers/round-table_check.py)).

**Hint ladder** (give one step, then walk away):

1. "Which clue tells you exactly where one animal sat? Write that one first." (Rabbit, in the star seat.)
2. "Try Owl in the first seat after the star seat. Where do Fox and Bear go?" (Fox three seats after the star, and then Bear lands in the star seat, which is Rabbit's. Owl three seats after the star fails too: Fox lands in the star seat. So Owl is two or four seats after the star.)
3. "Owl is two or four seats after the star. In which one can Turtle sit next to Owl?" (Two. With Owl four seats after the star, Owl's neighbours are Bear and Rabbit.)

**Easier (younger children, or anyone stuck at step 1).** Four seats. Turtle sat in the star seat. Owl sat across the table from Turtle. Fox sat 1 seat clockwise from Turtle. *Answer:* clockwise from the star seat, Turtle, Fox, Owl, Bear. *Done?* "Turtle passes a note 2 seats clockwise, and on it goes. Who never gets it?" (Fox and Bear: K–1 Problem 1's hop 2 on 4 dots.)

**Harder (older children, or anyone done in two minutes).** Six seats and six animals, with Mouse. Mouse sat in the star seat. Fox sat 2 seats clockwise from Owl. Bear sat 2 seats clockwise from Fox. Owl sat right next to the star seat. Bear sat across the table from Rabbit. *Answer:* clockwise from the star seat, Mouse, Owl, Rabbit, Fox, Turtle, Bear. (Owl on the other side of the star seat puts Bear straight across from the star seat, and then Rabbit would need Mouse's seat.) *Done?* "Mouse passes a note 2 seats clockwise, and on it goes. Which animals never get it?" (Owl, Fox and Bear: they make their own loop. This is the midpoint's question, met early.)

**Early finishers.** "Cross out clue 4. Find every answer that fits now. Write a new clue that makes it exactly one answer again." (Two answers: the one above, and Rabbit, Fox, Turtle, Bear, Owl. "Bear sat right next to Rabbit" fixes it, checked, and so do others.)

**On the floor (minute 6).** Five children who solved it take the signs and sit where the answer says, Rabbit on the star seat. The room checks each clue out loud, thumbs up or down, counting the seats with the sitters. They stay seated: they are the launch's five dots. Bridge question: "Rabbit passes the note 2 seats clockwise, and each animal who gets it does the same. Does every animal get it before it is back with Rabbit?"

**The yarn star, step by step.** Everyone sits in one circle on the floor, adults joining or stepping out to make 12. The star sheet goes in front of the first holder, who keeps the loose end. *Pass 3:* the room guesses how many starts it will take. The holder rolls the ball to the person 3 along, everyone counting "one, two, three" from the next person. Each catcher holds the yarn with one hand and rolls the ball on. When it is back with the first holder: "Back!" An adult cuts the yarn, and the holder keeps both ends. "Who has no yarn?" Start again there. Three starts make three squares. Everyone lays the yarn down and an adult clears it. *Pass 5:* guess, then roll. ## Answers for the grades 2–5 packet (adults only)

The guide's answers use the old 2–3 and 4–5 numbers; these are the 2–5 packet's. "Starts" is the number of times you start; it is always the biggest number that goes into both the number of dots and the hop.

1. Rings of 8: hop 1, 1 start; hop 2, 2; hop 3, 1 (a star); hop 4, 4.
2. Rings of 12: hop 2, 2; hop 3, 3; hop 4, 4; hop 5, 1; hop 6, 6. Hop 5 is the surprise: one start reaches all 12 dots.
3. Decoding at D: STAR, FOX, WHEEL, PENCIL, I CAN DRAW A STAR.
4. Partners' messages: check by decoding.
5. Four more rings: 9 dots hop 2, 1; 15 hop 5, 5; 16 hop 6, 2; 12 hop 8, 4. The last two break the tempting rule "the hop if it divides, otherwise 1": 16 hop 6 and 12 hop 8 (hop 4 backwards) need 2 and 4 starts.
6. Cracking: A STAR CAN HAVE TEN POINTS (setting H); MEET ME BY THE BIG TREE (setting Q). Way in: a one-letter word is A or I.
7. Second setting that gives the word back: D then X; H then T; N then N; W then E. The two moves add up to 26 places.
8. Without drawing: 20 dots hop 8, 4; 24 hop 10, 2; 30 hop 12, 6; 36 hop 27, 9; 17 hop 5, 1; 100 hop 35, 5; 60 hop 45, 15.
9. The rule: the number of starts is the biggest number that goes into both. Why: one start only lands on that number's times table, so each dot in between needs its own start, and every start is the first one turned, so they share the dots out in equal piles.
10. Rings where every hop reaches every dot: 5, 7, 11, 13, 17 and 19 (the primes). Every other ring from 4 to 20 has a hop that shares a factor with it, such as hop 2 on any even ring.

The K–1 answers are in the guide's K–1 section, with the same problem numbers. Two K–1 wording changes since the guide: Problems 1 and 6 now say to try hop 1, hop 2 and hop 3 and draw a line for each hop, and Problem 8 has three blank rows (the third is the top row again).

## If it goes sideways

- A table finishes early: hand out its early-finisher card.
- K–1 Problem 4's 8-dot rings are too small for a counter: lay 8 counters in a ring on the table and hop a counter of the other colour round it.
- A child counts the dot the counter is on as "one": "Put your finger on the next dot and say one" (guide, K–1 hint 1), nothing more.
- A pair is stuck on a drawing: "You are back. Point to a dot with no line. Start there in a new colour."
- A 2–5 child writes "starts = hop" for every guess on 12 dots: leave it; hop 5 will surprise them, and that is the point.
- Decoding comes out as nonsense: the usual mistake is going inner to outer, which codes it again. "Find V on the outer wheel. Which inner letter is beside it?" (guide, hint 2).
- Cutting runs long: a spare wheel at 0:40; their own pages go home. Reading the codes slows a pair down: the Writer says the letters and the host writes.
- K–1 runs out of attention: the guide's pass-the-counter game at the table (4 children and the host make 5; pass by 2; then the host steps out and pass by 2 again).
- The room gets loud: "Hop stop!", then go straight to the next floor activity.
- No floor space for the finale: stand round the biggest table and pass a counter instead of yarn; the host draws the lines on the launch board as the counter moves.
- The yarn tangles or rolls away: an adult carries the ball to the right person, and the count goes on.
