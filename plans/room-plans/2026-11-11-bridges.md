# Room plan: bridges (pick up every counter)

## Why this follows November 4

Last week's shuffle machines moved children between chairs by a fixed rule. Today the rule lives in a town: cross a bridge, pick up its counter, and that bridge is closed. The 2–3 children met take-away games on October 21, taking counters from piles in turns; the finale here is the guide's bridge game, which is a take-away game played on a map, so they'll recognise it.

**The room.** On October 21 the circle ran three tables by age and most children moved between tables at some point. This plan keeps one theme at three tables, so a child who wanders still finds the same rule and the same counters, and the floor move at 0:28 is the planned moment to switch. Each table's early-finisher card doubles as its table card: an adult hands it to a child who walks up mid-round.

This week shares its theorem with Week 2's lamps, which went poorly on paper because nothing stopped a child changing just the lamp they wanted. Here the rule is physical: a crossed bridge loses its counter into your hand.

For adults: in a town in one piece, a walk that crosses every bridge exactly once exists exactly when zero or two islands have an odd number of bridges. With zero it can start anywhere and ends where it started; with two it must start at one odd island and end at the other. Reason: a walk passing through an island uses its bridges in pairs, in and out. On the floor town, purple and yellow have 3 bridges and the rest have 2, so the walk runs from purple to yellow or back. The opener asks a different question, visiting every house once, which has no counting rule. That contrast is worth saying to the 4–5 table.

- **Tape the floor town** (the house town from packet page 1, as in the guide), in open floor about 8 by 12 feet. Each coloured sheet is an island; tape it down on all four edges so nobody slips, and write its packet letter on it in big marker:
  - blue **D** and red **E** on the floor, 4½ feet apart, centre to centre (the bottom corners);
  - purple **B** 4½ feet straight up from blue, yellow **C** 4½ feet straight up from red (the square);
  - green **A** centred above, about 4 feet past the purple–yellow line (the roof).
  - Six tape bridges, island edge to island edge, each about 3½ feet: D–E, D–B, E–C, B–C, B–A, C–A. A sticky note in the middle of each.

## Opener: Pat's mail route (8 minutes)

A soft logic puzzle on the tables as children arrive, then walked on the floor town, whose five islands are Pat's houses. Pat's route starts with the guide's first legal move and leaves two notes on the floor, so the puzzle leads straight into the launch question. An adult reads the clues aloud at any table that wants it.

**The puzzle (most of the room).** Pat the mail carrier brought one letter to each of five houses (the house town: green on top, purple and yellow in the middle, blue and red at the bottom), one house after another. Number the houses in the order Pat went.

1. Pat went to the red house right after the blue house.
2. Pat went to the green house right after the yellow house.
3. Pat went to the yellow house some time after the red house.
4. The green house was not the last house.

**Answer:** blue 1, red 2, yellow 3, green 4, purple 5. It is the only answer, and every clue is needed, whether or not a child also assumes Pat walks along the paths (checked by computer, [mail-route_check.py](openers/mail-route_check.py)). Reading clue 3 as "right after" gives the same answer.

**Hint ladder** (give one step, then walk away):

1. "Which two houses always go together? Which other two?" (Blue then red; yellow then green.)
2. "Clue 3: which pair comes first?" (Blue and red come before yellow and green.)
3. "Where can purple go? Look at clue 4." (Not before blue or between the pairs, since then green is last: purple is 5.)

**Easier (younger children, or anyone stuck at step 1).** Three houses: green on top, purple and yellow below. Pat went to the yellow house right after the purple house. Pat did not start at the green house. *Answer:* purple 1, yellow 2, green 3.

**Harder (older children, or anyone done in two minutes).** The same five houses, and now the paths matter: from each house Pat walked along one path straight to the next. Pat started at a bottom house. Pat went to the red house right after the blue house. The top house was not first and not last. *Answer:* the same route. Red cannot be first, so blue is; from red the only new house is yellow; green is not last, so green then purple. Without the path rule it has 4 answers.

**Early finishers.** "Cross out clue 4. Find every order that fits now. Write a new clue that makes it exactly one again." (Three orders: purple first, purple third, or purple last; two if the child keeps to the paths. "The purple house was the last house" fixes it, checked, and so do others.)

**On the floor (minute 6).** Everyone to the floor town. Adult: "These are Pat's houses. Every bridge has a note on it. When Pat crosses a bridge, Pat picks up its note. A bridge with no note is closed." One solver plays Pat and starts on blue. The room says each clue as Pat walks, thumbs up or down: blue, along the bottom bridge to red, up to yellow, over the roof to green, down to purple. Pat holds 4 notes. Bridge question: "Every house got its letter. Two notes are left. Can Pat keep walking and pick them both up?"

## If it goes sideways

- A table finishes early: hand out its early-finisher card.
- A pair is stuck: "Put the counters back and start on a different island", nothing more (guide, hint 1).
- Writing walks as letters slows the 2–3 table: the child says the letters and the adult writes.
- The room gets loud: "Bridges up", then go straight to the next floor activity.
- No floor space: the guide's fallback. Counters on K–1 page 1's house town at one table; Pat is a token.
- Finale: from blue, red or green the first team can force a win; from purple or yellow the second can (checked). Do not say so.
- Jay and Rose: the use log has no bridges entry. If they have seen it (at home or in Bridge Courier), make them floor captains at 0:28 and 0:49, and 2–3 checkers who may say "try again" but not why.
