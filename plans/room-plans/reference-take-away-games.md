## Why it would follow November 4

Last week's shuffle machines ran one machine again and again; this week the other side is a player who answers back. Tower cities, which the K–1 table met on October 21, asked whether a set of clues fits one city, several or none. This week's opener is a clue puzzle of that kind, and the main activity asks the same question inside a game: does my way of playing work against every reply, not just the one I expect? It is also the kind of activity this room does well. The other player enforces the rule, the counters show the state, and a game of 10 takes under a minute, so children play many games before anyone asks them to explain.

For adults: with moves of 1 or 2, the piles you want to hand your opponent are the multiples of 3 (0, 3, 6, 9, ...), and you keep it that way by answering a take of 1 with 2 and a take of 2 with 1. With moves of 1, 3 or 4 the piles to hand over are those with remainder 0 or 2 on dividing by 7 (0, 2, 7, 9, 14, 16), so Goat's pile of 7 is a first-player win in the launch and a second-player win at the midpoint. In general a pile is one to hand over exactly when every move from it reaches a pile that is not; working up from 0 sorts every pile, and for any finite list of moves the pattern ends up repeating (a pigeonhole argument, 4–5 Problem 14). Two equal piles are always ones to hand over: copy your opponent in the other pile.

## Opener: whose pile? (8 minutes)

A soft logic puzzle on the tables as children arrive, in the spirit of year one's openers and of the tower cities clues, then acted out on the floor. The answer hands Goat's pile of 7 straight to the launch, which is the pile the guide's launch uses. An adult reads the clues aloud at any table that wants it.

**The puzzle (most of the room).** Duck, Frog, Goat and Mouse each have a pile of counters: one pile of 3, one of 5, one of 6 and one of 7 (drawn on the sheet). Write each animal's name next to its pile.

1. Frog has an odd number of counters.
2. Mouse has fewer counters than Frog.
3. Goat has more counters than Frog.
4. Duck does not have the biggest pile.

**Answer:** Mouse 3, Frog 5, Duck 6, Goat 7. It is the only answer, and every clue is needed (checked by computer: without clue 1 there are 3 answers, without clue 2 or 3 there are 7, without clue 4 there are 2).

**Hint ladder** (give one step, then walk away):

1. "Clues 2 and 3 are both about Frog. Can Frog have the biggest pile? The smallest?" (No: Mouse has fewer and Goat has more, so Frog has 5 or 6.)
2. "Which of 5 and 6 is odd?" (5, so Frog has 5. Mouse has fewer, so Mouse has 3.)
3. "Goat and Duck share 6 and 7. Which clue decides?" (Duck is not the biggest, so Goat 7 and Duck 6.)

**Easier (younger children, or anyone stuck at step 1).** Frog, Goat and Mouse, with piles of 3, 5 and 7. Goat has the most counters. Mouse has fewer counters than Frog. Children may write first letters. *Answer:* Mouse 3, Frog 5, Goat 7.

**Harder (older children, or anyone done in two minutes).** Duck, Frog, Goat, Hen and Mouse, with piles of 3, 5, 6, 7 and 8. Duck has an even number of counters. Hen has an even number of counters. Hen has more counters than Duck. Frog has exactly two more counters than Mouse. Goat has more counters than Frog. *Answer:* the main answer with Hen 8 (the even piles are 6 and 8; then Frog and Mouse are 5 and 3, since 7 and 5 would leave Goat nothing bigger than Frog). Every clue is needed.

**Early finishers.** "Cross out clue 4. How many answers are there now? Write a new clue that makes it exactly one again." (Two answers: Duck and Goat can swap. "Goat has more counters than Duck" fixes it, and so do others.)

**On the floor (minute 6).** Four children who solved it take the animal signs. Each picks up the cup that matches their answer and counts it out loud with the room, and the room checks each clue, thumbs up or down. Then Goat's cup becomes the launch's pile: Goat hands one counter to each of seven children, who stand in a line. A bridge question before the first move: "We take 1 or 2 counters each turn, and whoever takes the last counter wins. If I go first, can I make sure the last one is mine?" (Yes: take 1 and leave 6. Do not say so; play it.)

## If it goes sideways

- A table finishes early: its early-finisher card. The room gets loud: reset signal, then straight to the next floor activity.
- A pair is stuck: the adult plays them once from a small pile and asks "Which pile would you rather hand your partner?", nothing more.
- Tears after a loss (the guide warns of this): short games, swap who starts, start the next game at once, and the upset child picks the next pile and who starts.
- The human line turns silly or slow: Goat's 7 in a bowl on the floor with the whiteboard track, exactly the guide's launch. The human line and the one-in-each-hand rule are untried.
- Jay and Rose: the use log has no session of Week 7, so they meet it fresh. Whoever finds a table's secret first becomes its checker, who may say "try again" but not why, and a floor captain at 0:28 and 0:48. The same goes for a child who has played Pebble Duel in the app.
