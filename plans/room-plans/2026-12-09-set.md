Unpiloted, at the circle and at home.

## Why this ends the fall

All hour the children work with nine paper tiles, three shapes in three fills, and one rule: in an allowed three, the shapes are all the same or all different, and so are the fills. At the midpoint the same nine tiles turn up as giant cards from a real game, and at the end everyone gets that game to take home. The room should leave knowing the secret inside the box: SET is our nine tiles with two more features.

**The room.** One theme at all three tables, so a child who wanders finds the same tiles and the same rule. Tables work with paper tiles because the printed pages draw circles, triangles and squares; SET cards show other shapes, which would make younger children translate every picture. The giant deck appears on the floor only, where the change of costume is the point.

**K–1 judges shape and fill together from the start** (James, November 16). If a child cannot hold both after Round 1, the K–1 host switches that child to shapes only (the guide's fallback, page 2) and adds fills back with the near miss.

For adults: any two tiles have exactly one tile that completes them (for each feature, keep it if the two match, or take the missing one if they differ). There are 12 allowed threes, four through each tile, and exactly four ways to split the nine tiles into three allowed threes. The most tiles with no allowed three is four, and the adding game (take turns adding a tile without making an allowed three) always lasts exactly four moves, so the second player always wins. The guide proves all of this. In a full SET deck the same rule runs on four features and 81 cards; the most cards with no set is 9 among the 27 cards with one count, and 20 in the whole deck. Children don't need any of this said aloud.

## Before the children arrive

- Clear a patch of floor about 6 feet across for the big tiles and giant cards, with room for everyone to sit around it.
- On every table: opener half-sheets and pencils, so the first child in starts on the puzzle. Then that band's tile sets (still bagged), counters, coloured pencils and packet page 1 face down.
- The big tiles in a pile by the floor patch; the nine giant cards face down beside them. The gift decks out of sight.
- Early-finisher cards, one per table:
  - K–1: "Make two tiles for your partner. Can they find the tile that finishes the three?"
  - 2–3: "Lay out four tiles with no allowed three. Your partner adds a fifth. Is there always an allowed three now?"
  - 4–5 (with James's own SET deck, if it's there): "Take the 27 cards that show exactly one shape. Pick as many as you can with no set. Can you beat 8?" (The most is 9.)

## Opener: three friends, three tiles (8 minutes)

A soft logic puzzle on the tables as children arrive, then acted out with the big tiles, so the answer becomes the launch's first allowed three. An adult reads the clues aloud at any table that wants it. The sheet shows the nine tiles.

**The puzzle (most of the room).** Ana, Ben and Cy each picked one of the nine tiles. Their three shapes are all different, and their three fills are all different. Draw each friend's tile in their box.

1. Ana has a circle.
2. Ben does not have a square.
3. The solid tile is not a triangle.
4. The striped tile is a square.

**Answer:** Ana the solid circle, Ben the open triangle, Cy the striped square. It is the only answer, and every clue is needed (checked by computer, [three-tiles_check.py](openers/three-tiles_check.py)).

**Hint ladder** (give one step, then walk away):

1. "Ana has the circle. Which shapes are left for Ben and Cy?" (Triangle and square.)
2. "Clue 2: so who has the square?" (Cy.)
3. "Clue 4: who is striped? Then clue 3: who can be solid?" (Cy is striped. The solid one is not Ben's triangle, so Ana is solid and Ben is open.)

**Easier (younger children, or anyone stuck at step 1).** Ana and Ben each picked a tile: one a circle and one a triangle, one open and one solid. Ana has the circle. The triangle is open. *Answer:* Ana the solid circle, Ben the open triangle.

**Harder (older children, or anyone done in two minutes).** The fills may now be all the same or all different, which is the rule of the day in disguise. The clues are 1, "Ben's tile is not striped", 3, and "Someone has a striped square." *Answer:* the same. Its early finisher swaps clue 2 back to "Ben does not have a square", which leaves two answers: the same one, and all three striped.

**Early finishers.** "Cross out clue 2. Find every answer now. Write a new clue that makes it exactly one again." (Two answers: the original, and Ana solid circle, Ben striped square, Cy open triangle. "Ben has a triangle" fixes it, and so do others.)

**On the floor (minute 6).** Everyone to the floor patch. Three solvers play Ana, Ben and Cy and each picks up the big tile they drew. The room checks with thumbs: "Shapes all different? Fills all different?" Adult: "When the shapes are all the same or all different, and the fills are all the same or all different, we call it an allowed three. Today we hunt for them."

## If it goes sideways

- A table finishes early: hand out its early-finisher card.
- A pair is stuck on Problem 1: "Say the shapes out loud. Same, or all different? Now the fills." Nothing more.
- A K–1 child can't judge both features: shapes only for that child (guide, page 2), then add fills with the near miss.
- Someone claims five tiles with no allowed three: the partner hunts; there is always one (guide, page 3).
- A game ends after three moves or runs to five: check the last move together; every game lasts exactly four.
- The room gets loud: "Tiles down!", then go straight to the next floor activity.
- The decks come out early: they stay sealed until the gift moment; if time runs short, hand them out at the door with the slip.
- A child who already knows SET: make them a floor checker at 0:28 and 0:48 who may say "try again" but not why.
- A child is absent: the spare deck covers a newcomer; whether absent children get one later is James's call.
