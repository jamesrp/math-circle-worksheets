## Priority findings

1. **Page 1 shared rules: explicitly keep route interiors between the rims.** “Each yarn route goes upward from A to B” suggests the right model, but the minimum-crossing theorem assumes that the interior of a route stays in the open cylinder rather than using either rim as an extra road. Add a short condition such as “Except at A and B, keep the yarn strictly between the rims.” This makes legal routing checkable by the partner and closes an avoidable assumption gap. Do not add a technical definition of a proper arc.

2. **Page 4, Problem 7: avoid suggesting that twisting only one route must change the minimum.** The current closing question, “Why do adding the same twist to both routes and twisting only one route behave differently?”, is directionally right but can lead to an overstatement in the immediate minimum-crossing context. Applying the same twist always preserves the difference and its minimum. Applying one twist to one route changes the difference but need not change the minimum: winding pairs (5,5) and (6,5) both have zero. The equal-winding case immediately before this question makes the exception especially relevant. Prefer a question like “Does adding the same twist to both routes change the fewest crossings? What can happen if you twist only one?” Keep the rule discovery and the equal-winding test.

3. **Physical rehearsal is still required for the proposed yarn mechanics.** The source README is commendably candid about this. Ordinary tape may hold yarn too tightly to feed through while keeping a fixed geometric endpoint; loose tape may let the endpoint slide. A narrow, 6-inch-circumference paper cylinder may also buckle while children adjust several windings. Test the exact paper, yarn, and tape arrangement before calling the activity ready. The rehearsal must show that a child can alter a same-winding route while keeping both rim marks fixed and the strand on the surface. If that fails, revise the fastening method or use the drawn route as the mathematical record; do not quietly permit endpoint motion or rotation of one rim.

4. **Crossing yarn represents projected surface crossings, not knot over/under information.** Two physical strands cannot occupy the same surface point, but their visible overlap is the intended intersection. Make this interpretation clear in the short launch or adult notes, especially when a partner “removes” a crossing. Lifting one strand into space to pass over another is not evidence that the surface arcs avoid intersection. The current student phrase “must cross or stay apart” is reasonable; a long knot-theory warning need not be added to the worksheet.

5. **Preserve manageable pacing through the representation changes.** Page 1 requires cutting, joining, seam counting, and several physical routes; page 2 introduces the covering strip and an impossibility question; page 3 introduces a whole-surface rule; page 4 asks for minimum intersections. The requested examples are purposeful and substantial. This is enough work for more than one visit for some children. Treat pace concerns as an unpiloted hypothesis, not evidence that Grades 4–5 cannot do the mathematics. Keep the physical model and repeated-rectangle examples available together and allow children to stop after a substantive investigation.

## Mathematical audit

- **Winding convention and Problem 1:** A and B are at the same angular position, away from the seam. Traversing upward and counting rightward seam crossings +1 and leftward crossings −1 gives an integer winding. Upward, non-self-intersecting routes of winding 0, +1, −1, and +2 all exist. Different-looking routes with equal winding also exist. Rightward exit and left-edge re-entry at equal height are drawn correctly.
- **Problem 2:** the repeated strip labels are correct. A is at the middle of copy 0, while B copies −1, 0, 1, and 2 sit at corresponding angular positions. Paths to B in copy 2 and copy −1 have the requested windings. A straight lift, or a small upward-preserving bend, supplies different examples without changing the endpoint copy. The different aspect ratio of this drawing from the paper cylinder is harmless because the question is topological, not metric.
- **Problem 3:** equal-winding upward arcs can be slid into one another, with fixed endpoints, by interpolation between their lifted graphs; every intermediate graph still projects to an upward simple arc. Different winding cannot change under such a surface deformation because its lifted endpoint cannot jump continuously between distinct B copies. Extra physical yarn length may be fed at the same geometric endpoint; allowing that does not change the mathematical invariant. Crossing oneself is not the only issue: endpoint and surface restrictions remain essential.
- **Twist rule and Problems 4–5:** with angular coordinate modulo one turn and normalized height t, the + rule is (theta,t) ↦ (theta+t,t), and the − rule is its inverse. At both rims, every point is fixed modulo a turn. Compositions add winding by the sum of the signs. A shortest word for resulting winding n has |n| twists; zero twists is permitted when n=0. A six-twist word preserves every initial winding exactly when it has three + signs and three − signs. The test is independent of the initial route. The page neither asks a child to rotate a ring nor claims that a rigid paper sleeve physically deforms through this map.
- **Problem 6:** for upward lifts beginning at the same A, the difference of their horizontal coordinates starts at 0 and finishes at a−b. Each integer strictly between those endpoint values forces an interior meeting after projection. Straight lifts attain |a−b|−1 crossings when the difference is nonzero. Thus the six printed minima, in order, are **0, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1**.
- **Problem 7:** windings 2 and 7 have minimum **4**. Windings 5 and 5 have minimum **0**, achieved by distinct nearby routes with a small bow vanishing at the endpoints; drawing the identical route twice would create a forbidden shared piece. The general answer is max(|a−b|−1,0). Adding the same twist to both preserves the difference. Twisting one can raise, lower, or leave the minimum unchanged.

These are minimum counts over legal representatives, not counts guaranteed for every drawing. The printed prompt correctly asks children to remove unnecessary crossings and excludes shared endpoints, tangencies, and overlapping pieces. The source identifies the cited torus-shear problem as inspiration rather than falsely attributing the annular crossing formula to it.
