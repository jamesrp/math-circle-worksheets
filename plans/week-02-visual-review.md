## The request and the design response

The organizer liked the explicit actions, diagrams, and gently varied examples in the K–1 auxiliary. In grades 2–3, returning to a mat on another page, interpreting lists such as “1 only,” and reading the endpoint-change table create work before the actual investigation. The same presentation issue applies to grades 4–5. Paper is available; compactness is not a design goal.

The visual revision kept a working graph beside each task, pictured target states, broke dense records into visible attempts, and supplied diagrams for recording results. A lamp-state picture and a selected-road picture have different meanings and are demonstrated separately. The middle collection retains the ring representation instead of requiring a new line-of-circles encoding. Setup instructions distinguish placing counters in a displayed starting state from making a legal pair move.

That pass preserved the mathematical scope and the three separately named sets pending the organizer’s decision. The subsequent shared collection retains these investigations as Problems 15–32. The middle still investigates all and only the eight even states of a four-ring. The upper still tests order/cancellation, complementary move sets, forced decisions, shortest solutions, and the five-/six-ring contrast. The facilitator preserves the proofs and makes them optional adult conversations after concrete attempts. More page space is not a demand for more completed pages.

## Shared collection: recommendation subsequently adopted

The organizer subsequently approved this recommendation, now implemented in the [shared plan](week-02-shared-collection.md). It uses a common opening with a small number of routes chosen by interest and prerequisites, with permission to skip familiar examples after one or two attempts:

- Make and explore: match targets, move one light, invent a partner puzzle, build a larger board.
- Collect and explain: gather states, compare successful and unsuccessful targets, explain pair parity and completeness.
- Find and improve solutions: make a target in two ways, remove repeated presses, compare complementary sets, prove a minimum.
- Change theme: draw and divide rooms, then investigate neighboring-room marks.

These are choices, not a sequence every child must complete before reaching the next. The shared collection also includes a fifth route through components, bridges, and trees. Page completion depends partly on reading and recording pace; it should not decide mathematical readiness. Stable adult-led tables can remain while page selection becomes more flexible. The parent can run familiar exploration/drawing scripts and mathematicians can support classification and optimization. This does not require rotating stations or ten independent lesson plans. The initial visual pass deferred the merger at the organizer’s request; the later “Go with a shared collection” instruction authorized the current shared packet and unified guide. This adoption is a design decision, not a classroom result.

## Source grounding and limits

Relevant source passages were reviewed locally:

- *Math Circle by the Bay*, preface, printed pp. viii–x (PDF pp. 9–11), especially p. x: the authors describe using the same material across age groups at different pace/depth, and sometimes seeing younger groups proceed at the same speed. This supports flexibility; it does not establish our room configuration.
- Natasha Rozhkovskaya, *Math Circles for Elementary School Students*, Lesson 8, “At the lesson,” item 2 (`OEBPS/part0018.xhtml`): writing and coloring pace divided the group, leading to reduced copying. The recommendation to judge readiness by attempts rather than page completion is our inference.
- The same book, Lesson 3, “At the lesson,” item 1 (`part0013.xhtml`): children attempted and explained before a table was introduced, and additional tasks were prepared without all being discussed together.
- Lowell year 1, Handout 11, Problem 11.3: inventing and splitting a picture to challenge a friend provides continuity for partner-created problems. Availability of the handout does not show which children used it.

The [Week 1 classroom review](week-01-classroom-review.md) remains the source of actual local classroom observations about concrete examples and clear next actions. The September 28 printed-page feedback is a design preference and critique, not a new classroom trial.
