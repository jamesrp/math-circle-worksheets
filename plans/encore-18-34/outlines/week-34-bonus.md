Week 34: Hidden turns encore

Mathematical kernels

1. Design the symmetries that survive rather than eliminating all of them. For an n-position ring with fixed named kinds, matching rotations form a subgroup. If the least positive matching rotation is d spots (take d=n if none except the identity), then d divides n and there are n/d matching rotations including the identity. If any reflection matches, exactly n/d reflections match: composing a fixed matching reflection with each matching rotation gives them all, and composing two matching reflections gives a matching rotation. Therefore the number of matching reflections is either 0 or a divisor of n, never an arbitrary number. Binary six-rings can realize exactly 0,1,2,3,6 matching reflections. Examples for positive counts are 110000 (one), 100100 (two), 101010 (three), and 000000 (six). An eight-ring can realize positive counts 1,2,4,8. Concrete core: build for a requested surviving behavior and have a partner test the overlay. The child need not learn subgroup terminology; two flips acting together can be demonstrated physically. Do not count the whole turn as a nonidentity move, but include it when stating the rotation-count theorem.

2. Repairing a damaged symmetry is an optimization question. Fix one allowed motion g of the ring and change as few counter kinds as possible until that motion matches. Decompose positions into cycles under g. Each cycle must be monochromatic; its least repair cost is its length minus the largest frequency of a single kind in that cycle. Summing those independent costs gives the exact minimum. In particular a selected reflection or half-turn pairs spots (and may fix spots); each mismatched pair needs and suffices for one change, while fixed spots cost zero. For a one-spot rotation, all spots are one cycle, so the cost is n minus the majority count. This supports real comparisons between different chosen motions and multiple equally good repairs, beyond the base “change one counter and see what happens.” The target motion must stay fixed while optimizing; letting it change silently would answer a different question. No color-category relabeling is allowed.

3. Layered patterns keep only common symmetries. Overlay two independently recorded patterns on the same regular ring while preserving both layer identities; the combined kind at a spot is the ordered pair of its layer kinds. Its matching-motion set is exactly the intersection of the two layers’ matching-motion sets. Two patterns can each have a reflection while their combined pattern has no nonidentity symmetry. On a ring with n≥3, make one layer with a unique mark at one vertex and another with a unique mark at an adjacent vertex; each has one reflection, but no nonidentity polygon motion fixes both adjacent vertices. If the two unique marks are opposite on an even ring, a shared reflection survives. Conversely a motion common to both layers cannot disappear after stacking; two half-turn-symmetric layers still have a half-turn-symmetric stack. These are concrete construction/obstruction questions about intersection, not another least-color search. If both layers are merged by an uncolored union of marked spots, rather than remembering which layer supplied a mark, the intersection theorem fails; the distinction must be explicit and visibly enforced.

Suggested emphasis by level:
K–1: build a pattern with one visible matching flip, repair a small number of mismatched overlay pairs, and combine two distinct mark layers after physical practice; adults read and record. Do not require a motion census or general cycle formula.
Grades 2–3: design requested reflection behavior, minimize repairs for a specified half-turn/reflection, and search for symmetric layers whose stack loses those symmetries. Reliable overlay alignment and counting to eight suffice.
Grades 4–5: explain why reflection counts are constrained, defend repair lower bounds through independent pairs/cycles, and distinguish a common motion from different layer motions. The group language is optional adult context, not a prerequisite.

Materials

Per pair: regular six/eight-position mats or writer-selected sizes, 16 counters in each of two named kinds, at least three tracing sheets or clear overlays, two distinctly styled layer markers (e.g. filled red circles and blue outline squares), and removable tape. Keep the original pattern fixed. Ring positions must be ≥28 mm circles for ≥20 mm counters, with equal scaling in both axes and no permanent home mark. Use an overlay to flip rather than spilling loose counters. Record the target rotation/reflection on a separate motion card, not a symmetry-breaking tick on the ring. For repair, duplicate the starting pattern so rejected changes can be reversed and compared. For layers, show a small non-task visual with first layer, second layer, and ordered-pair combined spot symbols; both visible marks survive at overlaps. Do not permit swapping the two layers, swapping color names, or treating icon direction as data. Adult preparation includes checking the transparency marks can be read from either side; that check is a proposed physical pretest, not one already completed.

Base exclusions and drafting boundary

Do not repeat the least-kinds problem for 3/4/5/6 rings, the uniform 0,1,3 asymmetry construction, minimizing the minority on an eight-ring, or finding two inequivalent asymmetric eight-rings. New directions are prescribed surviving symmetries, minimum repairs, and intersections of layer symmetries. A successful stack may reproduce a known three-kind distinguishing pattern, but the new mathematical question is how separately symmetric layers interact; do not relabel the base construction as an additional investigation.
