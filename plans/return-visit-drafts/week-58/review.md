# Review of the Week 58 return-visit draft (W58-RV-K-v1, W58-RV-M-v1, W58-RV-U-v1)

Reviewed: draft/k-1.pdf (5 pp.), draft/grades-2-3.pdf (6 pp.), draft/grades-4-5.pdf (6 pp.). I rendered every page at 80 dpi and pages K–1 p. 1 and 2–3 p. 5 at 150 dpi, read gen.py and check.py, ran check.py, and separately recomputed the fewest cuts for every printed train and the marks in every panel order.

## Overall verdict

The packet is close. The format is right in all three bands: correct headers and footers, unnumbered shared rules only at the top, consecutive "Problem N:" labels, no titles, no encouragement, no exclamation marks, no dashes used for asides, and no Name/Date fields. The mathematics printed on the pages is correct: every train answer, the strip answer, the X Y Z three-cut answer, the worked marking round and the card-row half point all check out (details at the end). The order of problems follows the outline's development, and each band has enough work.

Three things need fixing before this goes to the organizer:

1. Nothing says which way a cut goes, so a single lengthwise cut solves the strip and panel problems. This defeats the main answer of K–1 P4, 2–3 P3, 4–5 P3 and 4–5 P4.
2. The train rule and its only example allow children to read "one piece per tray". Under that reading the two-cut trains look impossible, and children will cross out shareable trains in P2.
3. K–1 counts "pull-aparts", but sliding a middle piece out of a train is one motion that opens two gaps. The K–1 counting question in P1, P3 and P5 is therefore ambiguous.

Several smaller problems are listed below.

## Must fix

### M1. Cut direction is never stated (all bands)

- **Where:** K–1 P4 ("Share one red and blue strip … Mark where an adult should cut, using as few cuts as you can."), 2–3 P3 ("Can one cut do it?"), 4–5 P3 ("explain why fewer cuts cannot work") and 4–5 P4 (X Y Z row, "explain why fewer cuts cannot work"). Also 2–3 P5, 4–5 P4 and P5 ("A panel may be cut") and 4–5 P7 ("Here a card may be cut").
- **Problem:** One cut along the length of the 240 × 40 mm strip gives two 240 × 20 mm strips. Each has exactly half the red and half the blue, so it is exact for A and for B. A lengthwise ("hot dog") fold is the fold kindergartners reach for first. Under that cut:
  - K–1 P4 is solved with one cut.
  - The 2–3 answer to "Can one cut do it?" becomes "yes".
  - The 4–5 request to "explain why fewer cuts cannot work" asks for a false statement.
  - The same applies to cutting each X, Y, Z panel lengthwise.
  - The written value rule even contradicts itself here: "value follows length within a panel" would give a lengthwise half its full length, and so the whole panel's value.
- **Brief requirement:** The outline requires these cuts: "cut the separate full-size strips parallel to the colour join, measuring from the red end."
- **Fix:** State it once as a rule wherever a strip, panel or card can be cut. For example, in 2–3/4–5: "Every cut goes straight across, parallel to the line where red meets blue." For panels: "A panel may be cut straight across, and value follows length within a panel." For K–1 P4: "Each cut goes straight across the strip."
  - The panel-cut example already shows a crosswise cut, which helps. The strip has no such cue.

### M2. "Put each piece into one of two trays" plus a two-piece example invites a one-piece-per-tray reading (all bands)

- **Where:** The shared rules on p. 1 of every band, and the RBR|RBR → two trays example.
- **Problem:** The only example has exactly one cut and one piece in each tray. When the rule is read aloud once to K–1, "put each piece into one of two trays" is easily heard as "each piece into its own tray". Under that reading the following look impossible:
  - RRBB and BBRR in K–1 P1 and 2–3 P1;
  - RRRRBB, RRRRBBBB and RRRRRRBBBB in K–1 P2;
  - RRRRBB, RBBRRR, RRRBBBBR and RRBRRRBBBR in 2–3 P2.

  P2 in both bands offers "If a train cannot be shared, cross it out", so children will cross out shareable trains with confidence. Realising that two end pieces can go into the same tray is the mathematics. Knowing that a tray may hold several pieces is a rule, and a child should not have to infer it.
- **Fix:** Add one clause to the rules in every band, for example "A tray may get more than one piece." Keep the one-cut example. Do not replace it with a two-cut example, because that would show the key trick.

### M3. K–1 counts motions, not gaps

- **Where:** K–1 P1 ("pulling it apart as few times as you can"), P2 (same), P3 ("pulling it apart only once") and P5 ("needs three pull-aparts").
- **Problem:** For RRBB, the natural physical move is to slide the middle RB out of the line. That is one pull, but it opens two gaps.
  - A kindergartner will truthfully report "one pull" for a two-cut train. P3's game then has no clear winner, and P5's question loses its meaning.
  - P1's "draw a line at each place you pull it apart" records gaps correctly, but the counted quantity in the task is "times".
- **Fix:** Count places, not motions:
  - P1: "Share it by pulling it apart at as few places as you can, and draw a line at each place."
  - P2: "…pulling it apart at as few places as you can."
  - P3: "…tries to share it by pulling it apart at only one place."
  - P5: "Can you make a train that has to be pulled apart at three places to share it?"

## Should fix

### S1. K–1 P3: the game has no goal for the maker, and the recording request implies a full list

- **Quote:** "One player makes a train with four red cards and four blue cards, and the other player tries to share it by pulling it apart only once. Draw the trains that cannot be shared that way."
- **Problem 1:** Only the sharer has a goal. The maker's goal, a train that cannot be shared with one pull, is what makes the game work and what drives the discovery that the first four cards must hold two reds.
- **Problem 2:** "Draw the trains that cannot be shared that way" reads as "all of them". There are 34 such orders of the 70, and the page has 7 rows.
- **Fix:** For example: "One player makes a train with four red cards and four blue cards that cannot be shared by pulling it apart at one place. The other player tries to share it that way. Draw each train that could not be shared." This also works with M3.

### S2. 4–5 has no worked example of the marking game

- **Where:** 4–5 P5 gives the rules as one long paragraph with no picture.
- **Problem:** The marking game is new to every band; the first visit used cut-and-choose. The project standard asks for a small worked visual before the first use of an unfamiliar procedure. 2–3 P5 has a good one: round 1 of X Y Z, with labelled marks, "C takes this piece" and "A and B mark again from here". 4–5 does not. It cannot simply copy it, because X Y Z is one of the six 4–5 tasks.
- **Fix, either way:**
  - (a) Put the same round-1 picture above the six rows. Make the X Y Z row "finish the game started above", which reveals only the first piece of one game.
  - (b) If the organizer prefers a board demonstration at the 4–5 table, leave the page as it is. Say so in the run notes so the omission is deliberate.

  I would choose (a). Without it, the 4–5 reader also has to work out "the piece from the left end first becomes worth 4" with no picture.

### S3. 2–3 P5/P6: the "half-panel" orders still need a quarter-panel mark

- **Where:** 2–3 P5 (X Z Y) and P6 (Y X Z, Z Y X), with the rule "The other two mark again from the new left end".
- **Problem:** In round 2 of all three orders, the person who does not take the piece must mark at 112.5 mm, which is 12.5 mm (one quarter) into the last panel. I checked this independently:

  | Order | Round-2 marks | Taker |
  |---|---|---|
  | X Z Y | A 112.5, B 75 | B |
  | Y X Z | B 112.5, C 75 | C |
  | Z Y X | C 112.5, A 75 | A |

  The cuts fall only at 25 and 75, as the outline says. The marks, however, need "a quarter of 8 is 2", which the 2–3 emphasis ("halves of a panel … half of 8 is 4") did not plan for, and the page gives no quarter example.
  - The game still works if that child sees that by 75 mm they have only 2, so their mark must come later.
  - Read literally, though, the rule makes a third grader place a mark they have not been shown how to find. The referee may stall there.
- **Fix:** Use the smallest change the organizer accepts:
  - Leave the page and put a facilitator note in the run notes: "the losing mark in round 2 is past the winner's; its exact place, 12.5 mm into the last panel, is not needed".
  - Or adjust the rule so that only the order of the marks matters: "…whoever's mark comes first takes that piece."
  - Do not add a quarter-panel worked example to 2–3.

### S4. 4–5 P1's general question can be read as "which of these"

- **Quote:** "Share each of these trains with as few cuts as you can. Which trains of four red cards and four blue cards can be shared with one cut?"
- **Problem:** Coming straight after "these trains", the second sentence invites the answer "the second one". Only three of the six drawn trains have four reds and four blues. The intended destination is a general test: one cut works exactly when the first four cards hold two reds (36 of the 70 orders).
- **Fix:** For example: "How can you tell, without trying, whether a train of four red cards and four blue cards can be shared with one cut?" Or: "Which of all the trains of four red and four blue cards can be shared with one cut?"

### S5. 4–5 is missing the outline's "any two tastes" claim

- **Outline requirement:** "kernel 1's sliding-window explanation for any train and any two tastes".
- **What the packet asks:** P2 asks the train version. P3 asks only about the specific A and B cards: fewest cuts, why one cut fails, and uniqueness.
- **What is missing:** No problem asks whether two cuts always suffice on the strip for two people with any cards. That is Austin's two-knife statement and the continuous form of the same sliding window.
- **Fix:** Add one sentence at the end of P3, or a short new problem after it: "If the two people used cards with other numbers, could two cuts always do it? Explain." It would fit at the bottom of p. 2 if the P2 answer space is trimmed slightly. Otherwise give it its own space on p. 3 before the X Y Z problem.

### S6. K–1 P1's two-column layout can read as 8-card trains

- **Where:** K–1 p. 1. Six 4-card trains are drawn in two columns, with a 5 mm gap between cards and a 17 mm gap between columns.
- **Problem:** When a kindergartner looks across a row, the left and right trains read as one row of eight. This is the same length as the trains in P3, which makes it worse.
- **Fix:** Separate the trains more clearly, for example with a much wider column gap or each 4-card train on a faint backing strip. Alternatively, draw one train per row with slightly smaller cards. There is about 70 mm of unused space at the bottom of the page.

## Consider (minor)

### C1. Recording materials

K–1 P3 and P5, 2–3 P1 and P4 ask children to draw trains in blank boxes, but the materials list has only pencils. Either supply red and blue crayons or markers, or accept circles and squares in pencil. This is a prep note, not a page change.

### C2. Card supply (prep note; the pages are fine)

| Band | Problems | Cards needed | Table's supply |
|---|---|---|---|
| K–1 | P2, P3, P5 | up to 6 reds and 4 blues per pair at once | 3 reds and 2 blues per pair kit |
| 2–3 | P2 | up to 6 reds and 4 blues | same |
| 2–3 | P4 | invites up to 14 cards | same |
| 4–5 | P1 | up to 6 reds and 4 blues | one pair kit only (3 reds, 2 blues) |

The 4–5 table cannot build its own P1 trains without an extra unmounted p. 1. At K–1 and 2–3, combining kits lets only one pair work at a time. The outline's "one extra unmounted copy of p. 1 per pair" should be treated as required for this visit.

### C3. K–1 P2 has no recording instruction for shareable trains

P1 says "draw a line at each place"; P2 only says "cross it out" for impossible trains. Either add "Make each train and draw a line at each place you pull it apart", or rely on P1's convention. I would add "Make each train", as P1 does.

### C4. 2–3 P1 has 8 answer slots for 6 trains

This is acceptable. Children who find only six and wonder why are doing the mathematics. Do not label the extra rows (the standard bans describing blanks); just be aware that some children will hunt for eight.

### C5. 2–3 P4 gaps are too narrow

The gap between boxes is 2.4 mm with 10 mm boxes. Children have to draw cut lines in those gaps, and 2.4 mm is tight. About 4 mm would help (12 boxes per row is enough).

### C6. 4–5 P8 has two loosely linked questions in an awkward order

The bound ("never cuts more than two cards") comes after the example showing the bound is needed (two reds for three people). Put the bound first and the example second, so the link reads as "and two can really be needed":

> "Explain why the marking game for three people, on any row of whole goods cards, never cuts more than two cards. Three people all value every red card at 1 … Can each of them get at least a third of the total while only one card is cut?"

A tie-break clause in the game rules would also help: "If marks are at the same place, either of those people may take the piece." Three identical tastes on R1 R2 tie in round 1, and P6's "whatever values the cards give" includes ties.

### C7. Strip drawings are at 0.7 scale

The strips are drawn 168 mm long but labelled 0–240 mm. A child who puts a ruler on the page will get 0.7 × the intended number. 2–3 P3's "Measure from the red end" points to the real strip, so this is probably fine. If space allows, the K–1 drawings could stay unlabelled as they are now.

### C8. 4–5 P4: three people, two trays

Children may ask who gets the trays. If the organizer wants it, the frame "so that it would not matter to any of the three people which tray they got" carries the meaning without adding a rule.

### C9. 4–5 P7: the paper R3 is not marked

The code supports a dashed outline for the paper card but does not use it. Drawing R3 dashed would match "using the paper R3".

### C10. Ordering in 2–3

P4 (the three-cut train question) sits between the strip and the marking game. It would read more continuously right after P2: trains, then trains with a deeper question, then strip, then game. This is optional; the current order also puts the deeper question late, which the context asks for.

## What works and should be kept

- The shared train rule ("cut it at some of the gaps … every card stays whole") keeps trains separate from the card-splitting rule in 4–5 P7 ("Here a card may be cut, and its value is spread evenly across it"), as the outline requires.
- The panel-cut worked example, "Y, 50 mm, worth 8 to card A → two 25 mm halves worth 4", appears before the first use of panel cutting in both 2–3 and 4–5.
- The cards are labelled clearly: "two-person card A/B", "three-person cards A, B and C", and "card U".
- The K–1 problems are each one or two sentences, use no values and no "envy-free" or "proportional", and pose real mathematics. P2 includes an impossible train; P3 is a game; P5 is a "decide whether possible" question.
- Each band has a contrasting impossible train: RBRBR (three reds) in K–1, RBRRBRB (three blues) in 2–3.
- The marking rows are drawn at actual size, 50 mm per panel, so children can measure marks on the page. The 4–5 P7 card row is also actual size (25 mm, touching), so the real cards can be laid on it.
- "First becomes worth 4" correctly handles zero-value stretches, for example B on X.
