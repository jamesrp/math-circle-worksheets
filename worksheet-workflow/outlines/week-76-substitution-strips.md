Week 76: Substitution strips

Mathematical kernels

1. The substitution A -> AB, B -> BA, starting from A, gives nested prefixes A, AB, ABBA, ABBABAAB, and ABBABAABBAABABBA. The infinite Thue–Morse word is well-defined because each new word begins with the old word. Within an aligned child-pair the letters differ. Equal neighbors therefore locate a seam, and decoding AB -> A, BA -> B reveals the previous scale. No genuine word contains AAA or BBB. No genuine word contains ABABA or BABAB: either possible pair alignment would force AAA or BBB in its parent. Consequently every genuine crop of at least five letters has an equal pair that identifies the pair alignment. A short crop can have an ambiguous or incomplete parent; do not silently treat a cut edge as a generation boundary. A finite legal-looking crop is not automatically a full generated word.

2. The infinite word is not eventually periodic. Every four-letter substituted block is ABBA or BAAB, so equal neighbors occur arbitrarily far out; their first index is odd when the initial A is index 0. An odd eventual period would shift one equal pair to an even starting index, impossible inside a child-pair. If an eventual period is 2q, reading every even position (which recovers the parent word) makes q an eventual period. Repeated halving leaves an odd period, a contradiction. The seam/decode mechanism, not a long finite inspection, proves the infinite statement. A repeating ABBA strip passes the immediate bans above yet fails after repeated decoding; short local bans alone do not establish the full substitution language or nonperiodicity. The students need not present a formal infinite proof to explore its concrete mechanism.

Sources: Richard G. Swan, The Morse Sequence, https://www.math.uchicago.edu/~swan/expo/Morse.pdf (recursive sequence, local factors and unique alignment). Jean-Paul Allouche and Jeffrey Shallit, The ubiquitous Prouhet–Thue–Morse sequence, https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~shallit/Papers/ubiq15.pdf, sections 2–4, especially Propositions 3–4. The elementary decoding/period-halving proof and prototype instances are independently developed around this established sequence. Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss, An aperiodic monotile, https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~csk/hat/ provides the broader research connection: hierarchical substitutions also underlie aperiodic tilings. This packet is a one-dimensional substitution investigation, not a proof of the hat theorem, and uses no hat artwork.

Suggested emphasis by level: one shared Grades 3–5 prototype, by prerequisites. Children should read short A/B strings, preserve left-to-right order, recognize even/odd positions with support and work with a partner. Growing, decoding, locating seams and inventing or rejecting forged strips form the concrete core; infinite nonperiodicity is a readiness-dependent adult-supported continuation. Do not force K–1 or a separate Grades 2–3 edition. Younger children can participate only when they retain the choices rather than the adult doing the decoding.

Materials

Per pair: 32 reusable two-sided or two-color square counters/cards (distinguish by A/B labels as well as color), two horizontal rows or a foldable paper strip, four small divider markers, pencil, scissors if cutting supplied strips, and a simple printed replacement key. Use counters at least 15 mm wide if handled separately; do not expect 32 physical counters to fit on one portrait page. Provide printed long strips separately or let them extend onto the table. Keep original and growing rows distinct so a just-created letter is not substituted again in the same generation. A partner can enforce one replacement per original tile. No special fabrication or exact-fit cutouts are required; physical handling and classroom use remain untested.

Prototype scope

Aim for about four substantial student pages; the writer chooses the actual problems. Keep the action child-controlled, with several purposeful contrasting instances, recoverable state and little transcription. Show an explicit small non-task input/intermediate/output visual before the first unfamiliar substitution or decoding convention. Put hints and discretionary proof scaffolding with adults. Avoid merely copying successive rows, enumerating long words by rote, printing the intended discovery as the procedure, or implying that finite repetition tests prove aperiodicity.
