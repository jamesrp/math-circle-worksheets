The guide's shared launch (p. 3), after a few minutes of handling the pieces, covers a 2 × 2 square with four squares, circles the dot in the middle of one side as an outline dot, boxes the centre dot as inside, and, if halves are unfamiliar, has a child put two halves together to make one square. Mathematical kernels

1. Paper and outline dots. Build a shape on the 20 mm dot grid from the kit's squares and halves. Every corner sits on a dot, pieces meet along whole sides, and no gap is left inside. Count the paper in halves, with a square counting as two halves. Then the outline dots plus twice the inside dots equal the number of halves plus 2. When no dot is inside, the outline has exactly two more dots than the shape has halves, whatever the shape. This is Pick's theorem, area = I + B/2 − 1, since the area is half the number of halves. Why, in terms an adult can show with the pieces: build the shape one piece at a time. A half laid against the shape along one side brings exactly one new corner to the outline, and a square brings two. A piece that fits into a notch, touching along two sides, covers the corner dot between those sides, and that dot goes inside. A half there brings no new corner, so the outline loses one dot and one dot goes inside. A square there brings one new corner, so the outline stays the same and one dot goes inside. A square in a slot touching three sides closes in two dots and the outline loses two. Each time, the outline plus twice the inside grows by one for each half. One half starts at 3 = 1 + 2. Every outline dot is a corner of some piece, since a piece has no dots except at its corners. Instances. One half has 3 outline dots and one square has 4. Two halves make exactly three shapes, each with 4 outline dots, the same as one square: the square, the triangle (0,0), (2,0), (1,1), whose long side has a dot in its middle, and the parallelogram (0,0), (1,0), (2,1), (1,1). Three halves make exactly four shapes, each with 5. Four halves make 14 shapes. Thirteen have 6 outline dots and none inside; the tilted square (1,0), (2,1), (1,2), (0,1) has 4 outline dots and one dot inside. These counts treat turns and flips as the same; counting a flipped shape as different, two halves make 4 shapes and three make 6. So four halves can never give 5 outline dots. With whole squares only the count is always even; in general it is odd exactly when the number of halves is odd. With whole squares only, n squares give 2n + 2 outline dots, minus two for each inside dot. Four squares give 10 for four of the five shapes and 8 (one inside) for the 2 × 2 square. Five give 12 for 11 of the 12 shapes and 10 (one inside) for the 2 × 2 square with one more square. Six give 14 (27 shapes), 12 (7 shapes, one inside) or 10 (only the 2 × 3 rectangle, two inside). For whole-square shapes the outline dots equal the boundary sides, which Week 26's K–1 pages (W26-K-v2) already count for four to eight tiles. Those pages have every four-tile shape, the shortest and longest boundaries, and which boundary numbers five or six tiles can make. Do not repeat those tasks with dots. The new parts here are the halves, the slanted sides and the dots inside.

2. Closing in a dot. A dot is inside a shape only when piece corners go all the way around it. A square's corner and a half's square corner each fill a quarter turn; a half's sharp corner fills an eighth. A piece has no dot except at its corners, so at least four pieces meet at every inside dot. Three pieces of any kind can never close in a dot, and four halves can: the tilted square, the only shape of four halves with a dot inside. Exactly six shapes close in a dot with four pieces, one for each way of placing squares and halves around it (each half with its square corner at the dot), with turns and flips the same. They use 4, 5, 6, 6, 7 and 8 halves, and each has as many outline dots as halves, from the tilted square (4) to the launch's 2 × 2 square (8). Each inside dot takes the place of two outline dots. So for a fixed amount of paper, the fewest outline dots come from closing in as many dots as possible. Paper worth two squares makes the domino, with 6 outline dots, or the tilted square, with 4. Paper worth three squares gives at best 8 with whole squares but 6 with halves. Paper worth four squares gives at best 8 with whole squares (the 2 × 2 square) but 6 with halves. Only two shapes reach 6, each with two dots inside: the tilted domino (1,0), (3,2), (2,3), (0,1), with a dot in the middle of each long side, and the hexagon (1,0), (2,1), (2,2), (1,3), (0,2), (0,1), which is two squares with a roof on each end. Closing in two dots takes at least six pieces (the 2 × 3 rectangle of six squares is one way) and at least eight halves. A K–1 argument that three pieces cannot close in a dot uses the pieces: put square corners around a dot and see that it takes four to go all the way round.

Every count above was checked by exhaustive enumeration. Shapes were modelled as unions of quarter-squares, with area from the number of halves and with inside and outline dots read from the eight 45° sectors around each dot, so Pick's rule was never assumed. The enumeration reproduces the published polyabolo counts 1, 3, 4, 14, 30, 107, 318, 1116 for one to eight halves (OEIS A006074, quoted from memory, not opened). All 1,580 allowed shapes of one to eight halves satisfy kernel 1. The effect of one added piece was checked on every allowed shape of up to six halves, and for an added half up to seven; every allowed shape of up to eight halves can be built one half at a time through allowed shapes. The four-piece, two-dot and fewest-outline facts were checked over all shapes of up to six pieces and eight halves, and the whole-square counts over all shapes of four to six squares. The gap facts under Materials were checked the same way.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 only. An adult reads every problem aloud. Children do not read, write letters or use a formula. They count pieces and dots up to about 10 or 12 (the instances run from 3 to 10, apart from five or six whole squares), and the adult writes any count. There are no letters I and B and no fractions. A half is a piece, and "two halves make a square" is something children check with their hands, as in the launch. Use the launch's words and marks: dots on the outline (circled) and dots inside (boxed). Start with kernel 1 on small amounts of paper, where every shape a child finds gives the same outline count. Whole squares are a familiar entry and halves the new part. The tilted square breaks the pattern and leads into kernel 2 in the later problems: closing in a dot with the fewest pieces or halves, and the fewest outline dots for a given amount of paper. Saying the whole trade (each inside dot takes two outline dots) is for the quickest pair, with the adult. The grades 2–3 and 4–5 tables work at the same time from the existing shared packet (N57-S-final-v3), so the K–1 pages must use the same kit and theme and share the launch, without copying the older bands' problems.

Materials

The K–1 table uses the week's kit as the guide lists it (W57-FAC-v4, p. 2). The two materials pages (N57-M-v1) are printed once on card stock: p. 1 has 60 ruled 20 mm squares, and p. 2 has 60 squares with diagonals, cut into 120 right-angled halves with 20 mm legs. They are bagged as five partner kits of 10 squares and 20 halves, with 10 squares and 20 halves spare. Each K–1 pair has one kit: 10 squares, 20 halves, two pencils, an eraser and two coloured pencils. A kit also lists a straightedge and scissors. K–1 needs neither, and the scissors stay with the parent: a piece cut smaller changes every fewest-pieces and fewest-halves answer. The older tables nearby cut duplicates and loose pieces, so keep the K–1 table to its own uncut pieces. Every instance above fits within one kit; the largest uses six squares or eight halves. Nothing beyond the kit is needed. The kit has not been tried with K–1 children. The guide's rehearsal (whether younger children can handle 20 mm pieces, and whether pieces sit on the dots) has not been done, and Week 57 is unpiloted.

Physical constraints the pages must respect. The pieces fit only a grid with 20 mm dot spacing, equal in both directions and printed at 100%. A board of n spacings is 20n mm wide. With 15 mm side margins on US Letter (185.9 mm of width), at most nine spacings (ten dots) fit across. The older pages use a 7 × 7-dot board (120 mm square) and two 5 × 5-dot boards (80 mm) stacked on one page. Every shape of up to seven halves fits a 5 × 5-dot board (four spacings each way), and so does every shape named in the kernels; some shapes of eight halves, and of five or six whole squares, need five or six spacings. Any shape printed for children to cover must be drawn at actual size, with 45° slants. Pieces lie flat with every corner on a dot. A half turned an eighth of a turn, with its short sides along diagonals, cannot have all its corners on dots, so the rule matters.

Pieces hide the dots under them. Outline dots sit at piece corners on the edge, partly covered. An inside dot has four or more corners meeting on it and is covered completely. The older boards' dots are about 1 mm across and vanish under a corner. So children need a way to see the dots before marking them. The adult can trace around the shape before the pieces are lifted, or children can cover a printed outline and then lift the pieces. Larger printed dots (about 4 mm) would leave part of every outline dot showing beside the pieces while an inside dot disappears. That is a material check of the two kinds of dot, but hand-cut pieces leave cracks, and nobody has tried it. Whether hand-cut pieces stay on the dots across a row of four or five is also untested.

Gaps break the counts. Up to five pieces, no shape can close off a gap. Six pieces can already close off a gap that reaches the outside only at a corner (one square and five halves), and so can seven halves or seven squares. Seven halves' worth of paper arranged this way has 8 outline dots, not the 9 that halves plus 2 predicts, and seven squares around a corner gap have 15, not 16. From eight squares (or ten halves) a ring can enclose a real hole. The 3 × 3 ring of eight squares has 16 outline dots (12 outside and 4 around the hole), not 18. Each kit holds enough pieces for both, so the pages need a rule against any gap, or tasks that stay within five pieces.

Records stay light. The adult writes any counts, and the child's marks are circles and boxes on dots. A pair's kit (ten squares and twenty halves) holds only one or two small shapes at a time, so a shape kept for comparing later belongs on paper.
