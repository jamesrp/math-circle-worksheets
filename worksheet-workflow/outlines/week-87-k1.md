Week 87 K–1 pages: Voting and agendas

Page header: "Week 87 / Voting and agendas / K–1". Packet id W87-K-v1; the footer reads "Bellingham Math Circle / Week 87 / W87-K-v1".

Week 87 has a card packet for grades 2–5 (W87-S-v2), a separate line packet for grades 3–5 (W87-L-v2), an upper packet for grades 4–5 (W87-U-v2) and five materials pages (W87-M-v1). It has no K–1 pages. These pages join the card visit. The adult guide (W87-F-v2, p. 1) gives an untested adult-led K–1 route through shared Problems 1–2. The adult reads each card aloud ("Voter 1 likes A best, then B, then C. A or B?"), a child puts that voter's token in the chosen well, and the children count. Then they choose who waits and move the tiles on the knockout mat while the adult reads each vote. The route leaves out card design and the line visit. Finding the higher of two letters on a card is looking, not reading, so on these pages the children cast every vote themselves. The adult reads the problems and checks each vote against the card. K–1 children have met "beats in a circle" with Week 24's random decks and ranked preference strips in Week 27. No K–1 page has majority votes or agendas yet.

Mathematical kernels

1. A circle of majorities. Three voter cards each rank A, B and C from top to bottom: V1 ABC, V2 BCA, V3 CAB. In a pair vote each card chooses the higher of the two letters on offer, even when its top letter is not on offer. More votes wins. A beats B 2–1 (V1, V3), B beats C 2–1 (V1, V2) and C beats A 2–1 (V2, V3). So no letter beats both others, although every card has a clear first, second and third. Why: the three cards read the circle A → B → C → A from three starting points, so each letter sits above the next letter of the circle on two cards out of three. With three voters a pair vote never ties, and a count never goes above three. The launch cards DFE, EFD, FED are the other kind: E beats D, F beats E and F beats D, each 2–1. So F beats both, and the demonstration does not give the circle away.

2. Who waits decides. In a knockout, two letters vote first and the winner meets the waiting letter. A letter wins every knockout exactly when it beats both others in pair votes: if it waits, it beats whoever comes; if it plays first, it wins both of its votes. A letter that loses both pair votes can never win. On the fixed cards the waiting letter always wins. B and C first: B wins, then A beats B. C and A first: C wins, then B beats C. A and B first: A wins, then C beats A. Why: in a circle, the first winner loses to exactly one letter, and that letter is the one waiting. There are only three ways to choose who waits (the order inside the first pair changes no vote), and they give three different winners. With three voters and three letters only these two cases happen. Of the 216 ways to give each voter one of the six possible cards, 12 make a circle, where every letter can win by waiting. The other 204 have one letter that beats both others; it wins all three knockouts, and making either other letter win is impossible. For example, with CAB, CBA, ABC, C beats A and B, each 2–1, so only C can win. B loses to both there and can never win.

3. Changing the cards. The circle breaks easily. All 12 circle cases use three different cards: ABC, BCA, CAB or the mirror set ACB, CBA, BAC, in some order. That is 2 of the 20 sets of three different cards, and 2 of the 56 sets when repeats are allowed. So any repeated card ends the circle. Replacing any one fixed card by any other card (15 possible changes) also ends it, and A, B and C are each the new all-round winner in 5 of these changes. Swapping two letters on one fixed card (9 possible swaps) always makes the letter that moved up win every knockout. For example, V1's ABC becoming BAC makes B beat A and C, each 2–1 (BAC and BCA). Why, for swaps of neighbouring letters: on each fixed card, every neighbouring pair is in the order that won its pair vote. Moving the lower letter up turns its 1–2 loss into a 2–1 win, and in the circle it already beat the third letter. So one swap can make any chosen letter the all-round winner. To build three cards on which a chosen letter, say A, wins every knockout, putting A on top of two cards always works, since A then gets two votes in each of its pair votes. Putting A on top of no card never works. Beating B needs two cards with A above B, and beating C needs two cards with A above C. A card without A on top has A above at most one letter, so three such cards give at most three of the four needed. With A on top of exactly one card it works only when A is in the middle of both other cards, one topped by B and one by C: BAC and CAB with ABC or ACB, 2 of the 20 such card sets.

Every count, catalog and claim above was checked by exhaustive enumeration: all 216 ordered card profiles and their pair votes and knockouts, all 15 single-card changes and 9 swaps of the fixed cards, and the launch example.

The week's line visit is left out. In it, five voters' favourites sit on a 0–10 line and the middle favourite beats every other location. It is a separate visit with its own launch and board. Each vote asks for up to ten distance counts plus ties, and the guide places it at grades 3–5 readiness.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1. An adult reads every problem aloud. Children recognize the letters A–F, match V1–V3 between card and token, and count to three; no number on these pages goes above three. Any recording is a tile placed in a box or a circled printed letter. Children cast every vote on unchanged cards and run the knockouts on the mat. Start with kernel 1's pair votes and kernel 2's knockouts on the fixed cards. Then take kernel 2's contrast, a card set where only one letter can ever win. Kernel 3 comes late, for the quickest pairs: one swap on one card, and building cards on which a chosen letter always wins. Explanations are shown with the cards ("Show me why B can never win here"), not written. The grades 2–3 and 4–5 tables work at the same time from the existing packet W87-S-v2, and the 4–5 table continues with upper Problem 1 (W87-U-v2). So the K–1 pages must use the same kit and theme and share the launch, without copying the older bands' problems. Do not reprint shared Problems 1–3 ("Is there a letter that wins against each of the other two?", making A, B and C win in turn, designing two sets of three different cards) or upper Problem 1's card sets (ABC, ACB, BAC and ACB, CBA, BAC).

Materials

The Week 87 kit (week-87-materials.pdf, W87-M-v1), printed single-sided at 100%. The guide prepares three pair kits for the older tables, so the K–1 table needs its own: two kits, one per pair. Each kit holds:

- From materials p. 1, cut: the three fixed ranking cards V1 ABC, V2 BCA and V3 CAB. Each is 45 × 80 mm, with a V-label band on top, then 1st, 2nd and 3rd rows about 23 mm tall, each with one large letter. Six 28 mm option tiles: A, A, B, B, C, C. Vote tokens, 18 mm discs. Use V1–V3 only and set V4 and V5 aside: card votes have three voters, and four voters could split two to two.
- Materials p. 4, kept whole: the tally mat and the knockout mat. The tally mat (170 × 90 mm) has two 30 mm Option boxes for the tiles on offer above two 54 mm wells, each with five 18 mm token spots, and a Tie well that card votes never use. The knockout mat (175 × 100 mm) has First pair 61 × 39 mm, Waiting 33 × 39, Out 61 × 39 (two tiles side by side), First winner 33 × 39, Final pair 61 × 39 and Winner 34 × 39. The mat labels are words, so the adult reads them; their places do the work.
- For kernel 3 only, from materials p. 2, cut: three blank V1–V3 cards of the same size and eighteen 21 mm letter slips, six each of A, B and C. A slip fits a card row with about 1 mm to spare above and below. One copy per pair is enough to build or change three cards. The older tables also use p. 3, a second blank set, which K–1 does not need.

So the K–1 table adds two copies each of pp. 1 and 4, and two of p. 2 if kernel 3 is used. Only cutting is involved; the mats stay whole. The guide's preparation estimates are untested and do not count the K–1 kits. Two optional additions, each a minute's work for the parent volunteer. First, a crayon dot of one colour on each card's V label and on its token, so V1–V3 also match by colour. Second, covers for the letter not on offer: letter slips turned face down (the pages print single-sided, so the backs are blank). With three covers on the cards, each voter chooses the highest letter still showing, so the cards themselves show the vote rule. Neither addition has been tried.

Physical constraints the pages must respect. The cards are imaginary voters, not the children's own choices. They stay unchanged through every pair vote and both rounds of a knockout, and a child's own favourite never votes. A voter whose top letter is not on offer still votes for the higher of the two letters on offer; the launch shows this with V3's FED in the D/E vote. The only cut cards in the kit are the circle's. Any other card set is built from blank cards and slips, or printed as a picture on the page to be read. Children never copy a printed set onto blank cards. A printed card keeps the cut card's layout (V label, then three rows, top choice first), so pictures and cards read alike; three actual-size cards fit across a page (157 of 180 mm). A printed box that holds a tile must be at least 31 mm on a side, and a printed token spot at least 18 mm. Each pair has one card set, one tally mat and one knockout mat, so it runs one vote and one knockout at a time. Tiles go back between knockouts; cards never change during one. Keep records light: the winner's tile on the mat, a circled letter, or a tile in a printed box, with the adult writing anything else. The whole-group launch handles the tiles and shows one pair vote on non-task cards (shared p. 1 uses DFE, EFD, FED, which exist only as pictures). It shows the knockout with the same cards later (shared p. 2). The kit has never been tried with K–1 children, and the week itself is unpiloted: cutting, token fit, resets and timing are unrehearsed.
