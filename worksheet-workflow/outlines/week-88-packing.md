Week 88: Packing and kissing circles

Mathematical kernels

1. For equal radius-r disks in a plane, at most six nonoverlapping outer disks can touch a central disk externally. Joining centers gives lengths 2r, requiring consecutive angles at least 60 degrees. Six exist, and equality forces every neighboring outer pair to touch. A physical construction is evidence; the angle argument is a proof.
2. Disk centers turn nonoverlap into distance at least the sum of radii. A ring of congruent radius-r outer disks about radius-R center has maximum floor(pi/arcsin(r/(R+r))). Diameter-40 outer disks about diameter-80 center allow nine; reversing sizes allows four. Mixed outer sizes require separate pair-angle bounds: using both sizes outside gives maxima eight about the 80 mm center and five about the 40 mm center. Do not place trigonometry on child entry pages.
3. Staggered rows of 4,3,4 diameter-40 disks fit a 160 x 110 mm rectangle: center row spacing 20 sqrt(3), total height 40+40 sqrt(3)=109.282... mm. This proves existence, not global optimality. A 160 x 108 mm rectangle cannot hold this specific straight-row 4,3,4 arrangement; four-disk rows are forced horizontally, and middle disks need at least 20 sqrt(3) vertical gap from each. Other arrangements are outside that upper-bound claim.

Sources: classical planar kissing theorem; exact center-distance geometry; Boyvalenkov, Dodunekov and Musin, Kissing numbers—a survey, arXiv:1507.03631 §1 pp.1–2, for the classical context and distinction from three-dimensional kissing. The reserved family retains a finite packing, equal-case rigidity, unequal and mixed-size continuation, and precise row-constrained sensitivity question.
