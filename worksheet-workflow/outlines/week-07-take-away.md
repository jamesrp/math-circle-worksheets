Mathematical kernels

1. Backward induction. In a take-away game there is a pile of counters; two players alternate removing counters, and each move must remove an allowed number (for example 1 or 2). Whoever takes the last counter wins. Every pile size is either a win for the player about to move or a loss: a pile is losing when every allowed move leads to a winning pile, and winning when at least one move leads to a losing pile. Working up from an empty pile classifies every size. A winning strategy means always moving to a losing pile, and it must answer every possible reply of the opponent, not just the replies you expect. The same game can be played with one token on a number track: start on the pile size and move the token down by an allowed amount; whoever lands on 0 wins. The track shows the pile size without recounting, and colouring the squares you want to leave for your opponent turns it into a map of the whole game.

2. Repeating patterns. With moves {1, 2} the losing piles are the multiples of 3. With moves {1, 2, 3} they are the multiples of 4. With moves {1, 3, 4} the losing piles are those leaving remainder 0 or 2 when divided by 7 (0, 2, 7, 9, 14, 16, 21, …). With moves {1, 3, 5} the losing piles are the even ones. With moves {2, 3} the losing piles leave remainder 0 or 1 when divided by 5 (piles of 0 and 1 are losing because no move is possible from 1). With moves {1, 4} the losing piles leave remainder 0 or 2 when divided by 5. For any finite set of allowed moves the win/lose pattern eventually repeats: each label depends only on a fixed number of earlier labels, and there are only finitely many possible windows of labels (pigeonhole). Changing the rule changes the pattern, and the greedy move (take as many as you can) is often wrong.

3. Beyond one pile (extension). If the player who takes the last counter loses instead, with moves {1, 2} the losing piles are those leaving remainder 1 when divided by 3. Two piles of the same game, where each turn you move in one pile of your choice, are a loss for the player to move when the piles are equal (copy your opponent in the other pile); unequal piles need the Sprague–Grundy value (the smallest number not among the values of the positions you can move to). Sources: Berlekamp, Conway and Guy, Winning Ways; Siegel, Combinatorial Game Theory.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 plays 1-or-2 games with real counters on piles up to 10 and with a token on a number track, plays the adult, and hunts for the adult's secret; grades 2–3 play {1, 3, 4} games in pairs, colour a number track to find the piles they want to leave, and test a claimed strategy against every reply; grades 4–5 find and explain repeating patterns, change the rule and compare, and try the last-counter-loses and two-pile versions as extensions.

Materials

Counters, plenty. Printed number tracks of squares from 0 to 20 and from 0 to 30, squares about 3/4 inch (big enough for a pattern block to stand on as the token), several tracks per page for older children. One token per pair (any pattern block). Coloured pencils, paper, pencils, small whiteboards. Children play in pairs; at the 4–5 table the third child plays the adult or referees, rotating every game. The adults know the winning strategies and can play to win so children can try to work out how the adult always wins.
