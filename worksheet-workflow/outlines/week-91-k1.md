Week 91 K–1 pages: Rotor-router machines

Page header: "Week 91 / Rotor-router machines / K–1". Packet id F91-K-v1; the footer reads "Bellingham Math Circle / Week 91 / F91-K-v1".

Week 91 has one shared student packet for grades 2–5 (F91-S-v2), a grades 4–5 return packet on roads (F91-RV-v1) and seven materials pages (F91-M-v1). It has no K–1 pages. The adult guide (F91-FAC-v3, p. 2) gives an untried adult-read K–1 route. On the one-switch board the children send 12 counters and are asked "Can X come twice in a row?". On the four-exit tree they are asked "Send four counters. Does every exit get one?" and "Make the first counter land on D." The children move every counter and flip every card. These pages turn that route into real problems. Do not copy the older packet's problems. Problem 1 sends 12 counters through one switch with each first arrow, then asks which of XYXY, XXYY, YXYX and XYYX the switch can make. Problem 2 sends the first counter to D and asks for every order that keeps D first. Problem 3 asks for the orders ACBD, BDAC, ABCD and DABC. Problems 4–6 use the eight-exit tree. Problem 7 compares 12 coin tosses with 12 switch arrivals and asks for a six-counter order that only the coin could make.

The rule, as on the kit. Each card shows the NEXT exit. A counter enters at the top. At each circle it follows the arrow, and then that card is flipped side to side, so ↙ becomes ↘. Cards are never reset between counters, and one counter finishes before the next one enters. A counter waits beside a card, not on it, so the arrow stays visible. Exits keep their counters. A new trial means choosing new starting arrows and clearing the exits.

Mathematical kernels

1. One switch takes turns. Every departure flips the arrow for the next one, so a single switch sends counters X, Y, X, Y, … or Y, X, Y, X, …. The first arrow decides which, and no start ever sends two counters in a row to the same exit. Pair the counters (first and second, third and fourth, and so on): each pair puts one counter in each exit. So after any number of counters the two piles are equal or differ by one, and the extra counter goes to the first arrow's exit. Twelve counters give 6 and 6 from either start. Seven give 4 to the first arrow's exit and 3 to the other, and no start gives 5 and 2. At no moment is one pile two ahead. A coin is different: heads sends a counter to X and tails to Y, and the coin can make any order at all. Of the 16 equally likely orders from four tosses, 14 have two of the same exit in a row; only XYXY and YXYX do not. Twelve tosses split exactly 6 and 6 in only 924 of the 4,096 possible orders, about 23%. So the switch shares exactly evenly and is completely predictable, and the coin does neither. After one counter you can say where every later counter will go. The K–1 decisions here are possible or impossible (two in a row, 5 and 2 from seven), which first arrow gives a chosen exit the extra counter, and what always happens.

2. The four-exit tree shares fairly from any start. The tree has three cards: the top card, a left card above exits A and B, and a right card above C and D. Whatever the starting arrows, the first four counters land on four different exits. Every card is then pointing as it did at the start, so counters 5–8 repeat counters 1–4 in the same order, and so on. Why: the top card takes turns, so two of any four counters go left and two go right. Each lower card takes turns with its own two, sending one to each of its exits. Each card has flipped an even number of times, so it is back where it started. Consequences that K–1 can see from every start: eight counters give 2 to each exit and twelve give 3. No exit gets a second counter before every exit has one. Any four counters in a row, such as counters 3 to 6, reach all four exits. The fifth counter always lands where the first did. After five counters, only the first counter's exit has two. After six, the two exits with two are those of counters 1 and 2. These are always one of A, B and one of C, D, so A and B never both have two after six counters. After seven, only the fourth counter's exit still has one. D gets its second counter at counter 5 at the soonest, when D comes first, and at counter 8 at the latest, when D comes fourth. The fact needs every exit at the same depth, cards never reset, and one counter at a time. The kit's tree meets all three.

3. Choosing the start to get the order you want. Each card starts one of two ways, so there are 8 starts, and each gives a different order of the first four counters. Card words list top, left, right: LLL gives ACBD, LLR ADBC, LRL BCAD, LRR BDAC, RLL CADB, RLR DACB, RRL CBDA, RRR DBCA. A first counter's path touches only two cards: the top card must point to the chosen exit's side, and that side's card must point to the exit. The other lower card is never touched, so exactly two starts send the first counter to any chosen exit. To send it to A, use LLL or LLR; the right card can point either way. The second counter always goes to the other side of the top card. So two exits on opposite sides can come first and second in exactly one way (B then C: only LRL, giving BCAD), or in two ways if either order will do (LRL giving BCAD, or RRL giving CBDA). Two exits on the same side never can come first and second, whatever the start: A and B, C and D, or the same exit twice. Only 8 of the 24 orders of A–D can be made. Questions about the first one or two counters keep this at K–1 size. Use exits and pairs other than the older packet's D-first problem and its four target orders.

Every count, order and catalogue above was checked by simulating every start: the switch with each first arrow for up to 40 counters, and the four-exit tree from all 8 starts for up to 48 counters, including every window of four counters in a row. The coin counts were checked by listing all orders.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1. An adult reads every problem aloud and names the letters X, Y and A–D. Numbers stay at about 12 or below, and the adult may write arrival letters. Children choose the starting arrows, move every counter and flip every card, with the partner checking each move. Start with kernel 1 on the one-switch board, including the coin contrast. Then take kernel 2 on the four-exit tree (what always happens from any start, and soonest and latest). Kernel 3 (setting the arrows for the first one or two counters, possible and impossible) comes late, for the quickest pairs. The eight-exit tree (seven cards to follow) and the roads (counters that revisit circles, a conserved score, addition) are not for K–1. The pages need their own small picture of one move before first use (counter at a switch, counter moved to the arrow's exit, card flipped), drawn on a board or letters that no task uses. The grades 2–3 and 4–5 tables work at the same time from the existing packet F91-S-v2, so the K–1 pages must use the same kit and theme and share its launch (a whole-group demonstration of one move at one switch) without copying the older bands' problems listed above.

Materials

The Week 91 kit (week-91-materials.pdf, F91-M-v1), printed single-sided at 100%, with landscape pages kept landscape. The guide's three kits serve the older tables at the same time (guide p. 2), so the K–1 table needs its own: two kits, one per pair, without road cards. Each kit holds:

- The one-switch board (materials p. 1, portrait): a 32 mm switch circle marked ENTRY above 32 mm exits X and Y.
- The four-exit tree board (p. 2, landscape, filling the page): three 24 mm switch circles, the top one marked ENTRY, above four 30 mm exits labelled A, B, C, D from left to right.
- The seven tree cards from one copy of p. 6. Each is cut, folded and glued into an 18 × 22 mm card with NEXT upright on both faces, an arrow pointing down-left on one face and down-right on the other. It flips by turning it side to side.
- 16 counters no wider than 12 mm (for example 1 cm base-ten cubes) and a coin.
- Arrival strips from p. 7 (rows of twelve 15 mm squares).

The addition is 14 tree cards, 32 counters, two coins, two copies each of pp. 1, 2 and 6, and one of p. 7. The guide estimates, untimed, about an hour to prepare 33 cards and 3 markers, so this adds about half an hour. The road boards, road cards and eight-exit board belong to the older tables and stay off the K–1 table.

Physical constraints the pages must respect:

- The four-exit board needs a whole landscape page at actual size. A portrait K–1 page cannot reprint it for cards to sit on, so pages refer to the board and use smaller pictures for planning and recording. Any printed circle that holds a card must be at least 24 mm across, with room beside it for a waiting counter; the cards' corners already overhang 24 mm circles by about 2 mm.
- The four-exit tree uses three of a kit's seven cards, and the one switch uses one. With the four spares, a pair can lay a copy of its starting arrows beside the board, a record that needs no writing.
- The counters are identical, so exit piles show how many arrived but not in what order. Keeping an arrival order needs letters written in order (by the adult, or by a child who can write X or A–D), or a chart with one row per exit and a mark at each counter's arrival number. Do not ask K–1 to keep a tally while moving counters.
- If a page asks children to draw starting arrows, its circles must be large enough for a K–1 hand to draw a slanted arrow. The older packet's miniature circles are 8.8 mm across, far too small.
- The boards label exits with letters only. Pages should use the same letters, which the adult names, and not pictures that the boards lack.
- The cards and the partner enforce the rule. Resetting a card "to help", flipping the wrong card, or setting the counter on top of a card breaks the alternation and changes the answers, so the partner checks each move before the card flips. Trials of up to 12 counters fit the 16 in a kit. For the coin, the adult says which face sends a counter to X.

The kit has not been tried with K–1 children. Printing, cutting, folding, flipping, counter placement and timing are unrehearsed at every table (the guide's physical check is not done), and the week is unpiloted.
