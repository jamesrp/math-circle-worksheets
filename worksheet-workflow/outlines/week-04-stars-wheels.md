Week 4: Stars and secret wheels

Mathematical kernels

1. Skip-drawing stars. Put n dots evenly around a circle. Start at a dot and repeatedly draw a straight line to the dot k places clockwise, until you are back where you started. You visit n ÷ gcd(n, k) dots. If gcd(n, k) is bigger than 1 you must lift the pencil and start again at a dot not yet visited, and the finished drawing has gcd(n, k) separate pieces, all the same shape. With 12 dots: skip 1 or 5 (or 7 or 11) visits all 12 dots, and skip 5 draws a 12-pointed star; skip 2 draws 2 hexagons; skip 3 draws 3 squares; skip 4 draws 4 triangles; skip 6 draws 6 line segments. With 10 dots: skip 3 draws a 10-pointed star; skip 2 draws 2 pentagons; skip 4 draws 2 five-pointed stars; skip 5 draws 5 segments. With 5 dots, skip 2 is the five-pointed star. With 7 dots (a prime number), every skip from 1 to 6 visits every dot. Skip k and skip n − k draw the same picture in opposite directions. Hopping a counter around the dots is the same thing: hops of k on a ring of n places reach every place exactly when n and k have no common factor bigger than 1.

2. Shift wheels. Two paper circles carry the same 26 letters, evenly spaced, one circle inside the other, joined at the centre by a brass fastener. Turning the inner wheel k places encodes each letter as the letter k places later, wrapping from Z back to A. Turning back k places undoes it, and so does turning forward 26 − k places. Two shifts in a row make one shift by the sum (wrapping past 26). Because there are only 25 different shifts, a message in a secret shift can be cracked by trying shifts, and a short word gives itself away: repeated letters stay repeated (the double letter in BALLOON stays a double letter), and a one-letter word is usually A or I. Repeating a shift of k on a wheel of n positions visits the same positions as the skip-k star.

3. Times tables on a circle (extension). On n numbered dots (0 to n − 1), draw a line from each dot m to dot 2m, counting around past n; with 20 or more dots a heart-shaped curve (a cardioid) appears among the lines. With 3m instead of 2m a two-lobed curve appears.

Sources: Coxeter, Regular Polytopes, §2.8 on star polygons {n/k}; any elementary number theory text on gcd and cyclic groups (for example Judson, Abstract Algebra, Chapter 4); Rozhkovskaya, Math Circles for Elementary School Students, Lesson 2, the "Magic Coding Wheel".

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 hops a counter around rings of 4, 5 and 6 dots with skips of 1, 2 and 3, draws the stars, and decides whether one line reaches every dot; grades 2–3 draw stars on 8, 10 and 12 dots, predict how many times the pencil must be lifted, and send secret messages to a partner with a shift wheel; grades 4–5 find and explain the rule for how many pieces a skip-star has on many different dot counts, crack shift-wheel messages, and combine shifts, with the times-table circle as an extension.

Materials

Printed circles of evenly spaced dots: 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15, 16, 18 and 24 dots. For K–1 the circle is about 5 inches across and each dot is big enough for a counter to sit on; older children's circles can be smaller, several to a page. Coloured pencils, rulers, counters. Shift-wheel templates for the 2–3 and 4–5 tables: an outer circle about 5.5 inches across and an inner circle about 4 inches across, each with A to Z evenly spaced around the edge, to be cut out by the children (scissors on the table) and joined with a brass fastener (about 20 fasteners available). K–1 children do not read letters; any K–1 secret-turn task uses a ring of 6 or 8 pictures instead. Paper, pencils, small whiteboards. Children work in pairs: one draws or hops while the other checks, or one encodes while the other decodes.
