Week 63 return visit: Swaps, match totals and new forbidden homes

This is a return visit to the lettered cards and home strips of Week 63 (one shared packet, W63-S-v4). There, children hunted for home-free rows (2 for three cards, 9 for four, 44 for five), found that exactly n − 1 cards at home is impossible, repaired overlap counts with home-groups (24 − 6 − 6 + 2 = 14, then all four and five groups), and, at the 4–5 table, drew arrows from each card's letter to the home where it sits and rebuilt home-free rows from smaller ones, D_n = (n − 1)(D_{n−1} + D_{n−2}). Do not repeat those catalogs, overlap repairs or constructions. The questions below are new: how many swaps a row is from home, how many home matches a whole deck holds, and what happens when other homes are forbidden. The base week used loops only to split home-free rows into two families, and its guide named "an arbitrary new forbidden-place board" as a different problem needing fresh enumeration. Elsewhere in the library, Week 3's return visit counts swaps of neighbouring cards (inversions) and Week 2's return visit exchanges neighbouring unlabelled lamp counters. No worksheet counts swaps of any two labelled cards or their parity (the app's Cup swaps has the fewest-swaps count), asks children to total the home matches of a deck, or counts rows when a card is barred from a home other than its own.

Mathematical kernels

1. Fewest swaps and loops. A move swaps any two cards on the strip, near or far; the homes stay fixed. Draw each card's arrow from its letter to the home where it sits, so the row splits into loops, a card at home being a loop of one. The fewest swaps that bring a row home is the number of cards minus the number of loops. Swapping two cards in the same loop splits it into two, and swapping cards from different loops joins them, so every swap changes the number of loops by exactly one, and the home row has one loop per card. Any swap that puts at least one card home splits a loop, so it lies on a shortest route; one swap puts two cards home exactly when they sit in each other's homes. Instances: BAC 1 swap, BCA 2, BADC 2, BCDA 3, BADEC 3, BCDEA 4. Rows by fewest swaps from home: 1, 3, 2 for three cards; 1, 6, 11, 6 for four; 1, 10, 35, 50, 24 for five. The hardest rows are the single loops through every card, needing n − 1 swaps; there are (n − 1)! of them (2, 6, 24). Of the nine home-free four-card rows, BADC, CDAB and DCBA need 2 swaps and the other six need 3; of the 44 home-free five-card rows, 20 need 3 and 24 need 4. Starting from every card at home, leaving no card at home takes ⌈n/2⌉ swaps: 2 for three cards (reaching BCA or CAB), 2 for four (reaching BADC, CDAB or DCBA), 3 for five (for example swap A with B, C with D, then A with E, giving BEDCA). A swap changes only two cards, or equally, every loop of a home-free row has at least two cards. Parity: since each swap changes the number of loops by one, every sequence of swaps from a row to home has the same odd or even count, and a row can be brought home in exactly m swaps precisely when m is at least its fewest and has the same parity. BACDE comes home in 1, 3, 5, … swaps, never 2 or 4. Half of all rows are an even number of swaps from home (3 of 6, 12 of 24, 60 of 120). More generally, the fewest swaps from row R to row S is n minus the number of loops of the arrows from each card's home in R to its home in S.

2. Counting home matches two ways. Take the complete deck of rows: the six A–C and the 24 A–D outcome cards (for five cards, the 120 rows exist only as a prediction). Mark every card that is at home. The total number of marks equals the number of rows, n!, for every n ≥ 1, so the rows of a complete deck average exactly one card at home, however many cards there are. To see why, count the marks by card instead of by row: a named card is home in (n − 1)! rows (fix it and arrange the rest), and n × (n − 1)! = n!. Counting the same way by groups, the number of pairs of cards at home together, summed over the deck, is n!/2 (n ≥ 2), and the number of such triples is n!/6 (n ≥ 3). Instances: the A–C rows ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA carry 3, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1 marks, 6 in all, two for each letter, and 3 pairs, all in ABC. A–D rows with exactly 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 matches number 9, 8, 6, 0, 1, so 8 + 12 + 0 + 4 = 24 marks, six per letter, and 12 pairs. A–E, predicted without listing: 120 marks, 24 per letter, 60 pairs. A second fact comes from sorting into exact piles. The rows whose home cards are exactly a named set of k cards are the home-free rows of the other n − k cards, keeping their letters, so the pile of rows with exactly k matches has C(n, k) × D_{n−k} rows. For A–D, the 8 one-match rows are the two home-free rows of the other three cards, for each of the four letters, and the 6 two-match rows are one swap for each of the six pairs. For A–E the piles are 44, 45, 20, 10, 0, 1. The piles do not overlap, so n! = Σ C(n, k) × D_{n−k}, and 44 = 120 − 45 − 20 − 10 − 0 − 1 with nothing to repair, a contrast with the base week's overlapping groups. The home-free pile and the exactly-one pile always differ by one, alternately: 2 and 3, 9 and 8, 44 and 45, 265 and 264. That is, D_n − n × D_{n−1} = (−1)^n. From the base week's rule D_n = (n − 1)(D_{n−1} + D_{n−2}), the difference D_n − n × D_{n−1} equals −(D_{n−1} − (n − 1) × D_{n−2}). So it keeps size one and flips sign at each step, starting from D_2 − 2 × D_1 = 1.

3. Other forbidden homes. Replace "no card in its own home" by any list of forbidden spots (card X may not sit in home Y). The number of rows that avoid every forbidden spot is Σ (−1)^k × r_k × (n − k)!, where r_k counts the ways to choose k forbidden spots with no two sharing a card or a home. The base week's per-row cancellation still works; only the overlap sizes change. Requiring k chosen spots leaves (n − k)! rows when no two share a card or a home, and no rows otherwise. Own-home spots never clash, so r_k = C(n, k) and the base formula returns. The number of forbidden spots does not decide the count. With four cards and four forbidden spots: the own homes leave 9 rows; A-in-A, B-in-B, C-in-C and A-in-B leave 7; A and B kept out of homes A and B leave 4 (CDAB, CDBA, DCAB, DCBA); card A kept out of every home leaves 0. Strip rule: no card in its own home or in the home just to its right (E has only its own). The 2n − 1 forbidden spots A-in-A, A-in-B, B-in-B, …, D-in-E, E-in-E form a chain in which exactly neighbouring spots clash, so r_k = C(2n − k, k). Three cards leave 1 row (BCA). Four leave 3 (BCDA, CDAB, DCAB), and 24 − 7 × 6 + 15 × 2 − 10 × 1 + 1 = 3. Five leave 16, and 120 − 9 × 24 + 28 × 6 − 35 × 2 + 15 × 1 − 1 = 16. Ring rule, Lucas's ménage problem: E also may not sit in home A. The chain closes into a ring of 2n spots, r_k = (2n/(2n − k)) × C(2n − k, k) (Kaplansky), and the counts are 1, 2, 13, 80 for three to six cards (Touchard's formula). Four cards leave BCDA and CDAB, and 24 − 8 × 6 + 20 × 2 − 16 × 1 + 2 = 2. Five leave 13, and 120 − 10 × 24 + 35 × 6 − 50 × 2 + 25 × 1 − 2 = 13: exactly the 16 strip rows less the three with E in home A (ECDAB, ECDBA, EDABC). A smaller hunt: no card in its own home or either neighbouring home leaves only CDAB for four cards, and four rows for five (CDEAB, CDEBA, DEABC, EDABC).

Every count, catalog and identity above was checked by exhaustive enumeration: fewest swaps by breadth-first search through six cards (row to row through five), exact-m parity through five cards and m ≤ 7, match totals, pairs, triples, piles and D_n − n × D_{n−1} through seven cards, and every forbidden-spot catalog by enumeration. The strip, ring and four-card rules were also checked against their alternating sums: the strip rule through six cards, the ménage count through seven.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 (match a card's picture to its home's, count to ten; the adult reads and writes) starts with every card at home and swaps two cards at a time until no card is home, looking for the fewest swaps with three cards and then four (2 and 2), brings a mixed row such as BCA or BADC home in the fewest swaps, and puts a counter on every card at home on the six A–C outcome cards to compare counters with cards (6 and 6, two per letter); a short forbidden-picture hunt on a printed actual-size board (three cards: only BCA) is an option. Grades 2–3 (four letters, counting to 24, the adult may write the sums) take kernel 1 with four cards: fewest swaps for given rows, which home-free rows need only two, a hardest row, and why a swap brings two cards home only when they sit in each other's homes. They take kernel 2 on the 24-card deck: count the marks by row and by letter, and sort the deck into its 9, 8, 6, 0, 1 piles. Optionally they ask whether the number of forbidden spots decides the count, checked with the 24 cards. Grades 4–5 (the base week's arrows or a worked visual of them, totals to 120, arguments about every row) take kernel 1's loop explanation, five-card hardest rows and the parity question; kernel 2's five-card predictions without listing (120 marks, 60 pairs), the exact piles and the 44-and-45 surprise; and kernel 3's strip and ring rules (3 and 2 rows for four cards, 16 and 13 for five) with the repaired overlap count, as a continuation or second visit.

Materials

Physical constraints the pages must respect. Homes stay fixed and a swap exchanges two cards wherever they are, which differs from Week 3's return visit (neighbouring positions only). Pages must say "any two cards" and show one swap of two non-neighbouring cards, before and after, on non-task letters, as the base used U–X. Cards sit on the lower edge of their homes; when a card is placed, only the top 5 mm of a home (its letter and small picture) stays visible. Any mark a page adds to a home, such as pictures of refused cards, must sit in that top band or above the cell. With three or four cards, cover homes D and E, or E, without relabelling. A kit has two strips, so a pair can keep one start or target row unchanged while working on the other; never ask for two reference rows at once. A printed board on which real cards are placed needs homes of at least 35 × 45 mm. Five across take 175 mm, so one such board fits across a US Letter page. Keep records light: a running word of swapped card pairs (for example AB, CD, AE) and the final row, with swaps counted afterwards. Do not ask for a tally kept during the moves. Each deck has five cards, so six-card facts are adult background. The 24 outcome cards cover about 36,300 mm² before gaps: sort them on the table, not in a printed box. Their 9 × 10 mm cells take pencil marks or small counters, not cards. Two physical copies with the same row ID are one row. For kernel 3, the strips show only own-home pictures, so any new forbidden rule must be visible: state it per card, or draw it, for example the refused cards' pictures above each home or a card-by-home grid with forbidden cells shaded. A grid needs a non-task example before first use showing a row as one mark in each line and column. The arrows (from a card's letter to the home where it sits; reading QRSTP as P → Q is the reversed error) were met only on the base packet's p. 7 (Grades 4–5). Children who did not reach it need a worked non-task visual before first use. The K–1 parent can run the swap entry: the pictures show whether a card is home, and counts stay under ten.
