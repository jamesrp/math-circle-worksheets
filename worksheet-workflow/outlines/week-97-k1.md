Week 97 K–1 pages: Square remainders

Page header: "Week 97 / Square remainders / K–1". Packet id W97-K-v1; the footer reads "Bellingham Math Circle / Week 97 / W97-K-v1".

These pages add a K–1 packet to Week 97, which has one Grades 3–5 packet (W97-35-v2) and no K–1 pages. Until now the guide (W97-FAC-v3, p. 2) has given the K–1 table an adult-led route of about 15 minutes. The children build squares from 1 × 1 to 5 × 5 and put each into groups of 3 ("Can a square leave 2?"), then groups of 4 ("Can a square leave 2 or 3?"), while the parent reads and records. That shows what the parent can run: building squares up to 25 counters and taking away small groups. The guide calls K–1 success on this route a prediction, untested. The new pages should give the table its own substantial problems on the same action.

Mathematical kernels

1. Squares leave only some leftovers. A child builds a filled square with n counters along each side (n rows of n; side 0 is no counters), then takes away groups of m counters, any m at a time, until fewer than m are left. What remains is the leftover, and it can be 0. Sides n and n + m always give the same leftover. The bigger square is the smaller one plus two n-by-m strips and an m-by-m square, and each of those falls into whole groups, one row of m at a time. So the leftovers of sides 1 to m repeat forever, and they are the only leftovers a square of any size can make. Leftovers for sides 1 to 6: groups of 2 give 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0 (a square has the same leftover as its side). Groups of 3 give 1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, so never 2. Groups of 4 give 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, so never 2 or 3. Groups of 6 give 1, 4, 3, 4, 1, 0, so never 2 or 5. Groups of 5 give 1, 4, 4, 1, 0, 1, so never 2 or 3. The "never" belongs to squares, because piles and rectangles can leave anything. A pile of 2 leaves 2 in groups of 3, and so does a 2-by-4 rectangle (8). A 2-by-3 rectangle (6) leaves 2 in groups of 4, and a 3-by-5 rectangle (15) leaves 3. There are two explanations a K–1 child can check with tiles. For groups of 4, a 2-by-2 block is one group, so a square with an even side splits into blocks and leaves 0 (4-by-4 is 4 blocks, 6-by-6 is 9). A square with an odd side is the even square inside it plus two equal arms and a corner (3-by-3 = 4 + 2 + 2 + 1, 5-by-5 = 16 + 4 + 4 + 1). The two arms together make whole groups, so the corner is the 1 left. For groups of 3, cut strips three wide off the square; each strip falls into rows of 3. What remains is a corner square of side 0, 1 or 2, holding 0, 1 or 4 counters, which leave 0, 1 and 1. In a 5-by-5 square, three columns make five groups and three rows of the last two columns make two more. The 2-by-2 corner gives one more group and leaves 1. K–1 children can conjecture from sides 1 to 6 that a filled square never leaves 2 in groups of 3, however big it is, and an adult can show the strips. The repeat means sides 1 to 3 settle it, but at this age the repeat will be a pattern rather than a proof.

2. One leftover can hide several squares. A partner builds a secret square from a fixed set of side cards, takes the groups away out of sight, and shows only the leftover. Often nobody can name the square from that. What can be done is to name every square that could have made that leftover, and the answer depends on the group size and on which side cards are allowed. Sides 1 to 5, groups of 3: leftover 1 hides 1, 2, 4 and 5, and 0 hides only 3. Sides 1 to 5, groups of 4: leftover 1 hides 1, 3 and 5 (the odd sides), and 0 hides 2 and 4. Sides 1 to 5, groups of 5: leftover 1 hides 1 and 4, leftover 4 hides 2 and 3, 0 hides only 5, and leftovers 2 and 3 hide nothing. Sides 1 to 6, groups of 6: leftover 1 hides 1 and 5, 4 hides 2 and 4, 3 hides only 3, and 0 hides only 6. Sides 1 to 6, groups of 4: leftover 1 hides 1, 3 and 5, and 0 hides 2, 4 and 6. Adding a card changes the answers. With groups of 5, adding side 6 makes leftover 1 hide 1, 4 and 6. Take a prime group size p and sides 1 to p. The square of side p is alone with leftover 0. Every other leftover that occurs hides exactly two squares, and their sides add to p (1 and 4, 2 and 3 for groups of 5; 1 and 2 for groups of 3). The reason is that p divides (x − y)(x + y) only when it divides one of the factors. This is adult background; the guide (p. 5) shows it by taking a corner off a square of counters and re-forming the L that is left into a rectangle. A composite size can hide more squares within one round of sides: with groups of 8, leftover 1 hides sides 1, 3, 5 and 7. Seeing that takes 49- and 64-counter squares, but kernel 3 gives K–1 a smaller view of it.

3. Every odd square leaves 1 in groups of 8. Build an odd square from its middle out. Start with one counter. A ring of 8 around it makes 3-by-3, a ring of 16 makes 5-by-5, and a ring of 24 makes 7-by-7. Each new ring is a whole number of groups of 8 (8, 8 + 8, 8 + 8 + 8). The k-th ring has 8 × k counters because it splits into eight equal straight runs of k. So taking away groups of 8 always leaves the middle counter: the squares of 1, 9, 25 and 49 counters each leave 1. Even squares leave 0 or 4: sides 2, 4, 6 and 8 leave 4, 0, 4 and 0. So in groups of 8 a square can leave only 0, 1 or 4, and leftover 1 hides every odd square the side cards allow (1, 3 and 5 among sides 1 to 6). This is the older packet's composite surprise (with bundles of 8, output 1 hides four inputs), seen from the middle out. Each ring is also two of the squares' growth Ls together, two consecutive odd numbers: 8 = 3 + 5, 16 = 7 + 9, 24 = 11 + 13.

Kernel 1 is the core and extends the guide's route. Kernel 2 suits partner play. Kernel 3 is for the quickest children, late in the packet. Every number above was checked by enumeration: the leftovers by repeated take-away for squares up to side 20, the sets of leftovers that occur for groups of 2 to 8 through side 2000, the repeat for groups up to 12, the pairing for p = 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13, and the ring counts through side 99.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 (an adult reads every problem aloud; numbers up to about 10 or 12; children count along a side, at most 6, count out each group, at most 8, and say the leftover; they never need a square's total, such as 25 or 36, and the adult can write what the children find) builds filled squares of sides 1 to 5 or 6. The children take away groups of 2, 3, 4 and 6, decide which leftovers a square can and cannot make, and test whether a rectangle can do what a square cannot. They play the secret square, naming every possible square from a fixed set of side cards. The quickest build odd squares ring by ring and take away groups of 8. The grades 2–3 and 4–5 tables work at the same time from the existing Grades 3–5 packet, W97-35-v2, so the K–1 pages must use the same kit and theme and share the launch, without copying the older bands' problems. Those problems are: its worked example (bundles of 4, inputs 0 to 3, outputs 0, 1, 0, 1); Problem 1 (bundles of 5, inputs 0 to 4: find the inputs that share an output and the outputs that cannot occur); Problem 2 (the secret-input game with bundles of 7); and Problems 3–6 (bundles of 7, 8 and 9; output 1 with bundles of 15; bigger inputs with bundles of 7; a rule for odd primes). Groups of 5 and 7 may appear on the K–1 pages only inside a different question.

Materials

The base kit (guide p. 2), with one kit for each of the two K–1 pairs: 64 counters or square tiles, a whiteboard and pen, scrap paper, and about 16 reusable input cards marked by hand. The guide allows about 10 minutes for the whole room to count kits and mark cards. Use square tiles at the K–1 table if the room has them (1-inch colour tiles, about 25 mm, in several colours). Tiles keep a square filled and flat, make 2-by-2 blocks and rings visible, and do not roll. If the table uses round counters, they should all be one size, and a printed grid helps K–1 hands keep the square square.

The shared launch (guide p. 2) shows the whole room a 3-by-3 square of nine counters, two groups of 4 and one counter left, then the empty square, whose leftover is 0. The K–1 pages start from that action and should not set the 3-by-3-in-groups-of-4 case as a task. A kindergartner asked for a square may build a hollow outline, so the pages must show a filled square. "Side" means the counters along one edge, and a 1-by-1 square is one counter.

A board on which real tiles or counters are placed must be printed at actual size, with cells a little wider than the pieces. Measure the pieces before setting the size: 1-inch tiles need cells of about 26 mm. At that size a 6-by-6 board (156 mm) fits across a US Letter page inside 0.7-inch margins (about 180 mm), but a 7-by-7 board (182 mm) does not. One 6-by-6 board takes about half a page. A board with nested outlines from one corner, for sides 1 to 6, lets a child fill an n-by-n square without counting the total. Compact pictures are enough wherever the child builds on the table instead of on the page.

Groups are taken away as free handfuls of any m pieces, into a cup or a pile beside the board, and counting out a group needs counting only to m. A printed group card with m spaces, filled and then swept off, would enforce the group size with the material; that is the writer's choice. Groups must not be rigid straight pieces: covering a square with 1-by-3 strips is a different puzzle, and a child can get stuck with more left over than the true leftover. The square has to be rebuilt, or its pieces gathered back, for each new group size. Timing: a 5-by-5 square in groups of 3 means 8 take-aways, and 6-by-6 means 12, which is likely to be slow for a kindergartner. Groups of 4, 6 or 8 from a 6-by-6 square mean 9, 6 or 4.

Squares up to 6-by-6 (36 pieces) fit in one pair's kit. A 7-by-7 square (49) also fits, but it is a long build at this table, and 8-by-8 (64) uses the whole kit. Neither is needed: kernel 3 needs only 1, 3 and 5. For the secret square, a folder stood up as a screen hides the build (the guide screens it with a sheet). Every secret-square question must say which sides are allowed, preferably by laying out exactly those side cards, because the answers change when a card is added. The base kit's input cards are numerals marked by hand. K–1 side cards should show the number together with a small dot picture of its square, for sides 1 to 6, plus 0 if the pages use the empty square. Printing one sheet of picture cards to cut out is a small addition (about five minutes of cutting); with a cup and a folder for each pair, it is the only addition these pages should need. Two colours make the rings in kernel 3 easy to see, one colour per ring. With one-colour pieces, rings can be built and taken away one at a time. The older tables' printed square facts and larger bundle sizes stay off the K–1 table.

This kit has not been tried with K–1 children. The base week is unpiloted, and its kit, procedures and timing have not been rehearsed.
