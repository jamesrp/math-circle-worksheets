Week 106: Coloring maps

Page header: "Week 106 / Coloring maps / K–1" and "Week 106 / Coloring maps / Grades 2–5". Packet ids: W106-k-1-v1 and W106-grades-2-5-v1; the footer reads "Bellingham Math Circle / Week 106 / <id>". Use American spelling (color, coloring) on every page.

The organizer worries that map coloring is only "color some maps" and "find a map that needs four", with little variety. The kernels below are deliberately different kinds of experience: a surprise that always works, a rule about odd and even, a drawing challenge that keeps failing, a two-player game with a sneaky strategy, and a strange world where the answer changes. Let the packets feel varied in what the children do, not only in which map they color.

The basic rule everywhere: a map is split into countries; two countries that share a border (a stretch of line, not just a single corner point) must get different colors. Countries that meet only at a corner may share a color.

Mathematical kernels

1. Scribble maps need only two colors. Draw any number of lines that run all the way from one edge of the page to another (straight or wiggly, crossing each other as much as you like), or any number of closed loops (circles, or one long scribble that ends where it started and crosses itself many times), or a mix. The countries can always be colored with just two colors. Reason: pick a country, give it color A, and color every other country by whether you cross an even or odd number of lines on a path to it. Crossing any one border changes that number by exactly one, so neighbors always differ, and the answer does not depend on the path, because a line or closed loop is crossed an even number of times by any path that leaves a country and comes back. It breaks as soon as a line stops partway, at another line or in the middle of the page. A "T" (one line from the left edge to the right edge, and a second line from the bottom edge that stops when it reaches the first) makes 3 countries that all touch each other, so it needs 3 colors; so does a "Y" of three lines from one point in the middle out to three edges. (A loose line that ends inside a country and separates nothing changes nothing.) Smallest instances: one line makes 2 countries; two crossing lines make 4; three lines that each cross the other two at different points make 7, four make 11 (1 + n + n(n−1)/2); three overlapping circles in the Venn picture make 8 regions counting the outside.

2. Odd rings need an extra color. A ring of countries around a lake (each touching the one before and the one after, the last touching the first) needs 2 colors if the number of countries is even and 3 if it is odd: going around, the colors would have to alternate, and an odd ring cannot alternate all the way round. A middle country completely surrounded by a ring touches every country of the ring, so it needs a color of its own: a middle with a ring of 4 needs 3 colors, a middle with a ring of 5 needs 4, a ring of 6 needs 3, a ring of 7 needs 4. Surprise for older children: the middle-with-a-ring-of-5 map needs 4 colors even though it has no 4 countries that all touch each other (any 4 of its countries include two ring countries that are not next to each other). Needing many colors is not the same as having many countries that all touch.

3. All-touching countries, and the four color theorem. You can draw 4 countries that each share a border with the other three (for example a middle country with three countries around it, the outer three each touching the other two; or a pizza cut into three slices with a round country in the center). Nobody can draw 5 countries that each share a border with all of the other four on a flat page: this is impossible (it is the fact that the complete graph on five points cannot be drawn without crossings, provable from Euler's formula V − E + F = 2). The stronger fact is the four color theorem: every map on a flat page can be colored with four colors. It was proved by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken in 1976 using a computer to check about 1,500 cases, and re-proved (also with a computer) by Robertson, Sanders, Seymour and Thomas in 1997. It is a theorem children can try to break: "draw a map that needs five colors" is a challenge that every child will fail, and adults know why. Real example: the map of the United States needs four colors because Nevada is surrounded by a ring of 5 states (California, Oregon, Idaho, Utah, Arizona).

4. The painter and spoiler game (Brams's coloring game, popularized by Martin Gardner in 1981). Two players share a set of colors. They take turns choosing any uncolored country and coloring it with any color that does not clash with an already colored neighbor. The Painter wins if the whole map gets colored. The Spoiler wins if some uncolored country gets stuck: its neighbors already use every color, so it can never be colored. The surprise is that a map that can be colored with few colors may still be spoiled. Checked by computer (every line of play) on small maps, with the Painter moving first unless said otherwise:
   - a strip of 3 squares in a row, 2 colors: Painter always wins; a strip of 4, 2 colors: the Spoiler wins if the Painter moves first, the Painter wins if the Spoiler moves first; a strip of 5, 2 colors: the Spoiler wins whoever starts.
   - a ring of 4 countries around a lake, 2 colors: the Spoiler wins if the Painter moves first, and loses if the Spoiler moves first. Spoiler's trick: color the country opposite the Painter's country with the other color; both countries between them are now stuck.
   - a ring of any size with 3 colors: the Painter always wins.
   - a 3-by-3 grid of square countries (like a tic-tac-toe board; squares touching only at corners are not neighbors). It needs only 2 colors (a checkerboard). With 3 colors the Spoiler wins whoever starts; with 4 colors the Painter wins whoever starts. The Spoiler's idea is to surround one uncolored square with three different colors.
   - a middle country with a ring of 4 around it, 3 colors: the Painter wins if the Painter moves first, the Spoiler wins if the Spoiler moves first; with a ring of 5 and 3 colors the Spoiler wins either way (the map cannot even be colored with 3), and with 4 colors the Painter wins on rings of 3 to 7.
   Verify any other map you use with code before printing a claim about it.

5. Donut world. On a board whose edges wrap around like an old video game (leave the right edge and come back at the same height on the left; leave the top and come back at the same place on the bottom) the surface is a torus, the surface of a donut. There, 7 countries can each touch all 6 others, so some donut maps need 7 colors, and 7 is always enough (Percy Heawood, 1890). A tube (wrapping left to right only) gives nothing new: 4 colors still always suffice, because a tube can be flattened onto a page. One explicit seven-country donut map: tile the plane with hexagons in rows, number the hexagon in column a and row b (each row shifted half a hexagon to the right of the row below) with a + 3b mod 7 (or any labeling where each hexagon's six neighbors carry the six other numbers); the pattern repeats, and one repeating block of 7 hexagons, drawn on the wrap-around board, is a map of 7 countries on the donut each touching all the others. The writer must check by code that, on the printed board with its wrap rule, every pair of the 7 countries shares a border (21 pairs) and that every piece of a country cut by the board's edge is labeled so a child can match its pieces. Physical bridge: rolling the printed board into a tube shows the left-right wrap; the top-bottom wrap needs imagination.

Sources: Robertson, Sanders, Seymour and Thomas, "The Four-Colour Theorem" (preprint, 1997), for the theorem and its history; John Steele, "Colouring the Torus", Parabola 35(2), 1999, for Heawood's seven-color torus map; Julia Robinson Mathematics Festival, "Map Coloring Teaching Guide" (odd rings and wheels as reasons for more colors, use with children); Shippensburg University math circle handout "Map Coloring: From Checker Boards to Möbius Strips" (maps of whole lines need two colors); Steven Brams's coloring game as described by Martin Gardner (Scientific American, April 1981) and the later literature on the game chromatic number (Kierstead and Trotter; Zhu); any graph theory text for K5 and Euler's formula (for example Wilson, Introduction to Graph Theory).

Suggested emphasis by level:
- K–1: color scribble maps and big loop maps with two pencils and find that it always works, including a scribble they draw themselves; find the fewest pencils for flower maps (a middle with a ring of 4 petals and with 5 petals) and notice the difference; play the painter and spoiler game with a partner on a small map with 2 colors. Large countries a five-year-old can color quickly; very little reading; no writing beyond a number of pencils or a checkmark.
- Grades 2–5: start with scribble maps and two colors (a second grader can begin at once), then find out when two colors stop working and say why; odd and even rings with and without a middle; draw 4 countries that all touch and try for 5; the painter and spoiler game on the tic-tac-toe map with 3 colors and then 4, looking for the Spoiler's trick; "draw a map that needs five colors" as an open challenge; donut world with seven countries for the readiest fourth and fifth graders, late in the packet.

Materials

Colored pencils: each pair has a set of at least 7 different colors (12-color sets are common), and K–1 pairs also get two thick pencils of very different colors. Crayons and rulers are not available; lines are drawn freehand, so pages must not depend on straight lines or precise drawing, and countries children color must be large enough to fill quickly (K–1 countries much larger). Pages print in black and white single-sided on US Letter at 100%; nothing on a page may rely on color printing, so any pre-colored country in a printed puzzle must be shown by a printed letter or pattern (for example R, B, Y written in the country) rather than by ink color. Game boards should be printed several to a page or repeated, because a colored-in game cannot be erased and pairs will want to play more than once. Small whiteboards with markers in 3 or 4 colors are on each table for drawing and redrawing maps. About 210 one-color counters exist but are not needed. Children work in pairs; in a game, the two partners are the Painter and the Spoiler and swap roles each game.
