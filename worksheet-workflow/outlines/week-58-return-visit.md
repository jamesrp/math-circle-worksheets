Week 58 return visit: Exact halves and fair thirds

Mathematical kernels

1. Exact halves for everyone: two tastes never need more than two cuts. A row of goods is cut into pieces, each piece goes into one of two trays, and each person values a tray with their own card, adding card values; on a strip, value follows length within a colour. A division is exact for everyone when each person finds the two trays worth exactly the same. Then it does not matter who gets which tray, and no chooser is needed. Claim: for two people, two cuts are always enough, although one cut may not be. Proof by a sliding window: take the piece from the left end that A values at exactly half, and slide it right, keeping A's value at half, until it reaches the right end. It ends as exactly what lay outside it at the start, so B's value of it moves from some v to B's total minus v. One of these is at most half and the other at least half, so in between B sees exactly half too, and that window is a middle piece cut out with two cuts. On a train of whole cards with an even number of each of two colours, a window covering half the cards changes its red count by at most one per step and ends at (all reds minus its starting count), so somewhere it holds half the reds, and so half the blues. Checked instances: on the 240 mm strip (red 0–120 mm, blue 120–240 mm) with the two-person cards A (whole red panel 3, whole blue panel 1) and B (red 1, blue 3), A's only exact one-cut point is 80 mm and B's is 160 mm, so no single cut is exact for both. Cuts at 60 and 180 mm make a middle piece (60 mm of red, 60 mm of blue) and two end pieces, each worth exactly 2 to both people, and this is the only two-cut answer. A's half-window slides from 0–80 mm to 80–240 mm while B's value of it rises from 2/3 to 10/3, passing 2 at 60–180 mm. Any pieces worth exactly half to both hold exactly half the red and half the blue (3r + s = 2 and r + 3s = 2 force r = s = 1/2), so for these cards "exact for both" means "each tray has the same red and the same blue", which K–1 children can see without numbers. Trains (cards stay whole, a cut is a gap between neighbours, and each tray gets half the reds and half the blues): with two reds and two blues, RRBB and BBRR need two cuts and the other four orders need one. With four of each, 36 of the 70 orders need one cut (exactly when the first four cards hold two reds) and 34 need two. With six reds and four blues, 100 of the 210 orders need one cut and 110 need two. An exhaustive check found no two-colour train with even counts, up to 14 cards, that needs three cuts, and an odd number of reds, such as R1, R2, R3, can never be shared equally. More tastes need more cuts. Lay the panels X, Y, Z (50 mm each) in a row and use the three-person cards, called A3, B3, C3 here, which value (X, Y, Z) at (4, 8, 0), (0, 4, 8) and (8, 0, 4); each totals 12. A tray worth exactly 6 to all three must hold exactly half of each panel (the 3 × 3 system has determinant 576), so each panel needs a cut inside it and three cuts are necessary. Cuts at 25, 75 and 125 mm, with alternate pieces in each tray, give 6 and 6 to all three, and a 0.5 mm scan finds no one-cut or two-cut answer. A three-colour train in blocks, RRBBGG, likewise needs three cuts. That k tastes or k colours never need more than k cuts for two trays is the Hobby–Rice and necklace-splitting theorem, proved with the Borsuk–Ulam theorem; it is adult background from the literature and was not checked here. In the first visit, the four one-red-one-blue divisions of R1, R2, B1, B2 tied at 4 and 4 for both people. New here are pieces of a fixed row, the fewest cuts, and why two cuts always suffice.

2. A third for each of three, whatever their tastes. Lay X, Y, Z in a row in any order; in this visit the panels may be cut, with value following length within a panel. Three people hold A3, B3 and C3. Each marks where the piece from the left end first reaches a third of their own total (4 of 12), and the leftmost marker takes that piece. The other two mark again from the new left end, still aiming at 4 by their own card; the leftmost takes that piece, and the last person takes the rest. Claim: for any additive tastes, everyone gets at least a third of their own total, and the same procedure with 1/n works for n people. This is Steinhaus's problem, solved by Banach and Knaster's last diminisher and by Dubins and Spanier's moving knife. Proof: each taker gets exactly a third by their own card. Whenever someone else took a piece, my mark lay at or beyond its end, so that piece was worth at most a third to me, and the last person keeps at least 12 − 4 − 4 = 4. The procedure does not promise freedom from envy, or a good division. Checked instances: in the order X, Y, Z the first marks are C3 at 25 mm (half of X), A3 at 50 mm and B3 at 100 mm, so C3 takes 0–25 mm. Then A3 marks 62.5 mm and B3 100 mm, so A3 takes 25–62.5 mm and B3 gets 62.5–150 mm. C3 and A3 have 4 each and B3 has 11, but A3 values B3's piece at 6 and envies it. Orders XZY, YXZ and ZYX cut only at the half-panel points 25 and 75 mm (in XZY, C3 takes 0–25 mm, B3 takes 25–75 mm and A3 the rest, worth 8). Orders XYZ, YZX and ZXY need one quarter-panel cut, at 62.5 mm. In all six orders the leftmost mark is never tied, the two takers get exactly 4, the last person gets 8 or 11, and at least one person envies another. Yet the whole panels A3:Y, B3:Z, C3:X give everyone 8 with no envy, so the guarantee is a floor, not the best division. In about 4,000 random three- and four-person trials, the procedure always met 1/n and nearly always left envy. Whole cards in a row behave like a strip, with value spread evenly across each card. Each mark cuts at most one card, so at most n − 1 cards are split; for two people, cut-and-choose on a row is envy-free and splits at most one card. If both people use the two-person A card on the row R1 B1 R3 B2 R2 (total 11), the exact half falls through the middle of R3, so the paper R3 is the card split, 5.5 each. In every order of three reds and two blues, this cut falls strictly inside a red card. The bound can be needed: three people who value every red at 1 (U/V cards) and share two reds need 2/3 each. Giving either red whole to one person leaves one red for two people who need 4/3, so both reds must be cut; the marks fall 2/3 of the way into the first red and 1/3 of the way into the second. The literature also gives a three-person division with no envy (Selfridge–Conway; with connected pieces, Stromquist). It is adult background only, and the first visit's guide deferred it to a separate plan.

Suggested emphasis by level:
K–1: kernel 1 with whole cards only. Make a train of two red and two blue goods cards, pull it apart as few times as possible, and share the pieces between two trays so that both trays hold the same reds and the same blues; then build longer trains from two kits. This needs matching and counting to 6, with no dot sums, and an adult reads. An optional cutting entry: fold the strip so that each tray gets half the red and half the blue, and an adult cuts where the child marks. Pages for K–1 should not rely on the words envy-free or proportional.
Grades 2–3: kernel 1 on longer trains (look for a train that needs three cuts, and decide whether one exists) and on the strip with the two-person A and B cards (no single cut is exact for both; two cuts can be). This uses dot sums to 4, halves of a panel and millimetre measuring. A ready group plays kernel 2's marking game as three recipients plus a referee, starting with the half-panel orders XZY, YXZ and ZYX (half of 8 is 4).
Grades 4–5: kernel 1's sliding-window explanation for any train and any two tastes, and why three tastes on X, Y, Z need three cuts. Then kernel 2 in all six panel orders: why nobody can get less than a third, and how its result compares with the whole-panel division worth 8 to everyone. When ready: whole cards in a row, with at most n − 1 split cards, and the case of two reds for three people. This uses quarters of a panel (12.5 mm, worth 2 of 8) and comparisons of three trays.

Materials

The first visit's kit, reused as it is: week-58-materials.pdf (W58-materials-v3), printed single-sided on US Letter at 100%, with pages 2–8 in landscape. There are five pair kits (two at K–1, two at 2–3, one at 4–5) and one three-person kit.
- p. 1: sturdy 25 mm goods cards R1, R2, R3 (a red circle icon) and B1, B2 (a blue square icon), mounted on stiff card, and one unmounted paper R3. The paper R3 is the only goods card that may be cut; the icon only names the card, and a cut card's value follows the area of the whole square.
- p. 2: two-person preference cards A (each red card 3, each blue card 1) and B (red 1, blue 3), 100 × 150 mm, with numerals and dots. On strips, these numbers are the value of a whole 120 mm panel.
- p. 3: U and V cards. Every red card is worth 1; the cards give no value for blue.
- pp. 4–5: three-person cards printed A, B and C, valuing the whole panels X, Y, Z at (4, 8, 0), (0, 4, 8) and (8, 0, 4), 100 × 150 mm, and grey panels X, Y, Z, 50 × 40 mm (one set per copy of p. 5).
- pp. 6–8: strips 240 × 40 mm, red from 0 to 120 mm on the left and blue from 120 to 240 mm on the right, three per sheet. The full stock is nine strips per pair.
- Two 150–180 mm trays per pair (three for a three-person trial), a ruler per pair, pencils, blank paper and three scissors held by adults.

Cheap additions, only for the work that needs them: one extra copy of p. 5 for each three-person trial on a cuttable X, Y, Z row (about four per table that runs kernel 2), and one more copy of pp. 4–5 if two tables run three-person trials at once. For trains longer than four cards, combine the two pair kits at a table (six sturdy reds and four blues), or print one extra unmounted copy of p. 1 per pair. Train cards are never cut. Splitting cards in a row (kernel 2's card version) needs cuttable copies, such as the paper R3s or an extra unmounted p. 1.

Constraints the pages must respect:
- The 240 mm strip fits only on a landscape sheet. Draw strips schematically on task pages, and cut the separate full-size strips parallel to the colour join, measuring from the red end. Mark in pencil first and cut only to hand out pieces; each successful division uses up one strip.
- Halving a panel means 60 mm on the red and blue strip and 25 mm on an X, Y or Z panel. A quarter of an X, Y or Z panel is 12.5 mm.
- The first visit treated X, Y and Z only as whole panels. Cutting them, with value following length within a panel, is a new rule that needs its own small worked example before first use, for instance a 50 mm panel worth 8 becoming two 25 mm halves worth 4 each.
- In a train, the cards stay whole. A cut is a gap between neighbouring cards, the pieces keep their order, and each piece goes whole into one tray. Keep this rule separate from kernel 2's card version, where a card may be split. A row of more than seven 25 mm cards does not fit across a portrait page at actual size, so build trains on the table and record them compactly.
- Three-person trials need three recipients and three trays. At the four-child tables the fourth child referees; at 4–5 all three children are recipients.
- The three-person cards use the letters A and B for different people than the two-person cards; the first visit's review suggested relettering them P, Q, R, and that change was not made. Pages must make clear which card is meant.
- Equal counts or equal lengths are not equal value, and pages never compare one person's number with another person's.

What the children already did with this kit, so the pages do not repeat it:
- With cards A and B, they found envy-free divisions of R1, R2, R3, B1 and every envy-free division of R1, R2, B1, B2. In the second set, the four one-red-one-blue divisions tie at 4 and 4.
- They did cut-and-choose on the strip, with A cutting at 80 mm and B at 160 mm, and explained why it always gives both people at least half with no envy.
- They tried a cut at the colour join with both people using A's card.
- They found that U and V cannot share three whole reds so that neither would rather have the other's cards, until the paper R3 is halved.
- They repaired the X, Y, Z start A:X, B:Y, C:Z, where everyone gets 4 and everyone envies, to A:Y, B:Z, C:X.
- They saw why envy-free implies proportional, and that the two conditions are the same for two people.

The first visit's guide also suggested a return question: splitting one card of any odd number of identical cards. It is not a kernel here.
