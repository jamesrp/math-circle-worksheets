Week 95: Count the unseen

Mathematical kernels

1. A closed bag contains N objects, all physically alike except for visible identity or marking. Capture M different objects, mark them, return ALL to the bag and mix. Take a second catch of n objects without replacement within that catch. Its R previously marked objects are overlaps, not new individuals. Exactly M+n-R different objects have been seen. This gives a sharp lower bound on N, with no finite upper bound from the catch record alone: any additional unseen objects are compatible. More overlap can indicate a smaller population under a sampling model, but does not prove its exact size.

2. If every n-subset in the second catch is equally likely, each of the M marked objects has chance n/N to appear. Hence average overlap over the sampling distribution is E(R)=Mn/N. Matching an observed overlap to that average suggests N approximately Mn/R when R>0. This is an estimate, not an identity, guarantee, or unbiased estimator. R=0 makes this ratio undefined; it does not mean an infinite population. Small real samples fluctuate. The exact hypergeometric law C(M,r)C(N-M,n-r)/C(N,n) is facilitator checking mathematics, not an intended student enumeration exercise. Do not give a batch of plug-in ratio calculations.

3. With a fixed total number of handled objects M+n=s, expected overlap is maximized by making M and n as equal as possible. The reason is the product M(s-M), proved by comparing rectangles or moving one unit from the longer side to the shorter. This is an inference-driven experimental-design question with a substantive exact mathematical destination. It maximizes expected overlap, not every possible definition of estimator quality. Keep sample sizes feasible, e.g. N>=s for a simple comparison. Optional extensions distinguish a certain lower bound from a model-based estimate and test whether an inaccessible subgroup or lost marks defeats the model. Atlas AP-04 already addresses sampling-frame bias: the new primary mathematics is two-sample identity overlap, an estimator for unseen population size, and allocation of sampling effort.

Suggested emphasis by level

K–1: No standalone packet is required. The common catch-and-return demonstration can be watched or played, but this family honestly begins at Grades2–3. Do not force a Kindergarten simulation without its inferential question.
Grades 2–3: Make and compare possible populations consistent with actual catch records; count repeated identities once; compare overlap for fixed catch sizes using a few pooled trials. Counting, addition/subtraction within20; oral reading. The distinction between a guess and something forced is meaningful here.
Grades 4–5: Develop and challenge population estimates; choose a fixed-budget sampling design and explain why it maximizes expected overlap; distinguish model failure from random variability. Multiplication, simple fractions/division and reasoning about multiple compatible explanations are actual gates. Preserve richer work for return visits instead of forcing every result into one hour.

Materials

Per pair: one opaque bag, at most24 identical-size cards or flat counters, a divider/screen, paper/whiteboard, pencil. Visible marks must not be detectable by touch: identical paper cards can have blank versus dot faces, turned to mark after a first catch; cards may also carry small identities when the task needs matching. No folded corners, removable clips or different shapes in a blind draw. Supply necessary printable cards at about20mm square, or explicitly permit all-identical commercial counters with marker dots. Uniformity is the mathematical sampling assumption; a classroom shake is an approximation, not a guarantee. The keeper knows the population and preserves it unchanged during a round. Reveal at the end so guesses can be checked. Hold all second-catch objects OUT until recording overlap; restore everything and remove/redo marks before a new round that changes the first catch. No real animals, outdoor collection, or specialized equipment.

A worked visual before first use must show a small first catch, those SAME objects returned and mixed, then a second catch with some repeated identities; label first catch, return, second catch, and repeated objects consistently. Choose a non-task instance. One compact (M,n,R) record or arrangement is enough; no simultaneous tallies. A few pooled real trials can motivate uncertainty, but the mathematics must not depend on getting a convenient random result. Children retain meaningful choices of possible worlds, guesses or experimental plans; adults may read/record but should not operate the investigation for them.
