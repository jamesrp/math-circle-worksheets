Week 1 encore: Pattern blocks II

This is the second pattern-block session. In the first, children tiled boards with blue rhombi (each blue rhombus covers one up-pointing and one down-pointing small triangle, so a board with more up-triangles than down-triangles cannot be tiled), packed triangles with blues, and the oldest children found the six blue-rhombus tilings of a small hexagon, flips between them, and left/right ribbons. They enjoyed it and did not finish. This session continues with new questions and new pieces; it should not repeat those boards.

Mathematical kernels

1. A placement game. Two players take turns placing one small blue rhombus on a printed triangle-grid board, covering two whole small triangles, inside the board, without overlapping. A player who cannot place a rhombus loses. On a board that looks the same after a half turn about its centre there is a copying strategy. If the centre is a grid point, no placement is its own half-turn image, so the second player answers every move with its half-turn image and always has a move: the second player wins. If the centre is the midpoint of a grid edge, exactly one placement covers the centre; the first player takes it, then copies, and wins. A hexagon with sides a, b, c, a, b, c (in small edges) has a grid-point centre exactly when a, b, c are all even or all odd; an a-by-b parallelogram does exactly when a and b are both even. Checked by computer: the second player wins on the hexagons with sides 1,1,1 and 2,2,2 and on the 2-by-2 and 4-by-2 parallelograms; the first player wins on the hexagons 1,1,2 and 1,2,2 and on the 3-by-2 and 3-by-3 parallelograms. Triangles have no half-turn symmetry: the first player wins on the triangle with 3 small edges on a side (every first move wins), and the second player wins on the triangle with 4 small edges on a side, with no simple copying explanation that we know.

2. Up and down triangles again, with red trapezoids. A red trapezoid covers three small triangles: two up and one down, or one up and two down. (Add one green triangle to a trapezoid to complete a triangle with 2 small edges on a side; that big triangle points up exactly when the trapezoid covers two up-triangles.) If a tiling uses x trapezoids of the first kind and y of the second, then x − y = (up-triangles) − (down-triangles) and x + y = (number of small triangles) ÷ 3, so x and y are the same in every tiling of a board. Checked: the triangle with 3 small edges per side has 2 trapezoid tilings, each with three "two-up" trapezoids; the triangle with 6 small edges per side has 220 trapezoid tilings, every one with 9 "two-up" and 3 "two-down"; the hexagon with 2 small edges per side has 9 trapezoid tilings, each with 4 and 4. Separately, a triangle with n small edges per side holds n × n green triangles (1, 4, 9, 16, 25), of which 1 + 2 + … + n point up and 1 + 2 + … + (n − 1) point down.

3. Blue rhombus tilings are pictures of stacked cubes, and other pieces behave differently. Shade the rhombi of a hexagon tiling by direction (three directions: light, medium, dark) and the picture becomes a pile of cubes in a corner. A flip (three rhombi forming a small hexagon, turned to the hexagon's other filling) adds or removes one cube. So every flip changes the number of cubes by one, a route of flips that returns to its start has even length, and the fewest flips from the emptiest picture to the fullest is the number of cubes in the full box. The hexagon with sides a, b, c, a, b, c has these blue tilings: 1,2,2: 6 tilings, 4 flips from emptiest to fullest; 2,2,2: 20 tilings, 8 flips; 1,3,3: 20 tilings, 9 flips. In every tiling the rhombi in the three directions number ab, bc and ca (for 2,2,2: four of each). Red trapezoids on the hexagon with 2 small edges per side behave differently. Its 9 trapezoid tilings are a small hexagon in the middle filled by 2 trapezoids (3 ways) inside a ring of 6 trapezoids (3 ways). Turning the middle two trapezoids gives 3 tilings, each one move from the other two, so a route can return to its start in 3 moves, an odd number. To change the ring you must rebuild all six outer trapezoids at once: moves that rebuild five or fewer trapezoids never connect the three ring families.

4. Fewest pieces and bigger copies. Four small greens make the 2× green triangle, four blues make the 2× blue rhombus (one way), and four reds make the 2× red trapezoid (exactly one way). Small yellow hexagons alone cannot fill the 2× hexagon. Using small hexagons, trapezoids, blue rhombi and green triangles, the fewest pieces that fill the 2× hexagon is 6 (3 hexagons and 3 blues; at most 3 small hexagons fit). The fewest for the 3× hexagon is 12 (6 hexagons and 6 trapezoids), although 7 small hexagons fit; every filling that uses 7 hexagons needs at least 13 pieces, so putting in as many hexagons as possible is not best. Fewest pieces for triangles with 4, 5, 6 small edges per side: 5, 7, 9.

Sources: Berlekamp, Conway and Guy, Winning Ways, on symmetry strategies in placement games such as Cram; Thurston, "Conway's tiling groups" (1990); David and Tomei, "The problem of the calissons" (American Mathematical Monthly, 1989), the cube picture of rhombus tilings; MIT 18.312 lectures on rhombus tilings and plane partitions.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 makes bigger copies of single blocks on the Upscale pieces, fills picture outlines with the fewest and with the most pieces, and plays the placement game on small boards; grades 2–3 play the placement game and look for a way to always win, find fewest-piece fillings, and compare two-up and two-down trapezoids across different tilings (kernels 1, 2, 4); grades 4–5 play and explain the placement game, turn rhombus tilings into cube pictures and use cubes to explain flip routes, and compare with trapezoid tilings (kernels 1 and 3), with fewest-piece puzzles available.

Materials

21st Century Pattern Blocks, plenty for eleven children: green triangle (each edge 1 inch), blue rhombus (two triangles), red trapezoid (three triangles), yellow hexagon (six triangles), purple chevron (equal to two blue rhombi), plus pink right triangles, teal kites, and gray darts. About 40 blue rhombi and 30 red trapezoids are available for the 4–5 table. There are no orange squares or tan rhombi. Upscale pattern blocks at 2× and 3× size (triangle, rhombus, trapezoid, hexagon) are on the tables. They can serve as outlines to cover, but they would trivialize any "fewest pieces" task, so such tasks must say to use the small pieces. Snap cubes (about 30 for the 4–5 table) and the inside corner of a small box to stack them against. Coloured pencils in at least three shades, paper, pencils, small whiteboards. Children play games in pairs; at the 4–5 table the third child referees or plays the adult.

Any board on which children place physical blocks must be printed at actual size: the small triangle's edge is 1 inch (2.54 cm). A US Letter page has about 7.5 by 10 inches of usable space, so a hexagon with 3-inch sides (6 inches across) or a triangle with 6-inch sides fits only one to a page. Game boards need the triangle grid drawn on them. Drawings used only for recording can be smaller than actual size.
