Mathematical kernels

1. Shuffle machines and their loops. A shuffle machine is a mat with a row of slots on top, the same number of slots directly below, and one arrow from each top slot to a bottom slot, with every bottom slot receiving exactly one arrow. One turn: put a different block in each top slot, move every block down its own arrow, then slide the whole bottom row straight up into the top row. Repeat. Each slot has exactly one arrow in and one arrow out, so following the arrows from any slot comes back to that slot: the slots split into separate loops (a loop of 1 is a block that never moves). A block in a loop of length k is home after k turns, and again every k turns. The whole row is back in its starting order for the first time after the least common multiple of the loop lengths: loops of 2 and 3 give 6 turns. Possible first return times: with 3 slots 1, 2, 3; with 4 slots 1, 2, 3, 4; with 5 slots 1 through 6 (6 from loops of 2 and 3); with 6 slots still only 1 through 6; with 7 slots 1 through 7, 10 and 12; with 8 slots the largest is 15 (loops of 3 and 5). The largest return time for 1 through 10 slots is 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 6, 12, 15, 20, 30 (Landau's function), so adding a slot does not always make a slower machine.

2. Undoing and combining. Every machine can be undone: reverse every arrow. Running machine A and then machine B is a new machine. A then B can differ from B then A (for example, two swaps that share a slot), and they agree when the machines move separate slots. The machine that undoes "A then B" is "undo B, then undo A".

3. Card shuffles are machines. A perfect out-shuffle of an even number of cards (split the deck into equal halves and interleave them exactly, keeping the top card on top) is a shuffle machine. It brings the deck back after 2 shuffles for 4 cards, 4 for 6 cards, 3 for 8, 6 for 10, 10 for 12, and 8 for a full deck of 52.

Sources: Judson, Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications, Chapter 5 (permutations, cycle decomposition, order as a least common multiple); Diaconis, Graham and Kantor, "The mathematics of perfect shuffles" (1983); Landau's function g(n).

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 runs machines with 3 and 4 slots using blocks, counts turns until the blocks are home, and draws arrows to make a machine for a partner; grades 2–3 run 4- and 5-slot machines, find the loops, predict return times, build machines with chosen return times, and build the machine that undoes another (kernels 1 and 2); grades 4–5 find every possible return time for 5 slots and the slowest machines for 6 and 7 slots, combine machines in both orders, and test real card shuffles (kernels 1 to 3).

Materials

Children have trouble keeping a rule in their heads after school, so the mat makes the rule physical. Printed machine mats: a top row and a bottom row of square slots about 1.25 inches on a side, each big enough for one pattern block, with the bottom row directly below the top row and a clear printed arrow from each top slot to a bottom slot. A turn is always: move every block down its own arrow first, then slide the bottom row straight up. A turn never changes a block; the blocks only change places. Pattern blocks as the moving objects, a different colour or shape in each slot so the order is easy to see (green triangle, blue rhombus, red trapezoid, yellow hexagon, purple chevron, pink triangle, teal kite, gray dart; 21st Century Pattern Blocks, plenty). Blank mats with empty slots, for children to draw their own arrows. Children record with tally marks or by drawing or colouring the row; K–1 children do not read letters, and no one needs letter tables. Two decks of playing cards for the 4–5 table. Paper, pencils, coloured pencils, small whiteboards. For the whole-group demonstration, five chairs and masking tape: a big arrow map is taped on the floor, five children sit in the chairs, and on "switch" everyone walks along their own arrow.
