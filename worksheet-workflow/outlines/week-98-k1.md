Week 98 K–1 pages: Growing from a few seeds

Page header: "Week 98 / Growing from a few seeds / K–1". Packet id W98-K-v1; the footer reads "Bellingham Math Circle / Week 98 / W98-K-v1".

Week 98 has a grades 2–3 packet (W98-23-v3), a grades 4–5 packet (W98-45-v3) and an adult guide (W98-FAC-v4). It has no K–1 pages. The guide (p. 2) gives an untried adult-led K–1 route on the grades 2–3 pages. The parent reads the rule as one sentence: "A square gets a counter when two squares next to it, not corner to corner, have counters." The children place three seeds on a 3-by-3 board and grow them while a partner points to the two neighbours. They try to fill a 4-by-4 board with fewer seeds, and the parent draws each start that fills. Each child then grows one board of a removal test. The children place every seed and every counter themselves; the adult reads and draws records. These pages are meant to give K–1 its own problems.

The rule, as the older pages and the launch state it. Seeds are the squares filled at the start, and they get counters. Add a counter to an empty square when at least two squares sharing its sides are filled. Diagonal touches do not count, and there are no squares outside the board. Counters stay put, and a partner points to two filled neighbours before each addition. Growth stops when no more can be added, and then the board is either filled or stuck. Seeds and added squares use counters of two colours, so taking off the second colour resets the start.

Mathematical kernels

1. Fill or stuck, and the fewest seeds. One seed never grows. An n-by-n board needs exactly n seeds: two for 2-by-2, three for 3-by-3, four for 4-by-4 and five for 5-by-5. A slanting line of seeds from corner to corner always fills, because each square beside the line touches two seeds, each square beside those touches two filled squares, and so on out to the far corners. Why fewer never fill: think of a fence along every side of a filled square that faces an empty square or the edge of the board. A seed alone has 4 fence sides. When a square is added beside two filled squares, the two sides it now shares come off and its two other sides go on, so the same two pieces just move. Beside three filled squares, three come off and one goes on; beside four, all four come off. So a growing patch never needs a new fence piece. The full board needs 4n pieces (8, 12, 16, 20), and s seeds bring at most 4s (two side-by-side seeds bring only 6), so s is at least n. Along the slanting line's growth the fence stays at 12 on 3-by-3 and at 16 on 4-by-4. The four middle-edge seeds of 3-by-3 bring 16, and 4 come off when the centre goes in. How often a start fills: on 2-by-2, two seeds fill in 2 of the 6 placements, the two diagonal pairs, and a side-by-side pair is stuck at once; three seeds on 2-by-2 always fill. On 3-by-3, two seeds never fill (36 placements); the biggest finish is the 2-by-2 block grown from two diagonal neighbours. Three seeds fill in 14 of 84 placements. Writing (row, column) from the top left, and counting turned and flipped copies, these are the slanting lines (2), three corners (4), (1,1), (2,3), (3,1) (4) and (1,2), (2,3), (3,1) (4). The six straight lines of three never grow. On 4-by-4, four seeds fill in 130 of 1820 placements, 1 in 14, so random tries rarely work. A straight row of four and a 2-by-2 block of four never grow. Neither does (1,2), (2,4), (3,1), (4,3), though it has a seed in every row and every column; it and its mirror image are the only two of the 24 such starts that fail. Rectangles and strips obey the same fence count: at least half the sum of the two side lengths, rounded up, and that many always suffice on the sizes checked. So 2-by-3 needs 3 (10 of 20 three-seed starts fill), 2-by-4 also needs only 3 (8 of 56) and 2-by-5 needs 4. On a one-row strip an end square has only one neighbour, so it can never be added and must be a seed. A 1-by-3 strip needs its two ends, 1-by-4 needs 3 (seeds at squares 1, 2, 4 or 1, 3, 4), and 1-by-5 needs 3, where only squares 1, 3, 5 work.

2. Every seed needed is not the same as fewest. Take seeds away one at a time, as long as the board still fills each time. When no seed can go, every seed is needed, but the start need not be the smallest. On 3-by-3 the filling starts in which every seed is needed have three seeds (14 starts) or four (9 starts), never more. So taking seeds away always stops at 3 or 4, and it can stop at 4 although 3 is possible. The four middle-edge seeds (1,2), (2,1), (2,3), (3,2) fill, and removing any one leaves a start that finishes with 6 filled squares; yet three seeds can fill. Start from the five seeds of the top row and the left column. The seeds that can go are (1,1), (1,2) and (2,1). Taking away the corner (1,1) leaves (1,2), (1,3), (2,1), (3,1), in which every seed is needed (its four removals finish with 3, 6, 3 and 6 filled squares). Taking away (1,2) and then (2,1) instead leaves three corners, which fill. The four corners fill, and any one of them can go. The smallest case: three seeds on 2-by-2 fill; removing the corner of the L leaves a diagonal pair, which fills, and removing either end leaves a side-by-side pair, which is stuck. On 4-by-4 the same gap appears one higher: 360 filling five-seed starts need every seed (48 counting turned and flipped copies once), while four is the fewest. Both older packets use the start (1,1), (1,2), (2,3), (3,3) and its four removals (finishing 6, 3, 3, 6), so the K–1 pages should use other starts.

3. The finish depends only on the seeds. Growing the same seeds in a different order always ends with the same filled squares. Why: suppose one complete run added a square that another complete run never added, and take the first such square in the first run. When it was added, two of its neighbours were already filled, by seeds or by earlier additions that the second run also made. So the second run could still add it and had not finished. Instances: from (1,1) and (2,2) on 3-by-3, the first addition can be (1,2) or (2,1), and both runs end as the top-left 2-by-2 block. From (1,1), (1,2), (2,3), it can be (1,3) or (2,2), and both end with the top two rows filled. The slanting three has 4 possible first additions and 80 complete orders, all ending full. Every finish is also made of separate boxes: the filled squares form rectangles (a single counter, a domino, a straight line, a square block, a longer block or the whole board), and no two of them touch, even at a corner. If the filled squares bent round a corner, the empty square inside the bend would touch two filled squares and could still be added. If two pieces touched at a corner, the squares beside both corners could still be added. This was checked on every finished board up to 4-by-5. Three seeds on 3-by-3 finish as a straight line that never grew (6 starts), a domino and a separate counter (8), a 2-by-2 block (16), two full rows or columns (40) or the whole board (14). This kernel answers "what always happens", and it makes "fills" or "stuck" a property of the seeds: a child who stopped early has not found a different finish.

Every number above was checked by exhaustive enumeration on the boards named.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 only. An adult reads every problem aloud. The pages assume counting to about 10 or 12 and telling side neighbours from corner neighbours. They assume no writing beyond dots for seeds on a record board, which an adult may draw instead. The children keep the mathematical choices: where the seeds go, which square to add next, and whether the board is filled or stuck. Kernel 1 is the entry and carries most of the packet. It covers filled or stuck with very few seeds, every way on the smallest boards, and the fewest seeds on growing boards, where the run 2, 3, 4 invites a prediction for the next size. Kernel 2 comes later, for children who already tell filled from stuck reliably. Kernel 3 can be noticed during the other work or asked late. The fence is adult background, which the quickest children can see with sticks after they have hunted hard for a smaller start. The grades 2–3 and 4–5 tables work at the same time from the existing packets W98-23-v3 and W98-45-v3. So the K–1 pages must use the same kit and theme and share the launch, without copying the older bands' problems. Those problems are: three seeds that fill and three that get stuck on 3-by-3; the fewest seeds for 4-by-4 with three different starts; three-seed finishes of 3, 4, 6 and 9, and how many final totals three seeds can make; the same three seeds grown in two orders; the fewest seeds for 5-by-5 and 10-by-10 in pencil; exposed sides before and after an addition; ruling out three seeds on 4-by-4 and four on 5-by-5; and the four-seed start above with its four removal boards.

Materials

The week's kit, as the catalog and guide list it: per pair, counters or square tiles no wider than 25 mm in two colours, one for seeds and one for added squares. They are used on printed boards of 30 mm squares at 100%; the packets' boards are the workboards, and there is no separate mat. The kit also lists short sticks to mark exposed sides, without a size. The launch is shared by all three tables after the five-minute run. Children handle the counters. Then an adult shows a 2-by-3 board of 30 mm squares, drawn on paper or a whiteboard, with seeds at top left, bottom left and bottom middle. The adult adds the top middle while pointing to its left and lower neighbours. Neither right-hand square can then be added, so the board stops with 4 of 6 filled. The K–1 pages should use the same convention, and no worked example on them may be the answer to a K–1 task.

The K–1 table has two pairs. Give each pair about 26 counters: 6 of the seed colour and 20 of the growth colour. That is enough for a full 5-by-5 from five seeds, a full 4-by-4 from up to six seeds, or two 3-by-3 boards side by side. The kit's 16 per pair would not fill a 5-by-5 or two 3-by-3 boards. A child who runs out of counters has not found a stuck board, so every board a page uses needs enough growth counters. The extra counters for the table are a small addition, perhaps 5 minutes of counting. Flat counters 20 to 25 mm across, such as bingo chips or 25 mm plastic squares, suit K–1 fingers and leave the grid lines visible. Nothing larger should be used, since a counter that spills into a neighbouring square hides whether that square is filled.

Boards that take counters must be printed with 30 mm squares. A 2-by-2 board is 60 mm across, 3-by-3 is 90 mm, 4-by-4 is 120 mm, 5-by-5 is 150 mm, and a 1-by-5 strip is 150 mm long. The page area is about 186 mm wide. Three 2-by-2 boards or two 3-by-3 boards fit side by side, and four 3-by-3 boards fit on a page in two rows, as on the older pages. A 4-by-4 or 5-by-5 working board takes a page row by itself. Record boards for K–1 dots need squares of at least 15 mm. Records hold only the seeds, never the order of growth. A start is reset by taking off the growth colour, and when seeds are taken away, the seed colour shows which counters may go.

Nothing on the table enforces the rule, since a counter can be put down anywhere. The partner's two-finger point at two filled side neighbours is the only check, and diagonal touching is the likeliest K–1 mistake. The older tables use the same counters and pencil boards, and nothing else in the room changes an answer. If a page uses the fence, the sticks must lie along the 30 mm sides, in the 5 mm gap left between 25 mm counters that sit in the middle of their squares. Thin card strips about 28 by 4 mm, or cut coffee stirrers, 16 to 20 per pair, would do. They are an optional addition whose fit has not been tried. The kit, the counters and the partner check have not been tried with K–1 children, and the week itself is unpiloted.
