Week 66 K–1 pages: Lamplighter streets

Page header: "Week 66 / Lamplighter streets / K–1". Packet id GGT66-K-v1; the footer reads "Bellingham Math Circle / Week 66 / GGT66-K-v1".

A walker stands on a street of lamps. Every lamp starts off and the walker starts at home. One move is a step one place left (L), a step one place right (R), or a flip of the lamp under the walker (F). A target is a picture: which lamps are lit and where the walker stands. The question throughout is the fewest moves that build a target. "Fewest moves" always counts from the dark street with the walker at home, never from wherever the pair happens to be. Below, places are numbered from home, 0, with negative numbers to the left, as on the older pages. This is notation for the writer only: the K–1 pages should show places by the picture and a home mark, not by signed numbers.

The week's only packet is the shared Grades 3–5 packet, GGT66-S-v2 (five pages). Its Problem 1 builds three targets: lamp 1 lit with the walker at 1 (2 moves), lamps −1 and 1 with the walker at 1 (5), and lamps 0 and 2 with the walker at 0 (6). Problem 2 keeps lamps −2, 0 and 2 and moves the ending: walker at −2, 0, 2 (9, 11, 9). Problem 3 asks for the fewest moves to lamps −1, 0 and 1 with the walker at 0 (7), and for an argument that fewer cannot work. Problem 4 makes each one-move change of that target (6 each). Problem 5 asks whether every target has a next move that makes it harder, and Problem 6 asks which targets are dead ends. The shared launch in the guide shows R then F starting from place −2. It then shows the goal: lamp −1 lit with the walker at 0, reached by LFLRR (5 moves) and then LFR (3). Do not use the launch's goal or its mirror image (lamp 1, walker at 0) as a K–1 task, and do not copy the older problems. Until now the K–1 table had only the guide's adult-led route, which is unpiloted. It runs the launch, then the older Problem 1 and Problem 2's A and C as picture matching. Children build with the counters and the pawn while the adult reads and writes, stack one cube per move, and compare stacks for fewest.

Mathematical kernels

1. One flip for each lit lamp, plus the shortest walk. The fewest moves to a target is the number of lit lamps plus the shortest walk that starts at home, stands on every lit lamp and ends at the walker's place. Why: a flip changes only the lamp under the walker, and every lamp starts off. So each lit lamp needs at least one flip, and flipping it again only undoes it at the cost of moves. Flips never move the walker, so the walker must get to every lit lamp and end at its place. In the other direction, walking a shortest such route and flipping each lit lamp once on the way builds the target. On a line, the walk must reach the farthest lit lamp on each side of home, in one order or the other, and then go to the walker's place; the cheaper order wins. Instances, with one shortest word each. The home lamp lit with the walker home takes 1 (F). Lamp 2 lit: walker at 2 takes 3 (RRF); at 1 or 3, 4 (RRFL, RRFR); at home, 5 (RRFLL); at −1, 6 (RRFLLL). Lamp 1 lit with the walker at −1 takes 4 (RFLL). Lamps 1 and 2: walker at 2, 4 (RFRF); at 1, 5; at home, 6. Lamps 0, 1 and 2: walker at 2, 5 (FRFRF); at home, 7. Lamps −1 and 2: walker at 2, 6 (LFRRRF); at 1 or −1, 7; at home, 8. Order matters. For lamps −1 and 2 ending at 2, going left first takes 6 and going right first takes at least 10. For lamps −2 and 1 ending at 1, it is 7 against 9. Mirror pictures take the same number of moves. A smaller fact is the number of shortest ways. A lit lamp that the shortest walk stands on twice can be lit either time. So lamps 0 and 1 with the walker home have two shortest words (FRFL, RFLF), and so do lamps 1 and 2 with the walker home (RFRFLL, RRFLFL). Lamps 0, 1 and 2 with the walker home have four, and lamps −1 and 2 ending at 2 have only one. A K–1 argument for "no fewer" points at the street: each lit lamp needs its own flip, and the walker has to get out to the farthest lamps and then to its place.

2. A dead end. A target can be harder to build than every target one move away. Lamps −1, 0 and 1 lit with the walker home takes 7 (FLFRRFL): three flips and four steps, and both are unavoidable. Step left, and the new target, with the walker on lamp −1, takes 6 (FRFLLF). Step right: 6 (FLFRRF). Flip the home lamp off, leaving lamps −1 and 1 with the walker home: 6 (LFRRFL). So an eighth move makes a picture that is quicker to build from the dark street than the picture after seven. Why: a walker that must finish in the middle has to walk out to both ends and come back. One that finishes on an end lamp saves the last step back, and switching the home lamp off saves a flip without saving any walking. Every move changes the fewest count by exactly one (see kernel 3), so each move makes a target exactly one easier or one harder. Most targets have a harder move. Lamp 2 lit with the walker on it (3) becomes 4 after a step either way. Lamps −1 and 1 with the walker home (6) becomes 7 if the home lamp is lit. The dead ends are exactly the targets with the walker home, the home lamp lit and at least one lit lamp on each side. The smallest is the 7 above, which is also the older packet's Problem 3 target. Next come lamps −2, 0, 1 and lamps −1, 0, 2 (9; each next move gives 8), then lamps −2, −1, 0, 1 and lamps −1, 0, 1, 2 (10; each next move gives 9).

3. Every way to build a target has the same evenness. If one way to build a target uses an odd number of moves, every way does, and the same holds for even. Every number from the fewest upward in steps of two works. Why: the number of lit lamps plus the walker's place starts at 0, and each move changes it by exactly one. So it is odd exactly when an odd number of moves has been made. A flip and a flip back adds two moves to any route. A count a child can make: lit lamps plus the walker's steps from home is odd exactly when every way takes an odd number of moves. Instances. The home lamp lit with the walker home: 1 (F); 3 works (FFF, LRF, FLR); 2 and 4 never work. The walker at 1 with no lamp lit: 1 (R); 3 works (RLR, FFR); 2 never works. The dark street with the walker home: 0; 2 works (LR, FF); 1 and 3 never work. Lamps 1 and 2 with the walker at 2: 4 (RFRF); 6 works; 5 never works. At K–1 this is experiment and conjecture: children can find that some counts never work and that the counts that work go up by twos. Their likely explanation is that spare moves come in pairs (a step and a step back, a flip and a flip back). That explains why adding two always works, but not why an odd number of spare moves is impossible; that part is the adult's invariant.

Every count above was checked by code. Breadth-first search from the dark street was compared with the walk formula, on an endless street and on a street of seven lamps (places −3 to 3). All 896 targets of the seven-lamp street take the same fewest moves there as on an endless street, and at its ends no target has both of its available moves easier. Shortest words were listed by enumeration. The order split came from a search that records which end lamp the walker reaches first. The dead-end rule was checked over every target of the seven-lamp street. The exact-count claims were checked for every target with fewest at most 7 and counts up to 11.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 only. Children do no reading: an adult reads every problem aloud, and the pages show targets as pictures. Numbers stay up to about 10 (the K–1 instances above run from 1 to 10), and children compare them as cube towers more than as numerals. There are no signed numbers and no letters for children to write; the adult writes any move word, using the launch's L, R and F. Start with kernel 1 on single lit lamps and short walks, then two or three lamps where the ending place or the order of visiting changes the count. Kernels 2 and 3 are late, for the quickest pairs with the adult, and the writer may use one or both. The grades 2–3 and 4–5 tables work at the same time from the existing shared Grades 3–5 packet (GGT66-S-v2), so the K–1 pages must use the same kit and theme and share the launch, without copying the older bands' problems.

Materials

The K–1 table uses the week's kit, as listed in the guide's materials and K–1 paragraphs (GGT66-FAC-v2). Each of the two pairs has nine two-sided lamp counters about 17 mm across or smaller, one clearly different walker pawn, and about a dozen cubes per child, so about 24 per pair. The guide's fallback for counters is paper squares dark on one side. Linking cubes stand up better as towers. There are also pencils and the printed pages. Nothing needs cutting, and nothing beyond the guide's kit is needed. The kit has not been tried with K–1 children. Counter fit on the printed lamps is unrehearsed, and no part of Week 66 has been piloted.

Physical constraints the pages must respect. The older pages' working street has nine lamps, places −4 to 4, drawn as 13 mm circles on 19 mm centres, with numbers under them and a home mark at 0. A 17 mm counter there leaves about 2 mm between neighbours, which is tight for K–1 fingers. The older small target pictures (2.4 mm lamps on 6.3 mm centres) are too small to match lamp for lamp. The K–1 pages should print their own working street and their own larger target pictures. Seven lamps (home and three on each side) on about 24 mm centres take 168 mm of the 180 mm between the template's 0.7 in margins. That leaves room for lamp circles a little larger than the counters and a separate walker spot above each lamp. Keep every K–1 target within three places of home; then the seven-lamp street changes no answer. If only the common 2–2.5 cm counters are available, the street needs about 30 mm centres. Seven lamps then take 210 mm, which fits only across a landscape page (244 mm). The pawn stands in its spot above its lamp, never on the counter. Each counter stays on its own lamp, because a counter pushed beside a lamp loses its place. Whichever side of the counter means lit must match the printed pictures, where a lit lamp is a filled circle and an unlit lamp an open circle. The launch fixes this.

Two rules live only on paper: a flip changes only the lamp under the walker, and a step moves one place. Week 2's paper lamps failed because children changed whichever lamp they wanted. The older pages enforce these rules with partner jobs. One partner moves the walker, and the other flips only after pointing to the walker's lamp; they swap jobs between targets. At the K–1 table the adult also watches. Whoever makes a move adds a cube to the tower. The tower is the count and the built street is the state, so children need not record letters. Reset before each attempt: all counters to the unlit side, the pawn home, the tower taken apart. A pair has one street set, so a target to come back to, such as the picture after one more move in kernel 2, must be on paper, printed or coloured in by the child, not kept built. Comparing the dead end with its three neighbours as four standing towers takes 7 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 25 cubes, one more than a pair's two dozen. The pair can borrow from the other pair, or the adult can write each count.
