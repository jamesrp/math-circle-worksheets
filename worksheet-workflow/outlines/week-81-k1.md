Week 81 K–1 pages: Buckets of Fish

Page header: "Week 81 / Buckets of Fish / K–1". Packet id W81-K-v1; the footer reads "Bellingham Math Circle / Week 81 / W81-K-v1".

The game. Counters (the fish) sit in buckets numbered 1, 2, 3 from left to right. Players take turns. A move takes one counter out of a bucket that has one. Then, if the player wishes, they put one new counter from the supply into each bucket with a smaller number than the one they took from: into some of those buckets, all, or none, but never two into one bucket, never into the bucket they took from, and never into a bigger-numbered bucket. A player who cannot move loses, so whoever takes the last counter wins. With two buckets the rule read aloud is "Take one. If you took it from bucket 2, you may put one in bucket 1." Below, (a, b) means a counters in bucket 1 and b in bucket 2, and (a, b, c) adds bucket 3.

Mathematical kernels

1. Pairs win for the second player, and copying is how. The second player can always win exactly when every bucket holds an even number, that is, when each bucket's counters can be put in pairs; otherwise the first player can always win. Why: from a position where every bucket is in pairs, whatever the partner does leaves the bucket they took from with an odd number, so it is not empty, and the second player can take from that same bucket and put new counters exactly where the partner put them. Every bucket is back in pairs. The game always ends (kernel 2) and the empty board is in pairs, so the copier makes the last move. Copying works with three buckets too, and with any refill rule. From any other position the first player wins by making every bucket pairs and then copying. With the one-each rule there is exactly one such move: take from the rightmost bucket with an odd number, and put one in each bucket on its left that has an odd number. One bucket is the base case: no refill is possible, the game lasts exactly as many moves as there are counters, and the first player wins exactly when that number is odd. Instances with two buckets: of the nine starts with at most two in each bucket, the second player wins (0, 0), (0, 2), (2, 0) and (2, 2), and the first player wins the other five. The first player's one winning move on each start up to three per bucket: (1, 0) and (0, 1) to (0, 0); (1, 1) and (2, 1) to (2, 0); (1, 2) to (0, 2); (3, 0) to (2, 0); (0, 3) to (0, 2); (3, 1) to (4, 0); (1, 3), (3, 2) and (2, 3) to (2, 2); (3, 3) to (4, 2). So the choice to refill matters: (1, 1) is won only by taking from bucket 2 and putting one in bucket 1, while (0, 1) and (2, 1) are won only by taking from bucket 2 and putting nothing in bucket 1. Without refills at all the winner would depend only on whether the total is odd, and (1, 1) would be a second-player win. From (2, 2) the partner has three moves, to (1, 2), (2, 1) and (3, 1), and each has one winning reply, to (0, 2), (2, 0) and (4, 0), which is the copy. Three buckets: (2, 2, 2) and (0, 2, 0) are second-player wins; (0, 0, 1) is won by taking the counter and adding nothing; (1, 1, 1) is won only by taking from bucket 3 and putting one in buckets 1 and 2, giving (2, 2, 0).

2. Every game ends, and how long it can last. Bucket 2 of two never gets new counters, so every game takes from it exactly as many times as it started with; after that only bucket 1 can change, and it only goes down. With three buckets, bucket 3 never gets new counters, so after its last take the two-bucket game is left, which ends. This holds even though a move can add counters: from (0, 0, 1) the board can reach two counters, and from (0, 0, 2) four. Each move puts exactly one counter in the Removed box, and at the end the board is empty, so the counters in Removed count the moves, and every counter used in a game ends there. With the one-each rule, a two-bucket game from (a, b) lasts at least a + b moves (never refill) and at most a + 2b (always refill), and every number in between can happen: each counter in bucket 2 is worth at most two moves. With three buckets the range is a + b + c to a + 2b + 4c, every number between possible: a counter in bucket 3 is worth at most four. The longest game comes from always putting one in every bucket allowed, whichever bucket you take from. Shortest and longest: (0, 1) 1 and 2; (0, 2) 2 and 4; (0, 3) 3 and 6; (1, 1) 2 and 3; (2, 2) 4 and 6; (3, 3) 6 and 9; (0, 0, 1) 1 and 4; (0, 1, 1) 2 and 6; (1, 1, 1) 3 and 7; (0, 0, 2) 2 and 8; (1, 2, 1) 4 and 9; (0, 0, 3) 3 and 12; (2, 2, 2) 6 and 14. The winner is whoever moves last, so the refills are how the players steer whether the game has an odd or even number of moves: from (2, 2) games of 4, 5 or 6 moves can happen, but the second player can always make it even. Adult background, not a K–1 task: if any number of counters may be put in each smaller bucket, a game from (0, 1) whose first move puts M in bucket 1 lasts exactly M + 1 moves, so there is no longest game, yet every game still ends for the same reason. The grades 4–5 table works on exactly this.

Two misreadings change the answers. If a child hears "may" as "must" and always refills, every game has a fixed length and the first player wins exactly when bucket 1 is odd, so (0, 1) and (2, 1) become second-player wins. If a counter may go back into the bucket it came from, (1, 0) can move to itself and a game need not end; an earlier wording of the K–1 rule allowed this ("Take one. You may put one in bucket 1."), so any wording must name where the refill may go.

Every count, key, winning move, length range, board maximum and misreading above was checked by exhaustive search: winners for two buckets up to 8 each and three up to 5 each, the copy strategy for refill caps 1 to 3 on one to three buckets, the uniqueness of the winning move, and every length range for two buckets up to 6 and three up to 3.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 only. An adult reads every problem aloud; the pages assume numbers up to about 10 or 12, seeing a count of up to three dots at a glance, pairing counters to see "even", and bucket numbers 1, 2, 3 as labels, with no written records beyond what an adult can keep. Kernel 1 comes first and carries most of the packet: play one bucket and then two buckets, against a partner and against the adult, swapping who starts; find who can always win and the winning first move; notice pairs. The adult's guide can have the adult copy the child's moves without saying so, but naming the copy is the children's discovery, not printed. Kernel 2 comes later: work together to make a game as short or as long as possible, counting Removed, with starts whose longest game is 12 moves or fewer, then a three-bucket start where the board gains counters, and ask whether a game can go on forever. Three buckets belong late in the packet. The guide's current K–1 route is adult-led: after the launch, play the nine two-bucket cards with at most two in each bucket under the rule above and sort them into two piles; that K–1 children manage this is a prediction. These pages are meant to give K–1 its own problems. The grades 2–3 and 4–5 tables work at the same time from the existing shared packet W81-S-v3, so the K–1 pages must use the same kit and theme and share the launch, without copying the older bands' problems: which of (0, 0, 1), (1, 1, 0), (2, 1, 1) and (2, 2, 2) the first player wins; sorting all sixteen two-bucket cards into first-player and second-player wins; finding a rule for a winning move from (2, 0, 2), (1, 2, 2), (2, 1, 2), (2, 2, 1), (0, 2, 0) and (3, 3, 1); and, for grades 4–5 with any refill, making the (0, 1) game last 10, 30 and 100 moves and asking whether a game from (0, 3) or (1, 1, 1) can go on forever.

Materials

The week's kit, printed at 100% on US Letter, single-sided; one set per pair, so the K–1 table has two of each. The bucket board (packet page 2): three rounded rectangles 56 mm wide and 139 mm tall with 5 mm gaps, labelled Bucket 1, Bucket 2 and Bucket 3 from left to right above them, and below them two boxes 86 by 40 mm labelled Supply and Removed; it fills a page. Sixteen sorting cards (packet page 3), cut in advance along dashed lines: each card is 39 by 27.5 mm and shows two small boxes labelled 1 and 2 with 0 to 3 dots of about 4 mm, every pair from (0, 0) to (3, 3), an empty box meaning zero. About thirty loose counters per pair and a reserve; the kit does not give a counter size, and the buckets take several counters of up to about 20 mm. For two buckets, cover bucket 3 with a sheet of scrap paper without relabelling. The kit needs no addition for K–1.

The launch, shared with the whole room: remove one counter from the middle bucket, then add at most one counter to the bucket on its left, and show the result; the partner points to the chosen bucket before the removal and checks each refill. The older packet's worked picture is (1, 1, 2), then one taken from bucket 3, then one added to buckets 1 and 2, giving (2, 2, 1). Start pictures in that packet are small labelled boxes with dots, numbers 1, 2, 3 beneath; they are pictures to set up on the board, not places to put counters. A K–1 page that wants children to place real counters on it needs buckets about as large as the kit's; two such buckets take about 117 by 139 mm, roughly half a page.

Constraints the pages must respect. Kindergartners may not tell left from right reliably, so state where a refill may go by bucket number, or show it in a picture, not with "left" alone; the board's printed numbers carry the order. Any rule wording must forbid putting a counter back where it came from and must make the refill optional (see the misreadings above). The removed counter goes to Removed and refills come from Supply, which stays off the board and is not a bucket; sliding a counter from bucket 2 into bucket 1 gives the same buckets but loses the move from Removed, which matters when moves are counted. Two-bucket starts up to three per bucket keep six or fewer counters on the board; three-bucket starts up to (2, 2, 2) keep eight or fewer, but its longest game is 14 moves, more than K–1 should count. Only the partner enforces the rule, so rules must be easy to check by watching one move. The grades 4–5 table may be playing with any number of refills and writing numbers for counts at the same time; the K–1 table keeps the one-each rule. The kit has not been tried with K–1 children; the board, counters and cards have never been handled in a session, and the week is unpiloted.
