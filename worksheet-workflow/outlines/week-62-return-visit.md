Mathematical kernels

1. Allowed slots (list colouring). Each circle has the slots its card may use printed beside it; a schedule puts every card in one of its own slots with no line joining two cards in the same slot. Two allowed slots per card can fail on a network that needs only two slots, and whether identical or different lists are harder depends on the network. Six cards, A and B each joined to C, D, E, F: with every list {1, 2} it fits, but A {1, 2}, B {3, 4}, C {1, 3}, D {1, 4}, E {2, 3}, F {2, 4} cannot, because A and B have 2 × 2 = 4 joint choices and each fills both slots of exactly one of C–F. Of the 46,656 ways to give these cards two slots from 1–4, exactly the 144 of this form fail (A and B disjoint, C–F carrying the four mixed pairs). The familiar five cards (A and B each joined to C, D, E) never fail: all 7,776 two-slot assignments from 1–4 work, as do all 4,900 from any slots up to renaming. On a ring, two allowed slots per card fail only when the ring is odd and every card has the same two. Proof: otherwise two neighbours u, v have different lists; put v in a slot u lacks, go round away from u giving each card a slot different from the one before, and u at the end must avoid only its other neighbour. So the five-ring fails with every list {1, 2} and works once any one ring card has {1, 3}, with or without the leaf AF. Trees never fail with two allowed slots: work outward from any card, so each new card has one placed neighbour; in general, an order in which each card has more allowed slots than placed neighbours never gets stuck (the first-slot rule with lists). Adult context (Erdős–Rubin–Taylor): lists as long as each card's number of lines suffice on a connected network unless every block is all-joined or an odd ring; four all-joined cards with every list {1, 2, 3} fail, while the diamond with lists of 3, 3, 2, 2 slots at A, B, C, D (its line counts) always works from 1–4. Checked exhaustively: every count above, rings of 3–8 with lists from 1–4 and of 3–6 with any slots; both base trees, the star and the path with all two-slot lists from 1–4; all 576 diamond lists.

2. One card at a time (colour reconfiguration). A move changes one card's slot, and the schedule must stay legal after every move. With two slots, a connected network with a line has two schedules and no card can move; a spare slot is a parking place. Fewest moves with slots 1–3: line A–B, A1 B2 to A2 B1, 3 (A3, B1, A2); path A–B–C, A1 B2 C1 to A2 B1 C2, 4 (B3, A2, C2, B1); path A–B–C–D, 1212 to 2121, 6 (A3, C3, B1, A2, D1, C2); star, A1 with leaves 2 to A2 with leaves 1, 7 (A3, the leaves to 1, A2), while parking the five leaves first takes 11. Every schedule is stuck for four all-joined cards with four slots and for the diamond or a triangle with three; with four slots the triangle turns A1 B2 C3 into A2 B3 C1 in 4 (A4, C1, B3, A2). With at least two more slots than the most lines at any card, every schedule reaches every other: fix cards to their targets one at a time; an unfixed neighbour holding the slot the current card needs has few enough neighbours to step aside, and then the current card moves. So with four slots every path and ring is fully connected. Trees need only three slots, however many lines meet at a card, since a leaf can always step into the slot its neighbour is neither in nor moving to; generally two more slots than the degeneracy suffice (the most lines a card still has when cards are removed one at a time, fewest lines first). With three slots, a ring card is stuck when its two neighbours hold the other two slots, so an all-stuck ring repeats 1, 2, 3 and its length is a multiple of 3: rings of 3 and 6 have exactly 6 stuck schedules, rings of 4, 5, 7, 8 none. Trapped without being stuck: on a ring with three slots call each step up (1 to 2, 2 to 3, 3 to 1) or down; a card can move only when its two neighbours share a slot, and the move swaps an up-down pair, so the number of ups never changes. The five-ring's 30 schedules form two families of 15 (1 or 4 ups): every schedule has a movable card, yet A1 B2 C3 D1 E2 never becomes A1 B3 C2 D1 E3. Non-stuck ring schedules with equal up-counts always connect. With three slots the five-ring with leaf splits 30 and 30, and the six-ring with two leaves has 264 schedules: one family of 240 and six of 4, where a stuck ring holds still while its leaves move. Checked by search: every fewest-move count and route; rings of 3–8 with three and four slots; both base trees, the star and the path with three slots; the two-more-than-degeneracy rule on all 1,099 labelled networks of up to five cards.

3. Arrows: slots in order (precedence scheduling). Slot 1 now happens first. An arrow from X to Y means X goes in an earlier slot than Y; cards with no arrow between them may share a slot. A schedule exists exactly when no arrows run in a circle (A to B to C to A has no schedule). The fewest slots equals the most cards on one path that follows the arrows: that path needs as many different slots, and earliest placement uses exactly that many (a card with no arrow into it in slot 1, any other one slot after the latest card pointing to it). This is Mirsky's theorem, the dual of Week 19's Dilworth; unlike the first-slot rule on conflicts, earliest placement never wastes a slot. Instance: arrows A to B, B to C, C to D, E to B, F to C, G to H give A, E, F, G in 1, B and H in 2, C in 3, D in 4, and the path A, B, C, D shows four are needed. Back to conflict lines (Gallai–Hasse–Roy–Vitaver): give every line a direction. A schedule gives arrows from smaller to larger slot numbers, so no arrow path visits more cards than there are slots; conversely, arrows with no circle give, by earliest placement, a legal conflict schedule with as many slots as the longest arrow path has cards. So the fewest slots equals the shortest the longest arrow path can be made, and two slots work exactly when arrows can make every card all-out or all-in. On the five-ring with leaf, all 64 ways to point its six lines leave a path through 3 cards. Adult aside (Stanley): the number of circle-free arrow choices equals, up to sign, the first visit's schedule count with −1 slots: path A–B–C–D 8, diamond 18, four all-joined cards 24, five-ring with leaf 60. Checked exhaustively: the instance; earliest placement on all 29,853 circle-free arrow patterns on up to five cards, and its minimality on up to four; the arrow equality on every familiar network and on all 1,099 labelled networks of up to five cards, circles included; the four Stanley counts.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1: kernels 1 and 2 with counters on full-size networks of two to six cards: give every circle a counter of a slot printed beside it, where a card with one allowed slot forces its neighbours and a puzzle can be impossible (point to the line that breaks); then turn a shown schedule into a target one counter at a time (A–B, a three-card path, the star) and find the spare slot as a parking place. This needs matching numerals 1–4 to the headers' key counters, finding a line's two ends and comparing colours; an adult reads. Grades 2–3: forced chains, the familiar five cards that never fail against the six that can, and changed lists on an odd ring; fewest-move swaps and which rings can be completely stuck with three slots; arrow paths as a continuation. Counting to eight, comparing numbers, a short move word; an adult reads. Grades 4–5: explanations with objects or drawings: why the six-card lists fail and the ring rule; the up-count that traps five-ring schedules and why four slots free any ring; earliest placement against the longest arrow path, then arrows on a conflict network (five-ring with leaf). No arithmetic beyond counting; the demands are following a ring, keeping a move word and holding an invariant. Not every kernel belongs in every packet.

Materials

The base kit, reused as it is; nothing is added. Seven kits (two for K–1, two for grades 2–3, one for grades 4–5, one for the launch, one spare), each with activity cards A–H and slot headers 1–4, 31 by 38 mm, cut from week-62-materials.pdf (W62-M-v1, three kits per card-stock sheet); 32 counters, 8 in each of four colours and at most 15 mm across, one colour per slot number, the same in every kit (for example 1 red, 2 blue, 3 yellow, 4 green), with a key counter in each header's 16 mm dashed circle; two US Letter sleeves, a tissue and a folder or tray. Eight fine-tip dry-erase markers in all; pencil does not mark sleeves. The new student pages are the network mats; the base packet week-62-students.pdf (W62-S-v2) is not needed on the tables.

Physical constraints. At most eight circles per network, lettered from A–H with each letter once (one card per letter per kit). Only four slots: lists come from 1–4, moves use at most four slots, and an arrow path through five cards cannot fit (a usable impossibility). Pages print in black and white and slot colours are a kit choice, so give slot numbers, never colour names. Working circles stay 20 mm across with lines centre to centre; a counter covers the inside, so allowed-slot numbers sit just outside the circle, clear of lines, and arrowheads stay visible outside the circle they point to or sit mid-line. For moves, counters on a full-size network hold the state (cards optional), a target can be a compact copy with numbers in its circles, one move swaps one counter, the partner checks only the moved card's lines, and a running move word such as B3 A2 C2 B1 plus the final state is the whole record. For arrows, lay the headers in a row in order 1, 2, 3, 4; earlier means a strictly smaller number. Leave room below each header for all eight cards. Each new convention (allowed slots, a single move, an arrow) needs a small worked visual on a non-task network before first use. Print single-sided on US Letter at 100%. Physical handling, as in the base week, is unrehearsed.
