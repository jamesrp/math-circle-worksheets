Week 5: Tower cities

Mathematical kernels

1. Views of a row. Stand towers of heights 1, 2, …, n in a row, each height once. Looking along the row from one end at eye level, you see a tower exactly when it is taller than every tower in front of it. So the tallest tower is always seen from both ends; you see exactly one tower from the left exactly when the tallest is first; and with two or more towers you can never see only one tower from each end. For heights 1, 2, 3 the six orders and their (left, right) views are: 123 (3,1), 132 (2,2), 213 (2,1), 231 (2,2), 312 (1,2), 321 (1,3). For heights 1 to 4 there are 24 orders; the view pair (2,2) happens for 6 of them, (1,4) and (4,1) for one each, and (1,1) never. The number of orders of 1..n with k towers seen from the left is the Stirling number of the first kind: for n = 3 they are 2, 3, 1 for k = 1, 2, 3, and for n = 4 they are 6, 11, 6, 1.

2. Tower cities. An n-by-n city has one tower in each square, heights 1 to n, each height exactly once in every row and every column (a Latin square). A number outside the grid says how many towers are seen looking along that row or column from that end. Some clue sets allow exactly one city; others allow several, and removing one clue can let a second city in. There are 2 different 2-by-2 cities, 12 different 3-by-3 cities, and 576 different 4-by-4 cities. A good puzzle has exactly one answer, and a puzzle maker can check that by trying to build a second city. Every printed puzzle must be checked by computer: it has exactly one solution unless the task says otherwise.

Sources: Stirling numbers of the first kind and records in permutations (Stanley, Enumerative Combinatorics, Vol. 1, §1.3); Latin squares (van Lint and Wilson, A Course in Combinatorics, Chapter 17); "Skyscrapers" puzzles as published by puzzle magazines.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 builds rows of three towers, crouches to eye level to count what they see from each end, finds every order, and plays a hidden-row game with a partner (one hides a row behind a folder and says how many towers are seen from each end; the partner builds a row that fits); grades 2–3 build 3-by-3 cities, solve clue puzzles, and make a puzzle with only one answer for a partner; grades 4–5 solve a ladder of 4-by-4 puzzles from easy to hard and design puzzles with as few clues as possible that a partner can solve, with counting all rows of four by their views available.

Materials

Snap cubes (about 3/4 inch) in several colours, about 250 in all: 6 per K–1 child for one row of three towers, 18 per 2–3 child for a 3-by-3 city, 40 per 4–5 child for a 4-by-4 city. Building is slow: a 4-by-4 city has 16 towers, so grades 4–5 pages should not ask children to rebuild whole cities many times; children may also record a city by writing heights in a grid. Printed city grids with 1-inch squares and room for clue numbers outside the grid. File folders to use as screens. Children check a view by putting their eye at table level at the end of a row. Paper, pencils, small whiteboards. Children work in pairs: one builds, one checks the views, then swap.
