Week 10: Bridges and one-stroke drawings

Mathematical kernels

1. Walking every bridge once. A town has islands (dots) joined by bridges (lines); you can walk from any island to any other. A walk that crosses every bridge exactly once exists exactly when the number of islands with an odd number of bridges is 0 or 2. With 0 odd islands the walk can start anywhere and ends where it started; with 2 odd islands it must start at one of them and end at the other. The reason is pairing: every time a walk passes through an island it arrives on one bridge and leaves on another, so the bridges at an island where the walk neither starts nor ends are used in pairs. The number of odd islands is always even, because every bridge has two ends. Adding a bridge between two odd islands makes both even. The seven bridges of Königsberg join four land areas with 5, 3, 3 and 3 bridges: four odd, so no such walk exists. A one-stroke drawing (drawing a picture without lifting the pencil or retracing a line) is the same question with corners and crossings of the picture as islands.

2. Joining loops. A walk that comes back to its start can be lengthened by splicing in a loop at any island it visits: walk to that island, go around the new loop, and continue. Repeating this builds a walk through every bridge of a town with no odd islands (Hierholzer's method).

3. Delivery routes (extension). If a truck must cross every bridge at least once and return to its start, the bridges it crosses twice must pair up the odd islands; the fewest extra crossings comes from the cheapest way to pair the odd islands by routes. For example, four islands each joined to every other (six bridges, all four islands odd) need two extra crossings for a closed route, and one extra crossing for a route that may end elsewhere.

Sources: Euler, "Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis" (1736); Biggs, Lloyd and Wilson, Graph Theory 1736–1936, Chapter 1; any discrete mathematics text on Euler trails (for example Levin, Discrete Mathematics: An Open Introduction, §4.4); Edmonds and Johnson (1973) on the Chinese postman problem.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 walks a token over the bridges of small towns, picking up the counter from each bridge it crosses, tries one-stroke drawings, and builds towns for a partner with hexagon islands and craft-stick bridges; grades 2–3 find where walks can start and end, count bridges at each island, repair towns by adding a bridge, and design puzzle towns for a partner; grades 4–5 explain the rule, build long walks by joining loops, decide Königsberg, and find the fewest repeated bridges for delivery routes.

Materials

Printed town maps with large island circles (about 3/4 inch) and bridges drawn as wide bands that a counter can sit on. At the start a counter sits on every bridge; crossing a bridge means picking up its counter, so no bridge can be crossed twice and the counters left show which bridges remain. Counters, plenty. Craft sticks (4.5 inches) as bridges and yellow hexagon pattern blocks as islands, for children to build their own towns on the table. Masking tape to make one town on the floor that a child can walk. Pencils, coloured pencils, paper, small whiteboards. Children work in pairs: one walks, the other checks and keeps the counters; then they swap or make a town for each other.
