Week 53 return visit: Dearest links, paid trips and optional places

Mathematical kernels

1. The lowest dearest link, and opening links by price. Use the familiar kind of map: fixed places, whole printed links, a positive price on each link. For a route between two places, its dearest link is the largest price on it. Let h(u, v) be the smallest dearest link among all routes from u to v (a minimax or bottleneck route). Claim: in every cheapest network, the route between any two places has dearest link exactly h(u, v), for every pair at once. Conversely, a connecting network with no loop whose route between every pair has this property is a cheapest network. Why: suppose some route R from u to v uses only links cheaper than the dearest link e on the network's route. Return e; the network falls into two sides, R crosses between them on some link f cheaper than e, and buying f gives a cheaper network. For the converse, each unused link is itself a route, so it costs at least the dearest link on the network route between its ends, which is the base week's swap certificate. Opening links in price order shows this concretely. Open every link priced at most k. Groups of places that can reach each other only merge as k grows, and u and v first share a group exactly at k = h(u, v). Counting groups gives more. Let c_k be the number of groups when the links priced at most k are open, with c_0 the number of places. Then every cheapest network has exactly c_(k−1) − c_k links of price k. So all cheapest networks share one price list, and the cheapest cost is (c_0 − 1) + (c_1 − 1) + (c_2 − 1) + …, found without choosing any network. Limit: making only the network's own dearest link as small as possible does not make it cheapest. On the square AB=1, BC=CD=DA=3, the network BC, CD, DA has the least possible dearest link (3) but costs 9; the minimum is 7. Instances on the base week's maps: on the distinct-price square (AB=1, BC=2, CD=3, DA=4, AC=5, BD=6), h(A, D) = 3 although AD costs 4, h(B, D) = 3 although BD costs 6, and h(A, C) = 2 although AC costs 5. Its groups are {A,B}{C}{D} at price 1, {A,B,C}{D} at 2 and one group at 3, so the cost is 3 + 2 + 1 = 6. On the six-place map (AB=1, BC=2, AC=3, CD=2, DE=1, EF=2, DF=4, BD=5, CE=6), the price-1 links give {A,B}{C}{D,E}{F} and the price-2 links join everything: 5 + 3 = 8, and h(A, F) = 2. On the trap map (AB=1, BC=2, AC=3, CD=4, AD=5) the group counts are 4, 3, 2, 2, 1: cost 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 7, and h(A, D) = 4. The three cheapest networks of the four-place tie map (AB=BC=AC=1, CD=2, AD=3) all use prices 1, 1, 2; the three of the five-place map (AB=BC=AC=1, CD=DE=2, CE=3, AD=5, BE=6) all use 1, 1, 2, 2. Opening by price is a way to look at a map, not a method to print for finding the cheapest network. Adult background only: h(A, C) is never more than the larger of h(A, B) and h(B, C), which is Week 100's nested-worlds inequality; leave that theorem to Week 100.

2. Pay once or pay every trip. Keep the network rule (each bought link is paid for once) and add a second way to pay: a traveller pays a link's price every time the pawn walks it. The cheapest trip between two places is the cheapest route on the whole printed map. Claim: the cheapest networks depend only on the order of the prices, while cheapest trips depend on their sums. Adding the same amount c to every price adds exactly c(n − 1) to every connecting network with no loop, because each has n − 1 links (the base week's count). So the cheapest networks stay the same. A route with j links gains cj, so a route with fewer links can overtake. Doubling every price changes neither the cheapest networks nor the cheapest trips. Any order-preserving repricing, such as adding a constant or squaring, leaves the cheapest networks unchanged, because the swap certificate and the opening-by-price picture compare prices and never add them; it can change the cheapest trips. Separately, the cheapest network can make a trip much dearer than the cheapest trip. Instances: on the ring AB=BC=CD=DE=EF=1, AF=2, the cheapest network (5) leaves out AF, so the A–F trip costs 5 on it against 2 on AF. With four places, AB=BC=CD=1 and AD=4, the cheapest network is AB, BC, CD (3) and the cheapest A–D trip is ABCD (3). After adding 2 to every price, the network is still AB, BC, CD (now 9), but the cheapest A–D trip is AD (6 against 9). Adding 1 already makes AD win (5 against 6). On the six-place map the cheapest network AB, BC, CD, DE, EF is unchanged after adding 1, 2 or 3, doubling or squaring. Its cheapest A–F trips are ABCDEF and ACDEF (8). After adding 1 they are ACDEF and ACDF (12). After adding 2 only ACDF is cheapest (15), while the network's own A–F route costs 18. After doubling, the trips are again ABCDEF and ACDEF (16). Limit for adults: if prices are lowered to 0 or below, the cheapest loop-free network is still the same, but the cheapest connected purchase can include a loop. With a 1, 1, 1 triangle lowered by 1, buying all three links also costs 0; lowered by 2, the whole loop (−3) is the only cheapest purchase. Keep printed prices positive.

3. Optional places. Mark some places as optional with a different symbol. A network must connect every required place; it may pass through optional places and need not reach them. Connecting an optional place can lower the cost. With only two required places, a cheapest network is exactly a cheapest trip between them; with every place required, it is the base week's cheapest network. Between these lies the Steiner tree problem in graphs, for which no fast general method is known (its decision form is one of Karp's 21 NP-complete problems). The base week's local test fails here: a purchase that no single buy, return or one-for-one swap makes cheaper can still cost more than the cheapest. Positive prices still rule out loops, and every optional place in a cheapest network has at least two bought links, since a single link to an optional place could be returned. With k required places, at most k − 2 optional places have three or more bought links, because every leaf of the network is a required place. Instances: in the star map, A, B and C are required, with AB=BC=AC=4, and an optional S has SA=SB=SC=2. Without S the cheapest cost is 8 (any two triangle links). With S it is 6, and SA, SB, SC is the only cheapest network. The three purchases of two triangle links are stuck: no single change that keeps A, B and C connected is cheaper. Every chain of single changes from AB, BC to SA, SB, SC passes a purchase costing at least 10, for example buy SA (10), swap AB for SB (8), swap BC for SC (6). With S's links at 3 the optional place does not pay (8, S unused, three cheapest networks). With SA=SB=2 and SC=4 there are six cheapest networks at 8, some using S and some not. On a square with sides 4 and an optional centre X joined to each corner at 2, the cost is 8 through X against 12 without it. With required A and B, AB=5, and optional S with AS=SB=2, the cheapest network is 4, through S. On the six-place map with only A and F required, the cheapest networks are exactly the two cheapest A–F trips (8).

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1: lay a tape marker on every one-dot link, move the pawn to find which places it can reach, then open the two-dot links (kernel 1); pay for a network joining three circled places with and without the optional place and compare the two payment piles (kernel 3). Use dot prices up to 4 and totals up to 12. The adult reads and the child chooses, lays markers and pays. Most K–1 children will not have met Week 53, so the shared launch must re-teach buying and paying. Grades 2–3: routes with the lowest dearest link and groups as links open (kernel 1), then paying per trip against paying once, including adding the same amount to every price (kernel 2). This needs counting links on a route, adding 1 or 2 per link and totals up to about 20. Many will have met only the base week's pages 1–4, so do not assume the swap test. Grades 4–5: why every cheapest network contains a lowest-dearest route for every pair, why all cheapest networks share one price list, why adding to every price cannot change the cheapest networks but can change trips, and why the star map has a stuck purchase that is not cheapest (kernels 1–3). Explanations can use markers and two drawn sides; zero prices, the converse and the k − 2 fact are extensions.

Materials

The base week's kit, reused as it is. Five kits (two K–1 pairs, two 2–3 pairs, the upper trio), each with 30 equal counters about 12–15 mm across, ten bought-link markers (strips of removable tape about 5 by 20 mm, laid along a link beside its price and lifted off to return it), one small pawn or a pointing finger, a sleeve with a dry-erase pen, and an off-map payment tray. The demonstration adds 15 counters and four markers. Add one sheet of scrap paper per kit as a second payment area, so two totals (a network and a trip, or before and after a price change) can lie side by side. The base week has no separate materials PDF: its maps are on the pages of the student packet, week-53-students.pdf (W53-networks-v2). Familiar maps can be redrawn with their prices for this visit: the trap map (p. 3), the four- and five-place tie maps (pp. 2 and 4), the six-place map (pp. 6 and 8) and the distinct-price square (p. 9). The new pages carry their own maps.

Physical constraints. Counters go in the tray, never along links. Any map a child works on has at most ten links, and any single payment a child is asked to make (a network, a trip, or a total after a price change) is at most 30 counters. After adding 2 to every price, the six-place map's links total 44, so children compare named totals there rather than buying everything. K–1 pages show every price as a numeral with that many dots (at most six). Each price label sits on its own link, clear of other links and of place circles; the base week's first print misplaced two labels on the six-place map. Drawn length never encodes price, and an unmarked crossing is not a place. Working maps need place circles about 10 mm across and at least about 40 mm between places so a pawn and markers fit; small record maps are for pen only. Draw an optional place with a symbol unlike the circled places (for example a square) and define it once in the shared rules. When prices change, print the changed prices on a second copy of the same map in the same layout, so the original stays unchanged as a reference. Record a trip as its letters (such as ACDF) and pay after the trip, not with a running tally. Before first use, show each new convention (paying per trip, an optional place, opening links by price) on a small non-task map: start, one step, result. Unpiloted: the counters, markers and tray have never been handled in a session, in the base week or here.
