Week 50: Closer-looking paths, stubborn lengths

Mathematical kernels

1. Every monotone horizontal/vertical staircase joining opposite corners of a rectangle has total length width+height: horizontal pieces together span the width and vertical pieces together span the height. Subdividing into finer staircases near the diagonal does not reduce that length. In a 120×160 mm rectangle all such paths measure 280 mm, while the straight diagonal measures 200 mm (a scaled 3–4–5 triangle). The exact diagonal number can be supplied or measured; the short-versus-bent comparison does not require a square-root lesson.

2. Equal-step staircases can stay arbitrarily close to the diagonal as the number of steps grows, although their lengths stay fixed and strictly longer. This is an elementary counterexample to preservation of length under uniform/Hausdorff approximation of curves. The student endpoint can be a finite construction inside a narrower diagonal corridor plus a proof of the unchanged length. There is no final finite staircase that becomes the diagonal. Do not talk as if infinitely many little steps were a completed physical walk. A visual example must match horizontal pieces to one total-width strip and vertical pieces to one total-height strip; show only enough to clarify how length is recorded, leaving comparison and invariance for discovery.

Sources: John D. Norton, University of Pittsburgh, Badly Behaved Curves, the diagonal/inverted-V construction and failure of length continuity: https://sites.pitt.edu/~jdnorton/teaching/paradox/chapters/measure_curves/measure_curves.html. The monotone staircase proof and the scaled 3–4–5 example are independently derived. This differs from the produced polyomino perimeter problem: the target is the mismatch between closeness of shapes and closeness of lengths.

Suggested emphasis by level:

K–1: build contrasting corner-to-corner routes with strips and compare their lengths with a straight shortcut.
Grades 2–3: explain why rearranging horizontal/vertical pieces preserves total length despite a closer-looking shape.
Grades 4–5: construct paths within successively narrower corridors and explain why improved visual approximation does not promise improved length.

Materials

Per child: one 120×160 mm rectangle board with the same endpoints and diagonal on each overlay; prepared 1-, 2-, 4- and 8-step staircase overlays; two 300 mm nonstretch strings; a ruler; and reusable horizontal/vertical paper strips. Supply a printed 100 mm calibration segment and print all measurement boards at 100%. Exact proofs use the stated widths/heights and rearrangement, not millimeter-perfect string handling. Do not require children to cut or trace dozens of tiny steps; four or eight steps are enough for the concrete phenomenon. All routes must be monotone and use only horizontal/vertical segments when applying the invariant.
