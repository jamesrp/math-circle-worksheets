\PageTitle{FACILITATOR}{Start together}
\Subhead{Prepare the materials}
Use the small (1\(\times\)) pieces from the 21st Century and Upscale Pattern Blocks. Put larger pieces aside. Print US Letter, single-sided, at 100\% / Actual Size. The grid edge is one inch; compare a real small block with a printed cell. If needed, change \texttt{\textbackslash blockside} in \texttt{common.tex} and rebuild. A poor print fit should not become a mathematical obstacle.

For each K--1 child: 6 blues, 12 greens, 3 purples, and a few reds/yellows. For each middle child: 10 blues, 5 greens, 3 purples. For the upper child: 8 blues, a pencil, and optional string for ribbons. Reuse pieces between boards. For the current 3/3/1 roster, gather \textbf{56 blues, 51 greens, and 18 purples}. The optional cutout sheet supplies 12 paper blues. No pink pieces are needed.
\Subhead{Whole-group warmup before separate starting tasks}
After a little free handling, bring everyone together around middle Problem 1. Invite children to place blues on Board B. Show how a blue covers two whole triangle spaces, how to turn it, and how to keep pieces inside without overlaps. Then try Board A and notice the remaining gaps. Demonstrate the task and let children contribute pieces; leave the explanation open. An unfinished attempt is not yet evidence that every attempt will fail.

Once everyone has seen the first action, distribute a suitable starting page. This responds to the organizer's report that separate starts at two tables took too much simultaneous management. The revised warmup and packets are proposals to try, not yet tested changes.
\Subhead{Readiness and a flexible hour}
\textbf{K--1:} match boundaries and turn pieces; no reading, writing, or arithmetic prerequisite. \textbf{2--3:} count spaces through 25 with help; compare up/down orientations and best packings. \textbf{4--5:} record arrangements, follow a move rule, and reason about paths and odd/even numbers; small sums help with ribbons. Read any instruction aloud as needed.

\textbf{0--10:} handle materials and explore. \textbf{10--15:} common demonstration. \textbf{15--35:} investigate. \textbf{35--40:} movement/reset. \textbf{40--55:} continue. \textbf{55--60:} share and tidy.

Give one page at a time. Keep the supporting parent mainly with K--1; the organizer circulates and returns for real discussion with the upper child. Offer hints after attempts. A child can spend the hour on a few useful examples; these pages are not a completion checklist. Keep solutions out of view.

Count gaps as whole triangle spaces, not pieces.

\PageTitle{FACILITATOR}{Tilings, flips, and routes}
\Subhead{Upper Problems 1--4 / F01-U-v3}
First give only page 1: one full-size H122 board and eight smaller recording boards. Each blue is recorded by a line between the centers of its two triangle spaces. Keep the bold edge fixed. Let children find and compare tilings before introducing the flip or the six reference cards.

Page 2 shows both fillings of the small hexagon, then highlights the hexagon that changes between A and B. A flip changes exactly those three blues. The six cards are the vertices of the map; children draw connections directly between them. The complete edge list is \textbf{AB, BC, BD, CE, DE, EF}.

Every card can reach every other. Shortest A--F routes: \textbf{A--B--C--E--F} and \textbf{A--B--D--E--F}, each four flips. Save ``How do you know for sure?'' for conversation: A must leave through B, F must be entered through E, and B to E takes at least two moves.

For odd returns, color \{A,C,D,F\} one color and \{B,E\} another. Every flip switches color. Three, five, or any odd number of flips cannot return to A. Trying a few routes suggests the result; the two-color argument covers every route. The ribbon gives another proof on the next page.
\Subhead{Upper ribbon problems: tracing before counting}
Start at the middle of the bold bottom edge. Cross a rhombus to its opposite horizontal edge, then continue upward. Each step moves left or right. Let children trace with a finger or string before writing the letters. Ask where a second route could branch: each entered rhombus has just one opposite edge.

\PageTitle{FACILITATOR}{What ribbons explain}
\Subhead{One tiling, one ribbon; one ribbon, one tiling}
Following upward is deterministic. More strongly, trace any rhombus with horizontal edges downward: height strictly decreases until it reaches the sole bottom horizontal edge. Hence all such rhombi lie on this same ribbon. After the ribbon is fixed, every remaining rhombus has the third orientation. Each remaining triangle has at most one partner in that orientation, so the rest of the tiling is forced.

The six orders of two L's and two R's are realized by the six cards. One can list the positions of the R's: 12, 13, 14, 23, 24, 34. Thus there are \(\binom42=6\) tilings. Listing the choices is enough; combinations notation is optional.
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{c|cccccc}Card&A&B&C&D&E&F\\\hline Ribbon&RRLL&RLRL&LRRL&RLLR&LRLR&LLRR\\R-position sum&3&4&5&5&6&7\\Possible flips&1&3&2&2&3&1\end{tabular}\end{center}
\Subhead{Exactly the adjacent RL and LR pairs can flip}
At a turn, the two consecutive ribbon rhombi and a neighboring third-orientation rhombus fill a unit hexagon. Its other filling changes RL to LR or LR to RL. Straight pairs RR and LL cannot form this hexagon. Conversely every unit-hexagon flip contains the two horizontal-edge orientations, both in the unique ribbon, and changes an adjacent turn. Children can circle the changed pair in the codes and find the same three pieces in the pictures.
\Subhead{A short proof of no odd cycles}
Number the four step positions from bottom to top. Add the positions of the two R's: RRLL has sum \(1+2=3\); RLRL has sum \(1+3=4\). One flip moves exactly one R one position, changing the sum by \(1\) or \(-1\). Its parity switches every time. A return to the same tiling has the same ribbon and sum, so the number of flips must be even. This proves every closed walk is even without inspecting individual graph cycles.

This score also proves that A to F needs at least four flips, since the sum rises from 3 to 7 and one move changes it by only one. Offer this after the concrete ribbon work, not as a prerequisite for finding a route.
\Subhead{Hints and follow-ups}
If a child cannot start, offer only one tiling card to copy. If a flip is hard to see, outline a yellow-hexagon-sized patch. If a map line is uncertain, compare which three pieces change. If a ribbon is confusing, follow the opposite horizontal sides with string. Return to the blocks whenever the letters stop having a visible meaning.

A completed construction, a checked map, or one useful explanation can be a stopping point. Keep optional extension packets for another discussion; the organizer did not use them in the reported meeting. Record the actual cases tried and arguments explained, rather than assuming the whole packet was completed.

\Subhead{Scaling and random moves / reserves}
Scale both a small regular hexagon and its three blue pieces by two: its two tilings are unchanged in structure. Keep that larger outline but use 1\(\times\) blues: twelve pieces are needed and there are twenty tilings. Changing relative size changes the problem.

Choose uniformly among legal flips on the six-state graph. Long-run visit proportions are \((1,3,2,2,3,1)/12\); the mean first positive return to A is 12 moves. Choosing uniformly among four potential centers and staying when the chosen flip is unavailable gives uniform proportions and mean return 6 turns; a stay counts as a turn and an immediate return. These are distinct random rules. The separate extension guide develops them; no probability is needed in the core tasks.
