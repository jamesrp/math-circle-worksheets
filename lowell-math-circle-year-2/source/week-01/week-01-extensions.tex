\ExtensionTitle{CHALLENGE 3 / COUNTING}{How many best routes?}
\RuleBox{Use three R slips and three L slips. Go from \Word{RRRLLL} to \Word{LLLRRR} using the fewest moves. Routes are different if their sequence of words is different.}
\Task{1. Find two routes.}{Each L must cross each R. A shortest route makes each of these nine crossings once. Number the L's from left to right; their order never changes.}
\Task{2. Keep a smaller record.}{Record how many R's each L has crossed. For example, \((2,1,0)\) means the first L has crossed two R's, the second one, and the third none.}
\begin{center}\begin{minipage}{.23\linewidth}\centering\RowPicture\end{minipage}\hfill\begin{minipage}{.71\linewidth}Shade left-justified rows in a \(3\times3\) square. Row lengths must satisfy \(3\geq x_1\geq x_2\geq x_3\geq0\). Every move adds one square while keeping that rule.\par Start \((0,0,0)\); finish \((3,3,3)\).\end{minipage}\end{center}
\Task{3. Count without listing every route.}{Add the route counts of all legal previous records. For \((2,1,0)\), use \((1,1,0)\) and \((2,0,0)\): \(1+1=2\) routes. Work \textbf{down the left half}, then \textbf{down the right half}.}
\begin{center}\ExtensionRouteTableBlank\end{center}
\WriteBox{Why does adding the previous counts count each route exactly once?}{.5in}
\textbf{Go further:} Repeat with two R's and four L's. Is the number of shortest routes the same as the number of possible words?

\ExtensionTitle{CHALLENGE 4 / CHANCE}{Does random mean equally often?}
The six nodes below are the six tilings from the earlier activity. A line is one legal flip. Use a counter on the graph; you do not need to rebuild the blocks each turn.
\begin{center}\WalkGraph\end{center}
\Task{1. Choose a legal move at random.}{Start at A. At every turn choose each neighboring node with equal chance. Use the randomizer rule below. Predict which nodes you will visit most, then try \textbf{30 turns}. Tally your node \emph{after} each turn; do not tally the initial A.}
{\small One neighbor: move there. Two: odd/even on a die chooses the alphabetically first/second neighbor. Three: reroll 4--6, then 1, 2, 3 chooses the first, second, third neighbor. Rerolls do not count as turns.}
\begin{center}\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.65}\begin{tabular}{c|p{.65in}|p{.65in}|p{.65in}|p{.65in}|p{.65in}|p{.65in}}Node&A&B&C&D&E&F\\\hline Tally&&&&&&\end{tabular}\end{center}
\Task{2. Explain a possible imbalance.}{Write one unit at every node on scrap paper. In a \emph{sharing model}, split each node's amount equally among its neighbors, all at once. Does every node receive one? Can you find starting amounts that come back unchanged after sharing? Fractions are allowed.}
\WriteBox{Stable starting amounts, and how I checked them:}{.65in}
\Task{3. Change the random rule.}{Make four identical folded slips labeled 1--4. Draw one, replace it, and mix again. Take the line with that label if it touches your node. Otherwise \textbf{stay put and count the turn}. Could this rule give equal long-run visit frequencies? Explain using both directions along a line.}
\textbf{Go further:} Starting at A, count turns until your first return, then start a new trial. Compare the rules. Under rule 3 a stay at A on turn 1 counts as a return. What might the averages approach?
