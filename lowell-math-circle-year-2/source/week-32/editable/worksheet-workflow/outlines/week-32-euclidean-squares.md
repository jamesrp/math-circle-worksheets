Week 32: Squares inside rectangles

Mathematical kernels

1. Cutting away a square preserves common measuring units. Start with an axis-aligned rectangle whose side lengths are positive whole numbers a≥b. Remove a b×b square, leaving a rectangle with side lengths b and a−b; repeat, stopping when no rectangle remains. The positive sizes decrease, so the procedure terminates. Every integer d divides both a and b if and only if it divides b and a−b. Thus the common divisors stay exactly the same at every cut. The side length of the last square is gcd(a,b). Example: 10×6 → remove 6×6 → 6×4 → remove 4×4 → 4×2 → two 2×2 squares. The last square has side 2, not 1. For 8×5, the successive square sizes are 5,3,2,1,1.

2. The last square solves a largest-equal-tile problem. An a×b whole-grid rectangle can be tiled by identical axis-aligned whole-grid squares of side d exactly when d divides both a and b; hence the greatest possible such d is the Euclidean algorithm's output. Smaller or larger greedy cuts are not arbitrary variants: the largest square at a stage fits across the current shorter side and reduces the same measuring-unit question to a smaller rectangle. The full claim is about identical axis-aligned whole-grid squares. Do not confuse it with minimum-number tilings using unequal squares, arbitrary rotated square packings, or a theorem that every rectangle ends with unit squares. The explanation can be carried by physical repeated-unit edges and reversible cutting rather than long-division notation.

Sources: Euclid, Elements VII.2, e.g. Henry Mendell translation https://web.calstatela.edu/faculty/hmendel/Ancient%20Mathematics/17th.%20Cent./Euclid/Euclid%20VII/Euclid.7.2/Euclid.7.2.html ; NRICH, “Euclid's Algorithm I,” https://nrich.maths.org/articles/euclids-algorithm-i , for square removal as the Euclidean algorithm. The original Week 4/9 guides contain subtraction-gcd as an adult proof scaffold, but do not center a square-removal/equal-square-tiling investigation. This family is a new concrete experience connected to that prior mathematics, not a claim that gcd itself has never appeared.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 fits prepared largest-square pieces and tracks the changing rectangle; grades 2–3 compare starting rectangles and the final unit that measures both edges; grades 4–5 explain invariance of common divisors and why the final square is the largest equal tile. Young children need matching and counting, while the general statement needs factors and repeated subtraction.

Materials

Per working pair or trio: at least three duplicate gridded card rectangles for each chosen instance, reusable square pieces, ruler, colored pencils, adult scissors, and removable tape. Use 1 cm unit squares and include rectangles with both coprime and noncoprime sides, no longer than 15 cm in either direction. Prepared square pieces must be truly square and match the printed unit grid. Provide enough pieces to complete each chosen rectangle; if using cover pieces instead of cutting, boundaries of the remaining rectangle must stay visible. Each child should be able to manipulate a rectangle rather than watch an adult execute the entire algorithm. Diagrams must show an input rectangle, one removed square, and the resulting labeled rectangle before using a compact arrow record. Do not supply an entire worked solution of the first main investigation; a tiny different instance can establish the action. Print working grids at 100% scale if loose square pieces are used.
