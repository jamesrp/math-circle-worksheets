Week 30: Two-pan weight kits

Mathematical kernels

1. A small kit can represent signed differences without negative-number notation. Each known weight exists once and may go on either side of an equal-arm balance or stay off. Keep the target object on a fixed side. Weights 1 and 3 represent all target masses 1–4; weights 1,3,9 represent all 1–13. For instance, target 5 with weights 3 and 1 beside it balances weight 9; target 7 with weight 3 beside it balances 9+1. Each arrangement is an equality of two nonnegative totals. This is not the counterfeit-coin search problem and has no unknown heavy/light coin.

2. Balanced ternary exists uniquely and is optimal. With m weights 1,3,9,…,3^(m−1), every integer from −(3^m−1)/2 to +(3^m−1)/2 is a unique signed difference. If the smaller kit covers −S,…,S uniquely, adding weight 2S+1 gives three disjoint consecutive intervals: [−3S−1,−S−1], [−S,S], [S+1,3S+1]. These come from putting the new weight on the two different sides or leaving it off. This proves coverage and uniqueness by induction. Any m weights have only 3^m placement choices; swapping left and right pairs positive with negative differences, while the all-off arrangement gives zero. Hence no kit represents more than (3^m−1)/2 distinct positive masses, and powers of three attain the bound with no gaps. With 1,3 already chosen, adding 10 misses 5, whereas adding 8 yields an overlap at 4 and reaches only 12. The children's task should preserve discovery of the next weight rather than announce the powers-of-three answer.

Sources: Ethan Bolker, Samuel Feuer and Catalin Zara, “Balance weighing — variations on a theme,” Mathematics Magazine 94(5), 2021, author-hosted full paper https://www.cs.umb.edu/~eb/weighing.pdf . The paper begins with elementary classroom investigations, including fourth graders discovering the two-pan sequence; the full theorem and later research are distinguished there. NRICH “Balance Power,” https://nrich.maths.org/problems/balance-power , offers supplementary pedagogical context.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 balances small unit totals using weights on either pan and compares two-weight kits; grades 2–3 investigate how much a three-weight kit can cover without gaps; grades 4–5 explain coverage and uniqueness with translated ranges and establish a three-placement counting bound. Formal signed numerals are unnecessary; the general optimality bound needs systematic counting.

Materials

Per working pair or trio: one equal-arm balance and one each of known mass bundles 1,2,3,4,8,9,10 unit masses, with printed numeral and dot-count labels, plus target bags/cups containing 1–13 units as needed. Identical linking cubes or calibrated equal counters can be sealed into bundles; do not assume equal-looking household objects have equal mass. Keep unselected weights off the table. There must be only one copy of each selected weight; duplicated weights change the mathematics. A paper two-pan mat with the actual unit cubes/bundles may substitute for a balance, using direct counting of both totals. Make the mat large enough for a target and two added bundles on the same pan. The student pages must show clearly that a weight may join the object: a small input/process/output example can balance target 3 plus weight 1 against weight 4 without revealing the optimal kit. Avoid balance-beam lever positions: distance from a fulcrum is not part of this equal-pan model.
