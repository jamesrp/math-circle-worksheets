Mathematical kernels

1. Distinguishable histories force separate memory states. A deterministic machine reads one symbol at a time from {red, blue}; it starts in one designated state, has exactly one outgoing transition for each symbol at every state, and answers yes/no according to its state when the input ends. Its current state is its entire memory. If histories x and y end in the same state, then every common continuation z ends in the same state from both: apply the same transition successively. Thus if some continuation must make one history a yes and the other a no, x and y cannot share a state. A collection of k pairwise distinguishable histories proves that every correct machine needs at least k states. The continuation may be empty. Different pairs may require different continuations. This is a universal lower-bound certificate, stronger than showing that one attempted drawing fails.

2. Exact small memory requirements. To accept exactly when the number of red symbols is a multiple of m, including zero, use m states in a red cycle, with blue loops, and mark only the starting state yes. This works because the marker tracks the remainder. At least m states are necessary: histories containing 0, 1, ..., m−1 reds are pairwise distinguishable; for histories with i and j reds, append enough reds to make i a multiple of m, and j will not be. In the m=3 case the histories empty, R, RR are separated using RR, R, or an empty continuation as appropriate; the exact minimum is three. RBR is no, RBRBR is yes, and any all-blue input is yes. A contrasting three-state machine accepts precisely strings ending RB: states mean “no useful suffix,” “last symbol R,” and “last two symbols RB”; red always leads to the second state, while blue leads respectively to the first, third, and first. The histories empty, R, RB require distinct states: B separates the first two, and stopping separates RB from either. These are complete finite minimality results. No undecidability, pumping lemma, infinite-language classification, or product construction is needed.

Sources: Luca Trevisan, Stanford CS103, “Notes on the Myhill–Nerode Theorem,” Lecture 15, May 12, 2014, §§1–2, especially Lemmas 2 and 4 (distinguishable strings must reach different states): https://theory.stanford.edu/~trevisan/cs103-14/lecture15.pdf . Suggested emphasis by level

- K–1: operate and invent very small symbol machines after an oral demonstration; follow arrows and match symbols, with no reading or remainder vocabulary required.
- Grades 2–3: design reliable machines and find continuations that expose lost memory; count small cycles and keep restart versus continuation distinct.
- Grades 4–5: pair a construction with a proof that no smaller machine can work; quantify over unseen inputs without needing algebra or formal-language notation.

Materials

For each of the three tables: twelve blank state discs about 4 cm across, two marker tokens, twelve red cards and twelve blue cards about 3 cm square, pencils, erasers, blank US Letter paper, and one whiteboard with erasable pens. Print symbols as well as colors so monochrome copies and color-vision differences do not change the rules. State diagrams need room for labels and both outgoing arrows, including loops. A machine uses one marker; the spare is for comparing two runs, not extra memory in a single run. Keep consumed input cards face down or out of the operator's sight. A reader may supply the next card, but cannot signal earlier cards or counts. Only the current state may influence the next move; marker orientation, a remembered count, unused counters, and the layout of discarded cards must not become extra states. The stop signal requests the state's fixed yes/no answer and does not carry additional information. Adults can draw arrows and read rules for beginning readers. No scissors, electronics, or special purchased kit is required.
