Week 34: A pattern that cannot hide a turn

Mathematical kernels

1. Color the vertices of a regular n-gon using named colors. A surviving symmetry is a rotation or reflection that carries every vertex to the same-colored vertex; the identity is excluded when asking whether any symmetry survives. The least number of colors that destroys all nonidentity symmetries is 3 for n=3,4,5 and 2 for n>=6. This is the distinguishing number of the cycle. Do not confuse minimizing colors with minimizing marked positions, or with proper coloring: neighbors may match. For the small lower bound, in any binary coloring of 3,4,5 positions the minority color occupies at most two positions. Zero, one, or two marked positions always admit a reflection of that regular polygon. Three colors suffice: give adjacent vertices two distinct unique colors and every other vertex the third color. A symmetry must fix both adjacent vertices and therefore is the identity.

2. For n>=6, mark positions 0,1,3 with one color and all remaining positions with another. The cyclic gaps between the three marked positions are 1,2,n-3, all distinct. No nonidentity rotation preserves three distinct cyclic gaps, and a reflection of three marked positions would fix one gap and interchange the other two, requiring equality. Thus two colors suffice; one plainly cannot. In a six-ring the cyclic word 110100 is a witness. These facts are the adult overview, not conclusions to print before children investigate. A child can instead find and defend one asymmetric six-ring. Exhaustive verification for n=3 through 10 is recorded in /workspace/shared/new-domain-expansion/symmetry-topology-checks.json; it supplements the proof.

Sources: Albertson and Collins, Symmetry Breaking in Graphs (1996), https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/view/v3i1r18 . This is an original research source for distinguishing colorings. The small cycle proof above is supplied independently and completely.

Suggested emphasis by level:
K–1: Build patterns and let a partner try to hide a nontrivial turn or flip; recognize a surviving symmetry physically, with oral rules and matching symbols.
Grades 2–3: Seek the fewest colors on three-, four-, five-, and six-position rings; defend a construction by overlay, without large catalogs.
Grades 4–5: Explain the small-ring impossibility and the three-unequal-gaps construction; completeness comes from geometry, not trying every binary word.

Materials

Per child: 18 counters at least 20 mm wide, six in each of three colors/shapes; regular 3-,4-,5-,6-position mats with circles at least 28 mm wide; one tracing-paper or transparent duplicate of each mat. Per table: one eight-position extension mat and two washable markers. Mats should be at least 140 mm in diameter, printed at actual size. Color names stay fixed. Labels denote colors/categories only: the direction in which a printed letter or icon points is not extra data or an extra symmetry-breaking mark. Counters must be replaceable without threading beads. A removable home tick may orient a demonstration but must be removed when testing the actual pattern: a permanent tick would destroy the intended symmetries. Use the overlay to enact rotations and reflections, rather than physically losing counters by flipping the board. Give a small labeled input/turned-output example of a legal comparison on a non-target pattern; show a match and a mismatch without giving the winning six-ring. Adult reads and scribes. No necklaces around bodies, small beads, or mirrors are needed.
