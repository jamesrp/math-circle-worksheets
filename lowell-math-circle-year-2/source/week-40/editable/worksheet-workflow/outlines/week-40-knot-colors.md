Week 40: Can colors tell two loops apart?

Mathematical kernels

1. A mathematical knot is a closed non-self-intersecting loop in three-dimensional space, considered up to ambient deformation without cutting or passing strands through one another. Its diagram needs an explicit over/under choice at every crossing. An arc continues through overcrossings and ends at undercrossings. A Fox three-coloring gives each arc one of three colors so that at each crossing the over-arc and two under-arcs are all the same or all different. A nonconstant coloring is required. The standard trefoil has nine total colorings, six nonconstant; the crossing-free unknot has only its three constant colorings. Having no nonconstant coloring does NOT prove a knot is unknotted: the figure-eight knot is a counterexample. Physical attempts to untie something are experiments, not impossibility proofs.

2. Reidemeister moves give bijections on the sets of three-colorings, preserving the constant ones. If an under-arc of color a passes under color b, its new color is a*b=2b-a modulo three. The three local identities a*a=a, (a*b)*b=a, and (a*b)*c=(a*c)*(b*c) verify the three move types. Small color-case diagrams can establish these identities without modular notation. Combined with Reidemeister's theorem that equivalent tame-knot diagrams are related by these moves, different numbers of colorings obstruct equivalence. The general Reidemeister theorem is a supplied mathematical fact, not something the child has proved by checking local pictures. Main goal: move from a physical knot to a trustworthy invariant, with local checks. Avoid making a catalog of many knots the main activity.

Suggested emphasis by level:
K–1: Optional after the child reliably distinguishes overpasses from underpasses and traces one full arc; manipulate adult-prepared loops and color a clear small diagram, with no expectation of the global proof.
Grades 2–3: Trace and color verified diagrams, then investigate one or two local deformations preserving colorability.
Grades 4–5: Connect local move checks to the trefoil/unknot obstruction and explain the test's one-way limit; modulo-three algebra is optional.

Materials

Per table: two short thick flexible cords already closed securely into a simple loop and a trefoil (roughly 400–600 mm total cord length each), plus two visually complicated unknots; a tray; three marker colors paired with letters/patterns. Per child: original knot diagrams at least 140 mm wide, with crossings separated by at least 25 mm, plus tracing paper. All cords remain on the table; never wear loops, tie children together, or use electrical cords, tight knots, exposed wire, or a human-knot activity. Adults assemble and check models. Do not ask children to cut the cords.

Before the main task give a small explicit over/under example and trace a single labeled arc through an overpass until its next undercrossing. Separately show valid and invalid isolated crossing colors, without precoloring the target trefoil. Every printed crossing must be independently visually checked; a single wrong crossing can turn a claimed trefoil into an unknot. The adult must physically pretest the intended cord models; that pretest is NOT performed here. Diagram verification and algebraic checks do not substitute for a model check.
