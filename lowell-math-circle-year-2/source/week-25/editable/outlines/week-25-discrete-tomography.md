Week 25: When do row and column shadows determine a picture?

Unscheduled library slot, unpiloted. The objects are binary pictures and their line counts, distinct from nonogram run clues, atlas AP-27's hidden tank parameters, and the existing coding/query activities. The writer chooses the student problems; theorems and exact examples below are mathematical support.

Mathematical kernels

1. A complete ambiguity and uniqueness test. A picture is an r-by-c grid with each cell either empty or occupied once. Its shadows are the count in each labeled row and each labeled column, retaining their order. Select any two different rows and any two different columns. If their four corner cells contain exactly two counters on one diagonal, move them to the other diagonal. This rectangle switch preserves all shadows; the rows and columns need not be adjacent. In fact, a picture is uniquely determined by its row and column counts exactly when it has no such switch. Elementary proof of the converse: no switch means the occupied-column sets of every two rows are nested by inclusion, since incomparable row sets give the two opposite corners of a switch. Put rows in decreasing order of their counts, keeping labels attached. The largest row must occupy every column with a nonzero count, because the row sets are nested. Any alternative picture with the same margins must do the same: the largest row's required count equals the number of nonzero columns, and zero columns cannot be used. Remove that now-forced row, reduce each affected column count by one, and repeat. All rows are forced, so the picture is unique. For comparison, 110/100/000 is unique with row and column counts (2,1,0), whereas row and column counts (2,1,1) on a 3-by-3 board admit exactly five pictures: 011/100/100, 101/010/100, 101/100/010, 110/001/100, 110/100/001. Slashes separate rows. This is an exact information-loss theorem, not only a collection of reconstruction puzzles.

2. All two-row solutions communicate, with an exact move distance. On a two-row board, a column total of zero forces two empty cells, and a total of two forces two occupied cells. Each column totaling one has its counter either above or below. If there are s such free columns and exactly k must be occupied above, every k-element selection of those s columns is a solution. A switch exchanges one top-only column and one bottom-only column. To transform a starting solution into a target, pair each unwanted top column with a missing top column and switch them. This takes exactly d switches, where d is the number of top columns occupied in the start but not the target. No faster transformation is possible because a switch corrects at most one of those unwanted top columns. For four columns all totaling one and top row total two there are six pictures, and any two are at most two switches apart. On arbitrary-height boards Ryser's interchange theorem still gives connectivity, but the elementary two-row proof and its exact distance do not prove that general result.

Suggested emphasis by level

- K–1: make genuinely different small pictures with the same row and column counts after an oral shadow-count demonstration; matching totals can already disprove unique reconstruction.
- Grades 2–3: find when shadows force a picture and when a rectangle switch preserves them, keeping row and column labels fixed.
- Grades 4–5: establish the nested-row uniqueness criterion or the complete two-row connectivity and shortest-switch theorem; arithmetic is only small counting.

Materials

For each of three tables: two reusable grids with at most 4 rows and 6 columns, twelve identical counters no wider than 18 mm, row and column count cards 0–6, two pencils, an eraser, and blank paper. Working cells should be at least 22 mm square; a 4-by-6 board then fits comfortably on US Letter. When comparing two pictures, provide one board for each rather than requiring the child to remember an erased state. A cell holds at most one counter. Empty rows/columns, if present, remain part of the board and keep their labels. Counters cannot be stacked or placed on grid intersections. Shadows mean counts along each entire line, not contiguous run lengths, visible outlines, or binary light blockage. Switches may span any chosen pair of rows and columns; intervening cells are untouched. Rotations, reflections, and row/column permutations do not identify pictures unless a different equivalence rule is expressly requested. Avoid preprinting a complete switch graph or nested ordering as an obligatory student scaffold.
