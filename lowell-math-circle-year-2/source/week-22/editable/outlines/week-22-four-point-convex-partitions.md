Week 22: Can four points always be split so their regions meet?

Library slot, not a scheduled meeting. Rebuild of atlas lead GA-22, unpiloted. The payoff is a complete planar convexity theorem and its sharp threshold. The old atlas's prescribed case split and fractional coordinate calculations are not a student sequence. The writer chooses fresh configurations and problems; statements, cases, and examples below are mathematical answer information.

Mathematical kernels

1. Four points force an intersecting partition. The convex hull of a group is its filled stretched-band region, including the boundary. A singleton's hull is that point; a two-point hull is the closed segment between them. Given any four labeled points in the plane, assign every label to exactly one of two nonempty groups. There is always an assignment for which the two hulls share at least one point. Here is a complete elementary proof. If two labels occupy the same location, put one of them alone and the other with the remaining labels. If the four locations are distinct and three are collinear, isolate the middle one of those three; its point lies in the segment between the other two, regardless of where the fourth lies. This includes all-four-collinear configurations. Otherwise there are no three collinear. The boundary of the four-point convex hull is either a triangle, with the remaining point inside it, or a convex quadrilateral. In the triangle case isolate the inside point; in the quadrilateral case pair opposite corners, whose two diagonals cross. Thus all arrangements are covered. Contact along a boundary is enough; positive-area overlap is not required. This is the dimension-two case of Radon's theorem, not merely a successful puzzle on a specially chosen board.

2. The threshold is sharp, while individual answers depend on geometry. Three distinct noncollinear points cannot be split successfully: every nonempty two-group partition puts a single vertex opposite the segment joining the other two, and the vertex is not on that segment. Therefore four is the smallest number guaranteeing success for every planar arrangement. Small checked four-point instances: a rectangle with cyclic vertices A=(0,0), B=(6,0), C=(6,4), D=(0,4) has successful partition {A,C}|{B,D}, meeting at (3,2). For A=(0,0), B=(6,0), C=(0,6), D=(2,1), the successful split is {D}|{A,B,C}; D is inside the triangle (its barycentric weights are 1/2, 1/3, 1/6, as an adult exact check). For A=(0,0), B=(2,0), C=(5,0), D=(1,4), {B}|{A,C,D} works by boundary contact. If four points are collinear in order A,B,C,D, exactly four of the seven unordered two-group partitions work: {B}|{A,C,D}, {C}|{A,B,D}, {A,C}|{B,D}, and {A,D}|{B,C}. The theorem asserts existence, not a unique split or one split that works for every arrangement. Coordinates, fractions, a seven-case catalog, and higher-dimensional linear dependence are not prerequisites for the geometric result.

Suggested emphasis by level

- K–1: make two filled regions from four marked points and find an actual shared point after an oral demonstration, including a point or segment as a legal region.
- Grades 2–3: challenge each other with contrasting arrangements and find a partition that defeats each attempted counterexample; no arithmetic beyond counting four is needed.
- Grades 4–5: organize an exhaustive planar argument, account for collinearity and boundary contact, and show why three points do not carry the same guarantee.

Materials

For each of three tables: eight movable labeled dot markers (two separate sets A–D), four US Letter tracing-paper sheets, two colored pencils with distinct line patterns available for monochrome use, one ruler, an eraser, ten blank sheets, and removable tape. Draw point centers clearly and leave at least 12 × 12 cm for a working configuration. Use translucent shading or hatching to display the entire convex hull, not just a triangular outline: a point strictly inside one triangle must visibly count as belonging to it. A straightedge and tracing-paper overlays are enough; physical geoboards, rubber bands, and pins are optional substitutes, not prerequisites. If rubber bands are used, adults must explain the filled region and must not let a singleton become a tiny circular disk or two pegs become an area-bearing loop. The ideal mathematical points have no thickness, and a thick drawn stroke must not manufacture an intersection. Every label is used once; groups are nonempty; coincident labels, if deliberately included, must both remain identifiable. Do not restrict point placement to a grid unless that narrower problem is intended, and do not pre-color the groups or supply the proof-case categories on every student board.
