# Week 23 final answers and assumptions

Three investigations: partial selection (1–2), merging two already sorted pairs (3–4), preserving equal-card identities (5–6). Entry uses fixed two-endpoint compare-exchange bars and ordered lanes; adult reading is fine. Minimum guarantees and stability explanations are later readiness gates. Printed networks are compact records; children operate the separate24×30 cm mat with30 mm cards. Physical swaps and tie handling are untested. No base full-sorter search, binary certification or parallel-stage optimization is counted as new work.

1. Minimum three bars. A simple selector is (1,2),(1,3),(1,4). The value in lane1 after each bar is the minimum of all values it has encountered, so it finishes as the global minimum. Until a distinct candidate loses a comparison, it can still be the minimum; each bar eliminates at most one of four distinct candidates, so at least three are needed. An independent fixed-network lower-bound certificate: with at most two bars the graph of four lanes and comparator edges is disconnected. Put the smallest card in a component not containing lane 1; it cannot ever reach lane 1. Other lanes need not be ordered; input1,4,3,2 stays unsorted after this selector.
2. The drawn network is (1,2),(3,4),(1,4),(2,3). After the first pair bars write a≤b in lanes1,2 and c≤d in lanes3,4. Final output is min(a,d),min(b,c),max(b,c),max(a,d). Each of the first two values is at most each of the last two: direct comparator inequalities give two of the comparisons; min(a,d)≤a≤b≤max(b,c), and min(b,c)≤c≤d≤max(a,d), give the others. Thus it puts the lower half first, including ties. Input3,4,1,2 produces2,1,4,3, an unsorted output. No minimum-four-bars claim is asked.
3. Minimum three bars; (1,3),(2,4),(2,3) works. Initially each pair is sorted, so the first comparison places the global minimum in lane1; the second places the global maximum in lane4; the third orders the middle values. All six distinct legal inputs listed on the page finish1,2,3,4. Two bars give at most four swap/no-swap records. A given record determines a permutation of inputs to outputs, so with target output1,2,3,4 it can sort at most one distinct input. Six legal inputs require at least three bars. The correctness argument also covers ties; the lower bound uses distinct values only.

Thus some invalid-pair input must fail for any successful three-bar merger. An input failing this construction is supplied by writer-check.json as well.

X outputs in page row order: (1C,2B,2A), (1C,2A,2B), (1C,2A,2B), (1C,2B,2A), (1C,2A,2B), (1C,2B,2A). Y outputs: (1C,2A,2B), (1C,2A,2B), (1C,2B,2A), (1C,2B,2A), (1C,2A,2B), (1C,2B,2A). X reverses the equal cards in the first and third rows only. Tags are secondary identifiers, never comparison keys, and equal compared values never swap.
6. No. To reverse order in a sequence, two cards must cross each other. An adjacent swap changes only the relative order of the two cards being swapped. Equal cards cannot directly swap because ties stay put. Hence any neighbor-only machine preserves their original relative order, whether or not its values finish fully sorted. A jumping bar can cross an equal card indirectly, as X does.
