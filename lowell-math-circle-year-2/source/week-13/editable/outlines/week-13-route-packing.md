Week 13: Route packing and bottlenecks (draft library slot, not a scheduled meeting)

Mathematical kernels

1. A best packing comes with a matching obstruction. In a finite directed network, fix one start s and one finish t; every edge has capacity one. A route follows arrows from s to t without revisiting a vertex. Several routes may meet at vertices but must not share an edge. The largest number of such edge-disjoint routes equals the fewest edges whose removal destroys every directed s-to-t route. Any set of closing edges gives an upper bound: each route must use at least one of them, and edge-disjoint routes use different ones. Thus k actual routes together with k closing edges that really block all travel certify an optimum, without listing all possible routes. The equality is the unit-capacity form of max-flow/min-cut; its existence proof uses integral flows and rerouting. A cut is a partition into a start side containing s and a finish side containing t; only arrows from the start side to the finish side count. A bottleneck can occur far from either endpoint. For example, on vertices s,a,b,c,d,e,f,t with arrows s→a,s→b,s→c,a→c,b→c,c→d,c→e,d→f,e→f,d→t,e→t,f→t, two routes s→a→c→d→t and s→b→c→e→t attain the two-edge cut {c→d,c→e}, although s has three outgoing arrows and t has three incoming arrows. This is a checked reference instance, not a required student problem.

2. Undoing part of a choice can make room for more. Greedily reserving one complete route after another can stop before the optimum. In the four-vertex network with arrows s→a,s→b,a→b,a→t,b→t, choosing s→a→b→t blocks every additional disjoint route, yet s→a→t and s→b→t fit together. In a residual network an unused arrow can be traversed forward and a used arrow backward, the latter meaning cancel its reservation. The residual route s→b→a→t cancels a→b and produces those two legal forward routes. It does not permit final routes to disobey arrows. More generally, start from zero flow and make these one-unit improvements. Values are integers and bounded by the number of edges leaving s, so the process terminates. At termination let S be all vertices reachable from s by residual steps. Since t is outside S, every original edge leaving S is used and every original edge entering S is unused. Conservation at internal vertices makes the flow value exactly the number of edges leaving S: a matching cut certificate. Removing directed flow cycles and tracing units from s to t produces edge-disjoint routes. This proves existence of the equal packing and obstruction, not just a collection of examples. No conclusion about multiple specified start–finish pairs or vertex-disjoint routes follows from this theorem.

Sources: Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, Algorithms, 4th edition, §6.4 lecture “Maximum Flow,” slides 20–26 (canceling backward flow), 28–37 (flow/cut bounds and theorem), 40 (integrality), https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall15/cos226/lectures/64MaxFlow.pdf; their official §6.4 page, exercise 2 and exercises 5–6, https://algs4.cs.princeton.edu/64maxflow/ (edge-disjoint connectivity and the different vertex-disjoint formulation). Suggested emphasis by level:
K–1: packing a few visible arrow-following routes and blocking all travel on tiny boards; spoken rules, tracing, and small counts, without symbolic flow labels.
Grades 2–3: matching a largest route collection with a smallest closing-edge set, including a shared interior meeting point; small counts and an all-routes obstruction argument.
Grades 4–5: rerouting a locally stuck packing and explaining a matching cut certificate; tracking used/unused arrows and conservation, with the general residual proof an optional adult-supported destination.

Materials

Preparation specification for ten children: a US Letter network board per child; pencils/erasers and three visibly different colored pencils per child or working pair; twelve small 15–20 mm paper closure markers per child (120 total). Colored routes may instead be distinguished by different line patterns or route labels so color is not the only cue. Provide spare paper or existing whiteboards for changing a route collection without redrawing a cramped network. Arrows must remain legible beside colored routes, and each edge must have an unobstructed midsection long enough for one closure marker. A marked junction is a vertex and may be shared; an accidental geometric crossing is not a junction unless a dot is printed there. Avoid diagram crossings where possible. Edge lengths do not measure time or capacity. Reserve edges for the entire simultaneous collection; sending counters down the same edge one after another would model a different problem. Closing means removing an edge, never removing its endpoint. All printed edges have unit capacity unless a later, separately stated mathematical variation changes it. Print single-sided US Letter at 100% scale. These counts are a proposed paper-and-pencil setup; no new road toys, apps, or measured stock of manipulatives is assumed.
