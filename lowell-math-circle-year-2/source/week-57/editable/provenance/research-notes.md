## Mathematical destination and limits

**Pick's theorem:** A=I+B/2-1 for a nondegenerate simple polygon whose vertices lie on a unit square lattice. Sides must be straight and noncrossing; the filled interior is connected and has no holes. I counts lattice points strictly inside. B counts **all** lattice points on the boundary, including collinear dots in the middle of a side, counting each only once. B is neither corner count nor Euclidean perimeter. Slanted sides and inward corners are allowed. Areas use one grid square as 1 and are positive multiples of 1/2. A smaller physical grid does not change the unit unless explicitly declared.

Children should experience exact area as determined by two dot counts, see that shape and number of vertices alone do not determine it, then seek a seam/additivity explanation. This is exact area, not an inside/outside cover approximation. Geometric area checks must be independent of the proposed formula: duplicate-and-rearrange, enclosing rectangle subtraction, or checked triangle decomposition. Counting several cases motivates a conjecture; the following argument proves the theorem. It need not be every child's first-visit destination.

For a connected region with h holes having pairwise disjoint simple lattice boundary loops, A=I+B/2-1+h. All hole-boundary dots count in B; hole-interior dots count nowhere in I. This is optional upper return work after substantial simple-polygon work. Curves, off-lattice vertices, crossings, point-touching/pinched boundaries and overlapping pieces are outside these claims. For c mutually disjoint components, with disjoint boundaries and h total holes, the correction is -c+h; touching at dots needs a different bookkeeping analysis and is not included.

## Proof without assuming the empty-triangle theorem

Let Q(P)=I+B/2-1. If P1 and P2 meet only in a full common side with k lattice points, including its two endpoints, and their union P is simple, then its seam's k-2 nonendpoint dots become interior while the endpoints remain boundary. Thus I=I1+I2+k-2 and B=B1+B2-2k+2. Substitute to obtain Q(P)=Q(P1)+Q(P2). Ordinary area is additive under exactly this join. The shared side may contain intermediate dots and the formula includes them.

An a-by-b axis-parallel lattice rectangle has I=(a-1)(b-1), B=2a+2b, giving Q=ab. A diagonal splits it into two triangles related by a half-turn, so they have equal area and equal I/B. Additivity proves the theorem for both axis-parallel right triangles. A lattice triangle with a horizontal or vertical side can then be joined to at most two such right triangles to make its bounding rectangle. Use successive full-side joins; omit any zero-area piece. This works even when the third vertex projects beyond the base.

An arbitrary lattice triangle needs another step: its bounding-rectangle complement need not consist of three right triangles. If it has no axis-parallel side, label its leftmost and rightmost vertices A and C, and its middle-x vertex B. The lattice points D1=(Bx,Ay) and D2=(Bx,Cy) lie on opposite sides of AC. Choose D opposite B. The quadrilateral A-B-C-D is convex: AC and the vertical BD intersect in their interiors. Cutting along BD gives triangles ABD and BCD, each with a vertical side. Cutting along AC gives the original ABC and triangle ACD, which has a horizontal side. The other three triangles are already proved, and two legal full-side joins give Q(ABC)=area(ABC) by subtraction. If any coordinates share an x or y, an axis-parallel side reduces directly to the preceding case.

For a simple polygon, remove redundant collinear vertices from its triangulation description without removing their dots from B. A nondegenerate simple polygon has an ear; its diagonal has lattice endpoints. Remove ears and reattach each along its full diagonal, so every union is again simple and the additivity hypothesis applies. This proves the polygon theorem without circularly assuming “empty triangles have area 1/2.” The general triangle bridge and legal-join justification were independently repaired after prompt generation; generated stage prompts remain historical inputs. The fresh math review checked this construction on all 2,148 noncollinear triples in a 5-by-5 lattice, alongside the complete geometric argument. Those finite checks support the construction but do not replace its proof.

**Correction example.** Triangle (0,0),(2,1),(6,6) has I=0, B=8 and area 3. Its bounding-rectangle complement includes a quadrilateral, so the earlier “at most three right triangles” shortcut was false. Taking D=(2,6) gives comparison triangles of areas 5, 10 and 12; the two legal decompositions yield 5+10=3+12. This is an adult proof correction, not a new student task or an edit to the older Week 31 material.

For holes, start with the outer polygon and subtract each hole polygon's area. If a hole has I_h strictly inside and B_h on its boundary, those I_h+B_h dots were inside the outer filled polygon but are removed from the region's interior; B_h is added to region boundary. Substitution gives +1 per hole. Hole boundaries must not meet other boundaries. This is subtraction of filled polygons, not a claim that holes are negative-area participants in a child's count.

## Pedagogy and prerequisites

**Source observations.** *Math Circle by the Bay*, printed p.129, explicitly says the authors did not expect children to find the formula; its purpose was to reveal how nontrivial it is. Printed p.130 records an attractive vertex/side weighting rule that fails on a triangle. P.131 says long expressions were not written in class and calculations were broken into parts because many children did not know operation order. P.134 states all tested formulas remain conjectures from the children's work and proof was beyond their students' readiness. These are actual elementary-circle experiences; they argue against making autonomous formula discovery or formal proof a universal requirement. The same preface, printed pp.viii-x, supports manipulatives, deeper destinations, varied pacing and recurring explanation.

Rozhkovskaya's Lesson 3 “At the lesson” item 1 (`OEBPS/part0013.xhtml`) was read locally: children first attempted a logic problem before a teacher table made reasoning visible. Lesson 8 “At the lesson” item 2 (`part0018.xhtml`) reports large differences in time spent writing/coloring tables and reduced copying for a later table. These support acting before tabulation and one recoverable shape/record, not multiple worksheets of transcription.

Neither establishes prerequisite knowledge of half-square area or Pick. README/context's report that hands-on tiles worked but paper lamp rules failed is the direct local teaching observation. Our dot marks, visible perimeter and duplicated cut shapes externalize the state.
