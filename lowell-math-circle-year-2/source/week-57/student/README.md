## Mathematical facts being used

For a nondegenerate simple lattice polygon with a single noncrossing straight boundary loop and no hole, `A = I + B/2 - 1`. Boundary means all dots on sides, not just vertices and not length. Congruent unit squares define area independently. Counting examples gives evidence, not a proof.

Write `Q=I+B/2-1`. Two filled simple lattice polygons must intersect exactly in one nonzero full common side, with disjoint interiors and no other boundary intersection; their union must again be a simple polygon. If the common side contains `k` distinct lattice dots, including its endpoints, then `I = I1 + I2 + k - 2` and `B = B1 + B2 - 2k + 2`. Its two endpoints remain on the outside boundary: otherwise the pieces would partition a whole neighborhood there and have additional local shared boundary, violating the intersection assumptions. The other `k-2` seam dots become interior. Substitution gives `Q=Q1+Q2`. Ordinary geometric area also adds. This includes `k=2`, when there is no new interior dot. Point-only contacts, several shared sides, closed seams, overlapping pieces, pinches and holes need different bookkeeping.

The following complete argument is the destination of Problem 8. Experiments support it; the universal geometric reasoning proves it.

1. **Axis rectangles.** An `a`-by-`b` rectangle for positive integers `a,b` has `I=(a-1)(b-1)` and `B=2a+2b`, hence `Q=ab`, its independent area. This includes width or height 1.
2. **Axis right triangles.** A rectangle diagonal gives two triangles exchanged by a half-turn about its center. This map sends lattice dots to lattice dots even when the center is a half-lattice point, so their counts and Q agree. The legal common-diagonal join gives `2Q=ab`; congruence independently gives each area `ab/2`. Integer translations and axis reflections cover all triangles with axis-parallel perpendicular legs.
3. **Triangles having a horizontal or vertical side.** All three vertices lie on the boundary of their minimal axis rectangle. The complement comprises at most two nondegenerate axis-right triangles; omit zero-area pieces. Attach them successively to the target triangle along its full sloping sides to obtain the rectangle. Every join meets exactly in one side and stays simple. Subtract the already established pieces' Q and area. This also works when the third vertex projects beyond an endpoint of the horizontal base; exchanging x/y covers vertical bases.
4. **Every remaining lattice triangle, using two legal diagonal cuts.** Its three x coordinates are distinct, or it already has a vertical side. Let A and C be the leftmost and rightmost vertices, and B the middle-x vertex. If A and C share y, Step 3 applies. Otherwise the points `(Bx,Ay)` and `(Bx,Cy)` lie strictly on opposite sides of line AC, whose height at Bx is strictly between Ay and Cy. Choose D to be the point on the opposite side from B. D is a lattice point. A and C lie on opposite sides of the vertical line BD, so A,B,C,D is a nondegenerate convex quadrilateral with both diagonals inside. Its BD cut produces ABD and BCD, both with a vertical side. Its AC cut produces the target ABC and ACD; ACD has a horizontal side. Step 3 covers these three comparison triangles. The legal joins give `Q(ABC)+Q(ACD)=Q(ABCD)=Q(ABD)+Q(BCD)`. Independent areas obey the same two decompositions. Subtracting ACD proves `Q(ABC)=area(ABC)`. No off-lattice intersection or bent-seam join is used.
5. **All simple polygons, including inward corners.** Remove redundant collinear corners without changing any boundary-dot count. A simple polygon has an ear triangulation using its existing vertices. Remove one nondegenerate ear, prove the smaller simple polygon by induction, and reattach the ear along its one full diagonal. That legal join proves the rule for the original polygon. This does not assume an arbitrary order of adding all triangles is legal, and does not use the older empty-triangle investigation.

For a concrete check of Step 4, take A=`(0,0)`, B=`(2,1)`, C=`(6,6)`, D=`(2,6)`. Target ABC has `(I,B,A)=(0,8,3)`; ACD `(7,12,12)`; ABD `(2,8,5)`; BCD `(6,10,10)`; quadrilateral ABCD `(12,8,15)`. The two decompositions give `3+12=5+10=15`. The draft's three-complementary-right-triangles shortcut does not cover this interior-vertex triangle: its rectangle complement also contains a quadrilateral. The repaired two-diagonal argument is author-developed in the independent mathematics review, not a quoted claim from NRICH. The checker also tests all 2,148 noncollinear triples on a 5-by-5-dot board: 1,600 already have an axis side and 548 use the quadrilateral bridge. Finite tests check implementation; Step 4 supplies the general proof.

For a connected region bounded by one simple outer lattice polygon and `h` genuine holes strictly inside it, with straight simple hole polygons having pairwise disjoint closures, `A = I+B/2-1+h`. Hole-boundary dots belong to B; dots inside a removed hole do not belong to I. The holes are distinct complement components, not nested loops defining islands. Curved sides, off-lattice vertices, crossings, overlaps, disconnected regions and touching boundaries are outside the stated claim.

To derive the extension, let the filled outer polygon have counts `Io,Bo` and filled hole j have `Ij,Bj`. Then `I=Io-sum(Ij+Bj)` and `B=Bo+sum(Bj)`. Independent area is outer area minus the filled hole areas. Applying the established no-hole theorem to each filled polygon and substituting gives `A=I+B/2-1+h`. Disjointness makes each removal count once. The supplied square ring has `(I,B,A)=(0,24,12)` and the no-hole expression gives 11; it suggests, but does not prove, the `+1` correction.

The revised Problem 2 triangle `(0,0),(3,0),(1,3)` has inside dots `(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)`. Its B consists of the four base dots and its top vertex; both sloping sides have no other lattice dots. A base of 3 and height 3 independently gives area `9/2`. Two congruent copies make a parallelogram of base 3 and height 3, area 9. The L-shape retains a contrasting even boundary count, intermediate side dots, no inside dots and whole-number area 5.

The checker verifies every printed shape, both join pieces, the fixed-count construction's existence, one- and two-hole constructions, both diagonal proof decompositions, and all six possible `(I,B)` records for area 6 within the supplied 7-by-7-dot board. For area 6, `B=14-2I` is even, and `B>=3`, so `I=0,…,5`; explicit witnesses realize all six records. These are supporting mathematical checks, not a facilitator guide or classroom evidence.
