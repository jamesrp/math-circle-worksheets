## Mathematical destination and scope

For a **complete planar m-by-n grid of square cells**, with fixed-length side bars, freely turning pin joints, and at most one rigid diagonal per cell, the row/column bracing graph is connected if and only if the framework is infinitesimally rigid. Here the graph has one vertex for each strip of cells (m row strips and n column strips), not one vertex for each physical joint. A braced cell produces an edge between its row and its column. The minimum brace count is m+n−1, and a minimum rigidifying brace set corresponds to a spanning tree of this bipartite graph.

The experiment asks whether a frame can change shape under a gentle flat push. That is a question about finite motion. An infinitesimal motion is a first-order velocity assignment satisfying bar-length constraints. These notions differ for arbitrary frameworks. In the complete square-grid case, the connectivity criterion also describes finite continuous rigidity, with the separate argument below and the broader primary result of Grasegger–Legerský. Rigidity here means **no continuous shape-changing motion from the given placement**, allowing whole-frame translation/rotation. It is not global rigidity (uniqueness of every disconnected, equivalent embedding), mechanical strength, resistance to bending, or rigidity in three-dimensional space.

Assumptions matter: all cells and side bars are present; sides are constant length; opposite cell sides begin equal and parallel; braces join actual opposite corner joints of individual cells; joints rotate without sliding; the motion is planar; the initial cells are nondegenerate. Holes, long cross-grid diagonals, string/cable braces, locked joints, lifted joints and missing bars require different analysis. Two diagonals in one square impose the same row/column connection here; do not count a crossing as a new joint. Finite cardstock slack is an experimental limitation, not a counterexample to the ideal theorem.

## Proofs available to the adult

**Triangle and square.** Hold one triangle side still. The third joint has fixed distances from both ends, so lies at one of at most two circle intersections. It cannot move continuously between them while the side lengths stay fixed. An unbraced square can become a rhombus. A square with a diagonal consists of two triangles sharing a fixed side; the planar shape cannot shear. Joined triangles can still fold out of the plane, so the flat-table restriction is essential.

**Exact first-order grid argument.** Put joints at p(i,j)=(j,i), for i=0,…,m and j=0,…,n. Let velocity be (u(i,j),v(i,j)). Every horizontal bar imposes u(i,j+1)=u(i,j), so u(i,j)=a(i). Every vertical bar imposes v(i+1,j)=v(i,j), so v(i,j)=b(j). Apart from two translations, the variables are the m differences A(i)=a(i+1)−a(i) and n differences B(j)=b(j+1)−b(j). Either diagonal in cell (i,j) imposes A(i)+B(j)=0. Define row turns r(i)=−A(i) and column turns c(j)=B(j); a brace says r(i)=c(j). A graph with k components allows one common turn for each component. One shared turn is ordinary whole-frame rotation, leaving exactly k−1 shape-changing first-order modes. If connected, only the two translations and one rotation remain. The adult can translate this algebra into linked turn cards; children do not need the algebra or calculus.

**Separate finite argument in this grid case.** Near the initial square placement, a four-bar cell with equal opposite sides remains on its nondegenerate parallelogram branch. Thus all horizontal bars in column strip j have a common direction h(j), and all vertical bars in row strip i have a common direction v(i). A square-length diagonal keeps the angle between h(j) and v(i) at 90 degrees in a braced cell. A connected row/column graph propagates these directions: all horizontal strip directions are one common direction and all vertical strip directions its perpendicular. The only continuous motions are rigid motions.

For disconnected bracing components, assign each component its own small rotation α. Rotate every h(j) and v(i) belonging to that component by α. Put each joint at the sum of its preceding horizontal and vertical bar vectors. Side lengths remain fixed; each braced cell's two vectors rotate together, so both its side lengths and chosen diagonal length stay fixed. A pair of directions in different components changes relative angle, giving a genuine nontrivial continuous flex. For sufficiently small rotations the initial distinct joints remain distinct. This constructs an actual flex rather than assuming that every first-order flex integrates. The finite construction is checked numerically in `math-checks.json` for one contrasting 3-by-3 placement; the symbolic reasoning is the justification for all grids.

**Minimum and redundant braces.** Initially the bracing graph has m+n isolated vertices. A new edge can reduce the component count by at most one, so at least m+n−1 braces are necessary. Connecting every row to the first column and the first row to every remaining column achieves that count. Within a connected graph, a brace is redundant for rigidity precisely when deleting its edge leaves the graph connected; an edge on a cycle is redundant. In a disconnected graph, a brace within a component does not remove a motion; a brace joining components removes one. A cycle of redundant constraints elsewhere does not compensate for an unjoined component. This is a geometric discovery with a graph explanation, not merely an unweighted tree-listing exercise.

Checked facts: 2-by-2 requires three braces; all four three-cell choices rigidify. A 2-by-3 grid requires four; 12 of its 15 four-cell choices rigidify. A 3-by-3 grid requires five; 81 of 126 five-cell choices rigidify. The upper-left 2-by-2 block plus the bottom-right cell gives five braces, covers every row and column, and still has two components. These exhaustive counts are adult audit data, not prescribed student enumeration. See `verify_kernels.py` and `math-checks.json`.
