Week 33: Prime-length necklaces

Mathematical kernels

1. Strings and rings have different counts because a ring has several starting views. Fix c colors and n labeled positions read in one direction. An ordered string has a marked first position; closing it into a ring identifies strings that differ by rotation only. Reflection is not allowed. For three positions and two colors, the eight words form four rotation families: {AAA}, {BBB}, {AAB,ABA,BAA}, {ABB,BBA,BAB}. For four positions, ABAB and BABA form a nonconstant family of size 2. The binary orbit sizes at n=4 are 1,1,2,4,4,4. This gives a concrete failure of blindly dividing the number of strings by the ring length.

2. Prime ring lengths give Fermat's little theorem. If p is prime, a nonconstant p-position ring has exactly p distinct starting views. Indeed, if rotation by k positions with 0<k<p fixed it, repeated k-steps would visit all p positions, forcing all colors equal. The c constant strings have one view each, and the other c^p−c words split into disjoint groups of p, so p divides c^p−c. For p=5 and c=2, there are two constant singleton families and six nonconstant families of five views: 32−2=30. This is a counting proof of a number-theoretic theorem, not a primality-test equivalence; composites can satisfy related congruences. Children need not calculate large powers to own the ring argument. The generic 4-necklace orbit investigation appears as unvetted atlas lead AD-24; this is a rebuilt, elementary-focused lead with a prime-length divisibility destination, not a second count of the same family's grade adaptations.

Sources: University of Oregon Math 391, Worksheet 6 (12 October 2020), https://pages.uoregon.edu/adding/courses/391/ws6.pdf ; UC Berkeley EECS 70, Discussion 10W (Fall 2014), “Fermat's necklace,” https://www-inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs70/fa14/section/sec10W.pdf . These are primary instructor sources for the exact proof; the K–5 manipulative design is unpiloted.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 matches three-position rings by turning and distinguishes one-color from mixed rings; grades 2–3 compare the different numbers of starting views at lengths 3,4,5 and find a short repeating pattern; grades 4–5 explain why a prime length forbids a shorter nonconstant period and turn the grouping into a divisibility proof. Systematic word counting and the prime-divisibility argument are optional readiness gates beyond the shared concrete entry.

Materials

Per pair or trio: two flat ring mats of each selected size 3,4,5, five counters of each of two colors or textures, a movable start-arrow marker, and a fixed clockwise-direction arrow. Flat mats are preferable to actual loose necklaces because accidental flipping changes the equivalence relation. Marks distinguish the first position but do not make it a permanent colored bead. Supply small picture cards for at least the eight binary three-position strings; if investigating all five-position strings, provide the 32 cards already printed rather than requiring repetitive copying or construction of all of them. Every picture must have a consistent start mark and reading direction. Ring diagrams large enough for counters should have diameter about 8 cm; two can fit across US Letter inside ordinary margins. The unfamiliar input→rotate start→new readout needs one explicit matched-label example, such as AAB → ABA → BAA; do not announce the later prime-length conclusion in student prompts. Reflections remain outside the rule even when a particular small binary ring happens to match its reflection.
