# Mathematical overview

Independent nonnegative rectangle sides A in [a,b] and B in [c,d] give area in [ac,bd], tightly when each endpoint pair is allowed. A known sum links sides and changes the range: for whole sides at least 1 and A+B=6, labeled pairs (1,5),(2,4),(3,3),(4,2),(5,1) have areas 5,8,9,8,5. The safe independent enclosure [1,25] loses the shared-sum information; the tight enclosure is [5,9]. There are three shapes up to rotation, or five labeled side pairs. Intermediate real areas and whole-side enumeration are different questions.

Guaranteed ranges for the SAME unchanged hidden length intersect. Independent lengths do not. If exactly one card is false, its interval must actually fail for the chosen witness, while the other cards must hold; merely ignoring a card is not enough. The printed examples make every proposed false-card witness disjoint from that card, so their logic is unambiguous. No measurement distribution, averaging rule or confidence percentage is assumed.

For independently allowed endpoints A in [a,b], B in [c,d], distance |A-B| has minimum max(0,a-d,c-b) and maximum max(|a-d|,|b-c|). The minimum is zero if ranges overlap, including one shared endpoint. Maximum occurs at opposite endpoints. All edges are allowed; values can lie between ticks unless a task specifies whole units. A can be longer exactly when b>c, B can be longer exactly when d>a, and equality is possible exactly when ranges intersect. Strictly longer excludes a tie.

Grades 2-5 can tile rectangles, intersect card possibilities and slide endpoints. Ready older children explain why dependence changes extremes, diagnose false guarantees and design near/far ranges. Trials provide allowed witnesses or counterexamples; all-side enumeration, interval intersections and extremal arguments prove the tight bounds.

Per pair: packet, twenty-five paper square tiles of side 20 mm, two sliding paper strips with opaque sleeves or cover cards, ruler, two endpoint markers, pencil and scrap paper. The page-1 working squares are exactly 20 mm, unlike its small non-task example. The page-2 ruler spacing is 45 points (15.9 mm) and page-3 spacing 39 points (13.8 mm); these are ideal unit diagrams, not millimetre-instrument uncertainty claims. Draw two equal physical unit rulers if using the same sleeves on both pages, or use endpoint markers instead. Strip range boundaries are stipulated guarantees. No calibrated error bound is inferred from a ruler's markings.

For eleven children in KK11 / 3333 / 445, prepare five pair kits: 125 paper tiles, ten strips and sleeves/cover cards, five rulers, ten endpoint markers, five pencils, and three anchored adults. The third older child checks cards and rotates into the pair. The arithmetic-rich extensions are optional adult conversation.

# Exact answers, hints, extensions

1. Enumerate A=1,2,3,4,5; the corresponding B values are 5,4,3,2,1. Areas are 5,8,9,8,5; the extrema are attained by 1-by-5 and 3-by-3. Rotations do not create new unlabeled shapes, but both A/B orders satisfy the problem. Neither 1 nor 25 occurs with the exact sum: 1 requires (1,1), sum 2, and 25 requires (5,5), sum 10. The honest tight interval is 5 to 9. Optional hint: can one more tile-width on one side preserve the sum without shortening the other? Extension: compare different fixed sums and use a cut-and-rearrange or (A-B)² argument for the most square-like rectangle; whole and real sides give different equality cases.

2. First row intersection is 5-6, including edges and intermediate values. Second row: the first card 1-4 may be false, with any length in 5-8 (e.g. 5); the last card 5-9 may be false, with any length in 3-4 (e.g. 3). The middle card cannot be the only false one because 1-4 and 5-9 have no shared value. Third row: only the first card 1-4 can be false; the two true cards intersect in 7-8 (e.g. 7), which indeed defeats 1-4. If the middle were false, 1-4 and 7-9 are incompatible; if the last were false, 1-4 and 6-8 are incompatible. Optional hint: keep two candidate true cards visible and ask for one endpoint they both permit, then check the discarded card really fails. Extension: invent a row with exactly two different possible false cards, or distinguish an inaccurate bound from an intentionally wide but true bound.

3. A=3-7, B=5-9: gap 0-6. Zero occurs at a common value such as 5; 6 at A=3,B=9. A can be longer (7,5), B can be longer (3,9), and ties are possible. A=2-4, B=6-8: gap 2-6. Minimum at 4,6, maximum at 2,8; B is always strictly longer and equality is impossible. A=3-5, B=5-7: gap 0-4. Zero only at 5,5; maximum at 3,7. B can be strictly longer, A never strictly longer, and equality is possible. Designed cards A=4-6,B=4-6 always give gap at most 2 and allow either order, for example 6,4 or 4,6. Many designs work; the task does not impose a widest-card optimum. Optional hint: try to make the ends coincide before looking only at opposing extremes. Extension: add a promise about their total and see which opposite extremes cease to be jointly possible.
