Week 31: Hidden orchard

Mathematical kernels

1. Visibility is a precise number-theoretic question. A nonzero integer lattice point (a,b) is visible from O=(0,0) if the open straight segment OT contains no other lattice point. It is visible if and only if gcd(|a|,|b|)=1. A target (4,2) is blocked by (2,1), whereas (3,2) has no blocker. This is an ideal point-obstruction model: a thick physical tree must not be allowed to hide a noncollinear dot. Every point (n,1) is visible. The elementary action is stretching a thread from a fixed lookout to a target and determining whether another dot lies exactly on it.

2. Every lattice ray has one first point, and gcd counts the other points. Write a=du,b=dv with d=gcd(a,b) and gcd(u,v)=1. The interior lattice points from (0,0) to (a,b) are exactly k(u,v) for k=1,…,d−1. Existence is immediate by equal stepping. Conversely, any lattice point on the ray has the form t(u,v); write t=r/s in lowest terms. The denominator s must divide both u and v, so s=1. Thus the first point is (u,v) and the open segment has d−1 lattice points. Examples: (6,4) has blocker (3,2); (6,3) has blockers (2,1),(4,2); (5,3) has none. The general converse requires fraction/divisibility reasoning; young children's exact demonstrations of finite cases are genuine but should not be mislabeled as a general proof. Do not turn a small-grid count into a purported proof of the limiting density 6/π².

Sources: “Visible lattice points along curves,” The Ramanujan Journal (2020), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11139-020-00302-w , generalizing the ordinary visibility criterion; Karl-Dieter Crisman, Number Theory: In Context, §24.6.1, https://math.gordon.edu/ntic/ntic/section-four-facts.html , for the lattice line-of-sight model. This is a new visibility investigation; the prior Week 9 billiards packet used coprime first-lattice-point geometry as part of an unfolding proof, so the connection should be acknowledged without repeating its billiards tasks.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1 tests visible/hidden targets on a small dot grid and shows a blocker with thread; grades 2–3 coordinate across/up step recipes and seek a shortcut to predict visibility; grades 4–5 characterize first points and number of blockers using common factors, with reduced fractions supporting a full converse when ready. Coordinate notation is optional for entry; exact collinearity is essential.

Materials

Per working pair or trio: one laminated or card-mounted 7×7 array of dots labeled 0 through 6 in each direction, grid spacing at least 2 cm, a taped thread anchor at (0,0), approximately 30 cm of thin nonstretch thread, ten removable small colored markers, ruler, pencil, and plain dot-grid paper. A 5×5 array may be used for the youngest group. Dots rather than grid cells are the locations. The anchor must stay fixed while a target is tested. Use thin crosses/dots for exact locations; if tree pictures are included, their centers are the obstruction and the outlines have no mathematical thickness. Student diagrams must identify origin, target and blocker consistently and show one visible comparison before asking children to apply the new representation. Scales do not affect the theorem, but square spacing and straight lines must be accurate; independent horizontal/vertical stretching is unnecessary.
