Week 35: Footprints that match only after a glide

Mathematical kernels

1. A glide reflection is reflection across a line followed by a nonzero translation along that line. Its square is translation through twice the glide distance. Children can generate an endlessly repeating border by alternating a genuinely asymmetric motif and its horizontal mirror image. Use an F-like footprint with an asymmetric toe, all motifs pointing to the right, at successive equally spaced positions alternately above and below the centerline. The resulting ideal infinite border is preserved by a one-step glide and a two-step translation; neither a bare one-step translation nor a bare reflection across the centerline preserves it. This exposes a symmetry that neither of its component moves possesses. Proof: trace one motif and its handedness through each candidate motion, then repeat using the generating rule. The infinite extension, not the finite strip's endpoints, is the object.

2. A frieze has a discrete nontrivial group of translations generated by a smallest positive translation along one axis, with no translations in other directions. Exactly seven symmetry-group types occur. For K–5, this theorem is adult context and an optional upper ceiling, not a demand to memorize seven names or classify worksheets full of cards. The core is constructing and testing borders with specified actual symmetries and discovering which further symmetries are forced. For example, two perpendicular reflection symmetries force a half-turn; a glide forces a translation. State exactly which claims children have established. Distinguish the symmetries of one motif from the symmetries of the whole infinite pattern; a symmetric motif can occur in a less symmetric arrangement. The full seven-type classification must not be claimed from a handful of experiments.

Build original motifs rather than copying source figures.

Suggested emphasis by level:
K–1: Make and extend physical footprint borders, matching a transparent copy by sliding or flipping; spoken instructions, no angle notation.
Grades 2–3: Construct a border with a glide that has no bare centerline reflection, then invent another border with a different set of symmetries.
Grades 4–5: Use actual motions to explain forced symmetries and distinguish minimum translation from glide distance; seven-type classification is optional adult discussion only.

Materials

Per child: 24 pre-cut asymmetric footprint/F motifs at least 25 mm long, with a distinctive notch and direction arrow; half should be the horizontal mirror form, and additional rotated forms should be available. Four paper strips at least 70 by 280 mm; two tracing-paper strips; washable marker or pencil. Per table: a 600 mm demonstration strip made from joined sheets, masking tape, and a ruler. Use movable paper motifs instead of ink to permit conjectures and redesign. Arrowheads at both ends indicate indefinite repetition; leave end margins visibly outside the mathematical pattern. Before work, give one small explicit original-to-reflected-to-shifted motif example with matching labels and arrows. This teaches the unfamiliar glide action but must not display a completed target border or the seven answers. Test motif asymmetry before printing: a simple oval or ordinary symmetric foot can accidentally add symmetries. Adult pre-cutting; no glass mirrors required.

Novelty and readiness: Beyond the 90 atlas. Week 3 composition explains noncommutativity, but this activity investigates infinite spatial patterns, glide-only symmetry, and forced closure of a pattern's symmetry set. Do not turn it into another order-of-two-keys exercise. Draft and unpiloted.
