Footprint borders facilitator guide
Mathematical overview
Object. A border is the ideal infinite repetition in both directions of a finite motif arrangement,
bounded across the strip. Only footprints and their orientations count. Ends, guide lines and
temporary labels do not. The intended examples are discrete, nonempty patterns with a shortest
positive translation P along the border. Finite scraps alone do not establish an infinite symmetry.
Glide theorem. Write H(x,y) = (x,-y) for reflection in the middle line and T_a(x,y) = (x+a,y) for a
slide along it. They commute. A glide G_a = T_a H with a ≠ 0 satisfies G_a² = T_2a. Thus a
matching 3 cm glide forces a matching 6 cm slide, but need not force a 3 cm slide or the bare
reflection H.
Shortest distances. If a border has a glide along this line but H itself fails, its shortest glide
length is P/2. Every glide displacement is (k + 1/2)P, k an integer. If H is a symmetry instead,
matching glide displacements are kP; the shortest nonzero glide length is P. These statements
require a primitive translation P and a fixed glide axis; an arbitrary matching glide need not be the
shortest.
Forced motions. Reflections in two perpendicular lines compose to a half-turn about their
crossing. The printed crossing lines are perpendicular. For general crossing lines the rotation angle
is twice their angle; a half-turn is not a theorem for arbitrary crossing lines. Every composition or
inverse of symmetries is a symmetry.
Limits. There are seven discrete frieze symmetry types, but these problems do not prove or
require that classification. A simple motif can accidentally add symmetries. The specified notched
F-like motif has no nonidentity planar isometry, so its handedness and direction distinguish
candidate moves.
What children are meant to discover
• K-1: Build repeating borders and use a copy to distinguish slides, flips, glides and half-turns. A
local matching attempt is evidence to investigate the repeated rule.
• Grades 2-3: Find a glide that works although the flip alone fails; compare shortest distances
and change which motions a border permits.
• Grades 4-5: Explain forced symmetries by composition and justify a full symmetry description
for one chosen construction. Sampling several moves is not a proof of completeness.
Status. All three final v2 packets contain six problems. Mathematical examples are verified;
physical cutout handling and classroom pacing are untested. The Grades 2-3 edit explicitly
permits moved footprints and a larger repeating part.

Preparation and session plan
Materials and readiness
For ten children, use three adult-led tables. Three-child groups can rotate builder, tester and
observer; budget five pair-sized material sets overall. Per pair supply 20-24 reusable footprint
pieces, four paper strips at least 70 by 280 mm, two tracing strips, a pencil or washable marker,
and a ruler. Provide a 600 mm joined demonstration strip per table. Keep repeated motifs
separated and use at least three or four visible repeats.
Motifs: use the exact notched F-like outline printed in the introduction, at least 25 mm long, with
its direction arrow. Supply the original, its horizontal mirror, its vertical mirror and its half-turn;
ordinary symmetric footprints can change the answers. An economical alternative is identical
rectangular cards carrying those drawings. State that card edges and blank backgrounds are
handling aids, not pattern data. Prepare visible marks on both sides or use tracing paper so a
reflection can be enacted accurately. Adults cut and prepare pieces, not children during the
investigation.
Preparation estimate: 30-45 minutes for a first reusable set, substantially less when reusing it.
Before class, check that the chosen pieces really distinguish all four forms, test one reflected
tracing strip, and rehearse the common launch. The packet is not a complete manipulative kit. If
the exact outline is hard to reproduce, trace and copy its printed example rather than substitute a
symmetric stamp without checking it.
Readiness: children need to extend a repeating arrangement and compare a moved copy with
every motif in the original. Reading and measured lengths can be adult-supported; the youngest
can compare slide lengths directly. If keeping the original fixed or mentally continuing the ends is
difficult, spend the session on one border and slide matching.
Common launch
Let children handle pieces first. Show the student introduction's single motif, its reflection across a
line, and then its slide along that same line. Keep the label A with the same piece during the
demonstration. This teaches the move without giving a target border. Compare two or three
interior repetitions; explain that the printed ends are a window on an endless pattern.
Demonstrate a half-turn about a chosen point when first needed, including a point between
motifs.
Flexible hour
0-8 minutes: handling and launch. 8-23: two different shortest periods, then the glide challenge.
Let a successful pattern remain on one strip while a second is built. 23-27: movement break,
using a hand-held copy to show a slide and a half-turn. 27-45: selected reflection/half-turn
construction or edit. 45-56: partner testing and one explanation of a forced motion. 56-60: save
the generating rule and note what children could actually justify.
A good stopping point is one border with a well-defended unusual symmetry. Upper Problem 6 can
occupy an entire later discussion. Offer hints only when needed: “Where must this one footprint
land?” then “Does its direction agree?” then “What happens if you do your matching move twice?”
