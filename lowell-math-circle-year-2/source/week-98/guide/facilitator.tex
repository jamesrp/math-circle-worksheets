\head{Mathematical overview: fewest and indispensable are different}
\textbf{The model.} An $n\times n$ square grid has an open boundary. Only side-neighbors count; there are no outside cells or wraparound edges. Choose seeds, then repeatedly fill any empty cell touching at least two filled side-neighbors. Filled cells stay filled. Stop only when no legal addition remains. A partner checks each addition.

\textbf{Every completed order has the same finish.} The board is finite and the filled set grows. Let $C$ be any closed set containing the seeds, where closed means no cell outside $C$ has two side-neighbors in $C$. Inductively every legal addition belongs to $C$. By that induction a terminal run lies inside every closed superset of the seeds, and it is itself closed, so it is the least one. Every terminal run is thus the same least closed superset. Different intermediate pictures or timings do not contradict this final-state result.

\textbf{Exactly $n$ seeds are needed to fill an $n\times n$ board.} Count exposed unit sides, including the board boundary and any hole boundaries. Adding a cell with $k$ already filled side-neighbors changes this count by $4-2k$. Legal additions have $k\ge2$, so perimeter never increases. A start of $s$ seeds has at most $4s$ exposed sides. The full board has $4n$, forcing $s\ge n$. Conversely the $n$ diagonal cells fill: induct on the distance $|r-c|$ from the main diagonal. Each cell in the next diagonal layer has two neighbors in the previous layer. This supplies both an impossibility certificate and an attaining construction.

\textbf{Every seed can be necessary in a non-smallest start.} Inclusion-minimal means removing any seed prevents filling; minimum means no filling start has fewer seeds. The four seeds $(1,1),(1,2),(2,3),(3,3)$ (row, column, counted from 1 at the top left) on a $3\times3$ board are all indispensable, yet three diagonal seeds fill. Neither notion implies that all legal growth orders have the same intermediate picture.

\textbf{What children discover.} The Grades 2--3 route emphasizes choosing, growing, resetting and comparing small seed sets. Grades 4--5 adds perimeter as a lower bound and the distinction between local removal tests and a global optimum. Reading can be supported; the child should retain the choice of seeds and legal additions. Counting exposed edges and interpreting a general $n$ are later readiness demands. No K--1 packet is claimed; the preparation section gives an adult-led K--1 route on the Grades 2--3 pages.

\textbf{Evidence and limits.} A completed construction proves one start works. Exhaustive checks prove the stated small-board counts. The closure, perimeter and diagonal arguments prove the general assertions. This is deterministic bootstrap percolation, not a lesson proving a random critical threshold. Familiar closure and perimeter ideas are deliberately reused for a new optimization question.

\head{Preparation, shared launch and pacing}
\textbf{Status.} Prepared for organizer review; unpiloted. Physical printing, preparation, handling and timings remain unrehearsed.

\textbf{K--1 route, adult-led.} No K--1 packet is printed; a K--1 table uses Grades 2--3 pages, with the parent reading and recording. Read the rule as one sentence: ``A square gets a counter when two squares next to it, not corner to corner, have counters.'' After the launch, use Problem 1 (page 1): the child places three seeds, grows them while a partner points to the two neighbors, and the parent asks, ``Did it fill, or did it get stuck?'' Then put counters on Problem 2's large $4\times4$ board: ``Can you fill it with fewer seeds?'' The parent draws each start that fills on a small board. End with Problem 5 (page 5): each child grows one of the four small boards, and the table compares. Key in plain words: three seeds in a slanting line fill the $3\times3$ board, and three in a straight row get stuck at once; four seeds can fill the $4\times4$ board, and three never can; every small board on page 5 gets stuck (6, 3, 3 and 6 squares), so the big start needs all four seeds, even though three slanting seeds fill the board. That K--1 children keep the two-neighbor rule for a session is a prediction.

\textbf{Per pair.} Sixteen counters or square tiles no wider than 25 mm, in two colors: one color for seeds, the other for added squares. The packets' 30 mm boards are the workboards: the $3\times3$ boards on Grades 2--3 pages 1, 3 and 4 and Grades 4--5 pages 1 and 6, and the $4\times4$ board on page 2 of both packets. No separate board or mask is needed. Keep a reset sketch visible. For $5\times5$ work supply 25 counters or use pencil diagrams. Short sticks or drawn strokes can mark exposed edges later. Pieces must not conceal neighboring cells; exact tile-to-cell fit is unnecessary. Print US Letter, single-sided at Actual Size. Smaller printed grids are records. For the launch, draw a $2\times3$ grid of 30 mm squares on paper or a whiteboard.

\textbf{Staffing.} Two pairs at the middle table and a pair plus rotating checker at the upper table. An adult reads as needed, but the partner should identify the two filled side-neighbors before a new counter is added. Rotate seed designer, grower and checker. Counting counters into bags for three pairs and a demonstration set takes about 10 minutes beyond printing; this estimate needs a real rehearsal.

\textbf{Launch after the movement opening.} Let children handle the counters for two minutes. On a separate $2\times3$ board use the input below. Fill the top middle, pointing to its left and lower neighbors. Neither right-hand cell can then fill. Diagonal touching alone is insufficient. The start stays marked while later counters are added.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
Input & One legal addition & Finish\\[3pt]
$\begin{matrix}S&\cdot&\cdot\\S&S&\cdot\end{matrix}$ &
$\begin{matrix}S&+&\cdot\\S&S&\cdot\end{matrix}$ &
$\begin{matrix}S&+&\cdot\\S&S&\cdot\end{matrix}$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
$S$ is an original seed and $+$ a later addition. This example teaches a whole run and stopping without supplying an optimal square start.

\textbf{A first visit.} After the five-minute movement opening and five-minute launch, allow 20--25 minutes for contrasting starts and the first fewest-seed search. Use the remaining 10--15 minutes to compare different successful starts or test the same start with different legal choices. Close with one construction and one question. The perimeter proof and indispensable-seed investigation can fill another visit; do not rush every page into one hour. First route: Grades 2--3 Problems 1, 2 and 4, Grades 4--5 Problems 1--3. Return visit: Grades 2--3 Problems 3 and 5, Grades 4--5 Problems 4--6.

\textbf{Hints only when useful.} If a child changes the rules, ask the checker to touch the two supporting squares. If the original seeds are lost, reset from the sketch before comparing counts. When a search suggests an optimum but cannot establish it, ask what a filled board must have that a start cannot gain. With Grades 2--3, introduce perimeter sticks then, rather than prescribing a proof before any exploration; Grades 4--5 meet exposed sides in Problem 4. Adult recording is ordinary support; adult seed selection or legal-move selection is a signal to reduce the board.
