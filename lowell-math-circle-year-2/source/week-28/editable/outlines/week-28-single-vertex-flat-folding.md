Week 28: Which crease patterns can close flat?

Unscheduled library slot, unpiloted. The core is local compatibility at one interior point, distinct from using folds as reflection tools in Weeks 15/21, cutting Möbius bands in atlas GA-32, or changing construction tools in AD-12. No physical folding was performed in this research pass. Reliable adult demonstration models must be physically tried during preparation before the session; the arithmetic checks below are not those tests. The writer chooses the student problems.

Mathematical kernels

1. A complete obstruction from alternating sectors. Work with a small disk of initially flat, ideal zero-thickness paper. All straight crease rays meet at its center; every listed ray must actually be folded fully, no additional crease or cut is allowed, and distinct layers may touch but may not pass through one another. Walking once around the center crosses every crease and reverses the orientation of the next sector at each crossing, so an odd number of active rays cannot return to the starting face orientation. With 2m rays and consecutive positive sector angles a1,...,a2m, the ray directions in the flattened model advance and retreat alternately. Returning to the starting ray requires a1−a2+...−a2m to be a multiple of 360 degrees. Its absolute value is less than 360 because the positive angles total 360 and both alternating groups are nonempty, so the difference must be zero. Equivalently, each alternating group fills exactly a half-turn. Comparing assembled angle wedges suffices; degree arithmetic and symbolic sums are not prerequisites. Thus sectors (45,90,90,135) cannot fold flat: their alternating sums are 135 and 225. The two patterns (60,120,120,60) and (30,60,150,120) pass the 180/180 test. Kawasaki–Justin's full single-vertex theorem also gives sufficiency when mountain/valley directions are NOT prescribed, but the obstruction proof above alone proves only necessity. The full converse can remain adult context; a child may complete substantial mathematics by proving an impossible pattern and constructing several valid ones. Several successful local vertices do not certify a multi-vertex sheet.

2. Mountain/valley counts and an exact four-ray classification. View all creases from the same original side of the paper. Maekawa's theorem gives M−V=+2 or −2 at an interior flat-folded vertex. An elementary adult proof takes a small cross-section around the folded tip: the resulting closed, possibly degenerate accordion polygon has a net full turn; each mountain or valley contributes a half-turn of opposite sign, leaving a difference of two. Equivalently apply the polygon angle-sum formula to 0-degree and 360-degree interior angles, as in Hull's proof. For four right-angle sectors there are exactly eight valid labeled mountain/valley assignments: choose the exceptional ray in four ways and its type in two ways, giving three of one type and one of the other. Necessity follows from the difference-of-two rule. For sufficiency, fold the disk in half along a diameter, then fold that two-layer semicircle in half along the perpendicular radius; when unfolded, one of the four crease rays is opposite in type to the other three. Rotating the labeled disk and reversing mountain/valley types realizes every permitted assignment. This is a mathematical construction, not a claim of a completed physical test here. For unequal sectors, a 3-to-1 count alone does NOT certify a specified assignment, even when the alternating-angle condition holds. A failed attempt with real paper likewise proves neither mathematical impossibility nor a child's lack of understanding.

Suggested emphasis by level

- K–1: refold and vary adult-prepared four-right-angle models, identifying actual mountain/valley rays and constructing legal alternatives; fine-motor difficulty must not become the mathematical gate.
- Grades 2–3: use matching angle pieces to distinguish a half-turn closure from an exact mismatch, with actual folded witnesses for successful cases.
- Grades 4–5: justify the alternating-sector obstruction and the eight-assignment right-angle classification, separating necessary tests, sufficient constructions, and physical trial evidence.

Materials

For ten children: thirty thin paper disks about 18 cm in diameter, ten additional plain disks, six adult demonstration disks, three sheets of tracing paper, three rulers, three protractors for adult preparation, removable mountain/valley label tabs, and pencils. Prepare ray marks accurately with one clearly marked center. Print patterns at 100% scale; provide repeated copies so an accidental extra crease does not change the next attempt. Prepare a separate set of removable angle wedges with the same center radius; cut wedges are measurement aids, never the folding specimen. Start with robust four-ray models before requiring dexterous many-layer folding. Adults must physically test each intended valid demonstration and record any construction difficulty before the meeting; this remains unperformed preparation. Printed ray boundaries are ideal lines, not broad sectors. Specify rays rather than full lines: a crease's type can change on the opposite side of the central vertex. Mark the original front side so turning over the paper does not silently exchange mountain and valley labels. All listed creases must be active, and no tearing, slit, glue, new crease, or overlapping loose wedge may substitute for a fold of the intact disk. A thicker stack may fail to lie perfectly flat because real paper has thickness; do not treat that as an exact counterexample.
