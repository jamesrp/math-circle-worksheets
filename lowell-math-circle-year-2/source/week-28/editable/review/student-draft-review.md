# Independent adversarial review: Week 28

## Verdict

Targeted revision is recommended before printing. The packet is mathematically sound in its stated finite tasks, and all 18 pages are cleanly rendered. The strongest problems are concrete and substantial. The principal weaknesses are the grades 2–3 packet's undefined change of manipulatives, the amount of repeated work in K–1, and a steep transition to a general theorem in grades 4–5. These are primarily usability and mathematical-development issues, not incorrect numerical answers.

I read PROMPT.md first, independently rendered all three delivered PDFs at 85 dpi, inspected every page, and checked the relevant TikZ-generation source. Each PDF has six US Letter pages. ## Findings requiring attention

### 1. Grades 2–3, page 2, Problem 2: the required matching wedges have not been identified

“Use the matching loose wedges” follows a page that specifies exactly seven loose wedges, A–G. Those are 30°, 30°, 60°, 60°, 90°, 120°, and 150°. They cannot supply the 45° and 135° sectors of disk B. They also do not include two 90° pieces for simultaneously assembling either alternating pair of disk A. Combining the earlier 30° and 60° pieces could help with 90°, but it cannot produce a 45° piece.

The activity outline does authorize adult-prepared matching measurement wedges, so this is not an impossible task if a separate set is supplied. The problem is that the actual student packet changes sets without naming that change or identifying the new pieces. A child or the non-mathematician volunteer could reasonably continue using the seven pieces just introduced. The exact mismatch in B is the mathematical centerpiece, so this dependency matters.

Revision: identify the separate matching set unambiguously, with labels tied to the four disk cases, and ensure that the actual supplied pieces include the appropriate multiplicities and matching radii. This can be handled through a small change to the task/diagrams and prepared objects; a new facilitator guide or a paragraph of adult instructions on the student page is not needed. The seven wedges on page 1 are good for their own problem and need not be changed.

### 2. K–1, pages 3–6: six pages overstate the amount of independent mathematics available to a quick child

The first two pages provide genuine construction and comparison. Afterward, nearly all the work is repeated enumeration within the same eight right-angle labelings:

- Problem 3 has four answers, all with ray 1 marked M.
- Problem 4 has two answers for each of three partial assignments.
- Problem 5 asks for the eight assignments, many already constructed or printed earlier.
- Problem 6 asks for the minimum number of changed labels; once the three-to-one pattern is understood, this is two.

The constraints in Problems 3 and 4 are legitimate mathematics, and this is not an argument to make K–1 shorter or easier. The issue is the brief's requirement that a quick child have continuing, substantial thinking to do. Once the child has noticed “three of one kind, one of the other,” the last four pages can turn into repeated letter filling. Physical refolding alone should not be what stretches them to forty minutes, because the brief explicitly says fine-motor difficulty must not be the mathematical gate. Six recording circles on Problem 6 do not themselves turn its single minimum question into a sustained task.

Revision: preserve the constructive beginning and the exhaustive-list goal, but replace or deepen at least one repeated enumeration page with a different decision or optimization using specific right-angle models. For example, the label-change question could contain several purposeful starting/target constraints or require all nearest alternatives. Keep the task short enough to hear once, and leave the method to the child. Do not merely add more blank circles.

### 3. Grades 4–5, page 5, Problem 5: the concrete-to-general progression is weak

The page immediately asks children to explain the full difference-of-two theorem for any flat-folded disk with one interior meeting point. Their previous counting/construction task involved only four right-angle rays. The six-, eight-, and ten-ray diagrams on this page are unassigned, unfolded patterns; they do not provide actual folded examples or a concrete object from which a child can see the turning argument. After the rule is supplied in the question, filling the count pairs is simple arithmetic, while justifying the rule is a much larger conceptual step.

This is mathematically correct and could work with strong adult support. It is nevertheless a significant planning risk for an unpiloted session, especially because page 4 has already requested a complete classification whose clean necessity argument is this rule. The page offers either a quick count exercise or an abrupt general proof problem, rather than a well-developed new investigation.

Revision: give this page a concrete investigation that earns the general question, or narrow the explanatory demand to the right-angle classification. Any cross-section explanation, folding method, or theorem proof belongs with the adult, not as a printed hint. If retaining the page unchanged, the organizer needs to decide explicitly how a tested model and adult conversation will bridge this jump.

## Smaller revisions and cautions

### 4. K–1, page 4: “each pair” has an avoidable referent ambiguity

“Keep the printed labels. Find every flat fold each pair allows” means each pair of prescribed ray labels, but the page shows three rows of three disks. A child hearing the sentence once could look for pairs of disks. Refer directly to the two printed letters on each disk or to the three cases. The repeated diagrams and row letters otherwise work well; no extra heading is needed.

### 5. Grades 2–3, page 3: make the requested witness count clear

“Make each possible fold” can mean every valid M/V assignment for each disk, rather than one successful witness for each possible disk. That larger classification task is not what the recording layout supports, and page 5 separately asks for all labelings of the right-angle disk. Ask for one flat fold of each disk that can close, while keeping the exact-mismatch explanation for an impossible case.

### 6. Keep physical preparation separate from mathematical verification

The printed pages do not claim that physical models were tested, which is correct. However, grades 2–3 Problems 3 and 4 require actual successful witnesses, and the K–1 opening assumes children can refold the model rather than create every crease from scratch. The supplied source's arithmetic checks do not establish that these are robust classroom constructions.

Before the session, adults still need to test the intended successful unequal-angle cases, prepare the marked-front right-angle models, prepare the matching wedge sets, and provide fresh specimens when an unwanted crease is made. The organizer's prompt already assigns this preparation; its absence from student pages is not itself a defect, and no extra student-facing preparation prose should be added. The review cannot certify physical readiness.
