Week 45: What does the visible side tell us?

Mathematical kernels

1. Three double-sided cards have colors RR, RB and BB. Choose one of the six card-side identities uniformly, show that face, and consider only red-showing outcomes. Exactly three equally likely identities survive: two hide red and one hides blue. The hidden red chance is two out of three. The theorem is conditional probability on a specified sample space, not a claim that the two remaining card identities are equally likely.

2. A different procedure chooses uniformly between RR and RB and deliberately shows red. Then hidden red occurs in one out of two outcomes. This is one investigation with two clue mechanisms, not two separately counted activities. A visual example must show the paired faces of a card, their distinct identity marks, a drawn face ticket, the face it exposes, and which face remains hidden. Give the selection rule precisely while leaving the hidden-side conclusion for discovery. A few physical trials illustrate the model but do not establish the probabilities.

Sources: Brian Mintz, Dartmouth, Probability Paradoxes, Bertrand's Box Paradox: https://math.dartmouth.edu/~bmintz/GSS_on_probability_paradoxes.pdf. Rebuilt atlas lead AP-02 (evidence selection); not a beyond-90 claim. No duplicate of the produced random-ten sample or recent 18.

Suggested emphasis by level:

K–1: understand paired card faces, follow a face-selection demonstration, and match possible hidden colors.
Grades 2–3: reason with six face tickets and the surviving red outcomes, using small counts.
Grades 4–5: compare precisely stated clue-producing protocols and explain why their answers differ.

Materials

Per child or working pair: three double-sided stiff cards about 50×70 mm, one RR, one RB, one BB; six same-size face-selection tickets; and an opaque ticket cup. Prepare ten sets for independent access (30 double-sided cards, 60 tickets, ten cups), reusable across groups. Each face needs a distinct small identity mark, shown identically on its ticket; colors alone cannot distinguish the two red faces of RR. A screen or the tabletop hides the other face. Tickets are mixed uniformly and replaced between rounds. Provide paired-face diagrams large enough to see both identities without physically exposing the secret during a guess.
