Week 38: What does the seam make?

Mathematical kernels

1. Compare a rectangle whose left/right edges are glued in matching order with one glued in reversed order. They form an annulus and a Möbius band. The annulus has two boundary components; the Möbius band has one. Proof by exact seam tracking: an annulus's top edge returns to top and bottom to bottom, while a Möbius seam sends top to bottom and bottom to top. Tracing one continuous edge is different from counting the apparent edges in a photograph. The paper is a thin model of a mathematical surface: its physical thickness is not part of the topology being investigated.

2. Cutting exactly along the middle circle of the annulus creates two annuli. Cutting the Möbius band along its middle circle creates one annulus. Label the upper and lower half-strips U and L before joining: matching gluing gives U-to-U and L-to-L, while reversed gluing joins U-to-L-to-U. On the latter two-trip strip the transverse direction reverses twice, restoring orientability. This proves the connected-piece result without depending on surprise or a failed attempt to separate the paper. The cut Möbius result is one connected surface with two boundary components. This distinction is central. Several half-turns can test the seam's parity, but knotting/linking and numerical twist counts are different invariants and should not be conflated with the abstract surface type.

Sources: Rebuilt atlas GA-32, with its exact seam derivation. Allen Hatcher, Algebraic Topology, Chapter 0 opening Möbius-band/core-circle discussion, https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~hatcher/AT/ATpage.html ; NRICH, Bands and Bridges: Bringing Topology Back (2011), https://nrich.maths.org/articles/bands-and-bridges-bringing-topology-back . The latter supports hands-on strip investigations (published age range 7–14), not a claim that the proposed K–1 adaptation has been piloted.

Suggested emphasis by level:
K–1: Trace edges and predict/compare one connected piece versus two using adult-prepared bands; adult handles all cutting.
Grades 2–3: Match seam arrows to physical bands and use the U/L connection to explain a cut result.
Grades 4–5: Explain boundary components and orientability from the gluing recipe, contrasting what is observed, predicted, and proved.

Materials

Per child: four paper strips approximately 45 by 400 mm, two already made into matched and reversed-seam bands; two spares for rebuilding; washable markers; four removable start dots. Per table: tape, a 90 by 600 mm demonstration strip, adult scissors, and flat seam diagrams at least 180 mm wide. Longer strips reduce frustrating creases. Every seam has two distinct end marks plus direction arrows so matching versus reversing is unambiguous. Centerlines must be drawn before joining; an adult makes any starter cut. A small explicit diagram must pair points on one flat seam and show the corresponding joined band with the same labels; it should teach the gluing representation without revealing the center-cut outcome. Never use glass, blades, wire, body-worn loops, or child cutting to make the activity work. Any child-scissors option is discretionary under local supervision; adult cutting is the default.

Preparation gate: Physically pretest both bands and the center cuts with the exact paper/tape before teaching. This pretest has NOT been performed during planning. Leave the student questions open rather than printing the surprising answer. A cut is a change to the object, not a permitted deformation demonstrating homeomorphism.

Novelty and readiness: Rebuilt GA-32 atlas lead, not a new mathematical idea beyond the atlas. Distinct from the existing flat-folding topic: seam identification, orientability, and connectedness are the kernels, not crease-angle constraints. Draft and unpiloted.
