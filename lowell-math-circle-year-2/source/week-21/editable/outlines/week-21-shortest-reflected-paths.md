Week 21: The shortest route that touches a line

Library slot, not a scheduled meeting. Rebuild of atlas lead GA-21, unpiloted. The central question is optimality of one contact point, distinct from Week 9's periodic billiard routes. The old worksheet's coordinate minimization and strict-convexity machinery are not the model. The writer chooses fresh problems; checked configurations below are mathematical evidence, not an assigned sequence.

Mathematical kernels

1. Reflection gives a universal shortest-path certificate. Let A and B lie strictly on the same side of a straight line l. A legal route consists of the straight segments A–M and M–B with M anywhere on l. Reflect B across l to B'. For every possible M on l, MB=MB', so the route has the same length as A–M–B'. By the triangle inequality this length is at least AB'. The straight segment A–B' crosses l exactly once, at M*, because its endpoints are in opposite open half-planes; at that crossing the lower bound is attained. Thus A–M*–B is shortest among all legal routes. Equality in the Euclidean triangle inequality requires M on the segment A–B', giving uniqueness of the contact. The whole proof can be made by folding, congruent lengths, and straightening a string. “This one looks shortest” or comparing finitely many measured attempts alone is not a proof against every possible contact point.

2. The legal region matters. If contact is restricted to a drawn line segment and M* belongs to it, exactly the same lower bound and attaining route prove optimality there. If the segment misses M*, the straight reflected route is not legal; the theorem has not solved that new optimization problem. Keep the initial mathematics to cases with a legal crossing rather than introducing an unproved endpoint shortcut. Independently checked configurations in arbitrary equal units: with l the horizontal axis, A=(0,3) and B=(8,3), M*=(4,0); both route legs have length 5 and the minimum is 10. With A=(0,2) and B=(6,4), M*=(2,0); the minimum is 6√2, though neither coordinates nor square roots are needed to construct or certify it. In the second case the optimum is not the midpoint of the two perpendicular feet. Both crossings lie in the respective finite segments from x=0 to x=8 and x=0 to x=6. The numerical lengths are adult checks only. No curved boundary, obstacle-avoiding route, multiple bounce, or physical law about light follows from this single-line proof.

Suggested emphasis by level

- K–1: make legal two-leg routes and compare lengths with an adult, using folding or a reflected drawing as a concrete entry; no numerical measurement or reading is necessary.
- Grades 2–3: discover a reflection construction that beats arbitrary guesses and check why the fold preserves the relevant length; ruler use and simple shape comparison suffice.
- Grades 4–5: explain why one straightened route proves optimality against every competitor and identify when a restricted contact region invalidates the construction.

Materials

At each of three tables: two 60 cm lengths of non-stretch string, two rulers or straightedges, four US Letter tracing-paper sheets, ten blank US Letter sheets, pencils, erasers, and removable tape. A printed working board must leave usable paper on both sides of the line for reflection; the route and reflected point must fit at 100% print scale. Use a working region at least 15 × 18 cm rather than miniature diagrams. The line is an ideal zero-width straight boundary; thick tape or a ruler edge marks its location but is not an obstacle with thickness. The marked dot centers are endpoints, not peg circumferences. Children may hold or tape the string at the endpoint centers and compare pinched lengths; do not make the length depend on stretching an elastic band or wrapping around thick pegs. If a finite permitted contact segment is drawn, choose and verify that the intended reflected crossing belongs to it. Directions must distinguish touching the boundary from traveling along it. Folding and tracing require no scissors, mirrors, compass, protractor, laser, calculator, or measured angles.
