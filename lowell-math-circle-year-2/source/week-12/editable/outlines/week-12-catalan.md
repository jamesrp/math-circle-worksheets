Week 12: Catalan bijections (draft library slot, not a scheduled meeting)

Mathematical kernels

1. The same objects in different disguises. Pair 2n fixed, ordered points, using every point exactly once and allowing no crossings: chords inside a circle, or arcs all above a line, with point 1 and reading order fixed. Reading from 1 to 2n, write U when a pair opens and D when it closes. There are n of each, and no initial segment has more D's than U's. These are Dyck paths: U=(1,1), D=(1,−1), from (0,0) to (2n,0), never below height zero. Conversely, each D must match the most recent unmatched U; any older choice would force a crossing. The procedures undo one another, proving a bijection rather than merely matching numerical totals. For n=3 the five words are UUUDDD, UUDUDD, UUDDUD, UDUUDD, UDUDUD. For example UUDDUD corresponds to the pairs {1,4}, {2,3}, {5,6}. Rotating, reflecting, or relabeling positions does not identify different pairings. If crossings are allowed, four points have three pairings instead of two, so the noncrossing restriction changes the mathematics.

2. A walk records a whole ordered tree. A rooted plane tree is a rooted tree whose children at each vertex have a fixed left-to-right order; labels on vertices are unnecessary. Walk from the root, visiting each child's entire branch from left to right before the next child. Record U on first going down an edge away from the root and D on returning along it. A tree with n edges gives a Dyck word of length 2n: current depth cannot be negative and the walk returns home. Recover the tree from the word by starting at a root, creating a new rightmost child and moving to it at U, and moving to the parent at D. These maps are inverse, so these ordered rooted trees are counted by the same numbers as the pairings. UUDDUD gives a root with two children, with one additional child under its left child; UDUUDD puts that additional child under its right child. These are distinct ordered trees, even though the underlying unordered rooted trees agree. Arbitrary unlabeled rooted trees, labeled trees, and binary trees have different counting conventions; the stated tree class is essential.

3. An exhaustive count comes from a forced split. Every nonempty Dyck word has a unique decomposition U P D Q, where the displayed D is its first return to height zero and P, Q are Dyck words. If the whole word has n pairs and P has i pairs, Q has n−1−i. Therefore C0=1 and Cn=sum from i=0 to n−1 of Ci C(n−1−i). This gives C0 through C5 equal to 1,1,2,5,14,42. Equivalently, in a noncrossing pairing, point 1 is paired with an even position 2i+2; the points inside that pair and after it form two independent smaller pairings. The partition into cases is disjoint and complete, so this is a proof of an all-solutions count, not a guess from a number pattern. The five size-three examples and both inverse encodings have been checked independently. The three representations are alternative routes into the same mathematics, not a requirement to rush through all of them in one hour.

Suggested emphasis by level:
K–1: noncrossing pairings of a few fixed points, with physical strings and an all-different collection; oral instructions and counting small sets, without symbolic codes.
Grades 2–3: a reversible pairing–path code and complete small collections; read a fixed left-to-right order and track rises and falls using counters or drawings.
Grades 4–5: an exact bijection involving ordered rooted trees or a first-return counting proof; keep the representation count flexible and require only small counts, organized cases, and reversible reasoning.

Materials

Preparation specification for ten children: five flexible strings 40 cm long per child (50 total), ten 15–20 mm paper endpoint discs per child (100 total), and ten 20 mm square direction cards per child (five U/up-right and five D/down-right; 100 total). Strings are reusable and may be shortened by folding surplus outside the diagram, but their mathematical routes must stay in the permitted region and cannot pass over or under another string at a crossing. Paper-and-pencil arcs are an exact fallback if yarn is unavailable. Provide blank paper, pencils/erasers, and a small whiteboard per table if already available. Pairing mats intended for objects need clearly separated endpoint positions, fixed numbered positions on circles, and enough interior space to distinguish nesting from crossing; line mats need at least 20 mm between neighboring endpoints and about 60 mm clear height above the working row. Path grids drawn on paper need no physical unit calibration. Tree drawings must show a root and preserve left-to-right child order; no numbered labels that accidentally turn them into labeled-tree enumeration. Print single-sided US Letter at 100% scale. These quantities specify proposed preparation, not a verified current supply of string or cards.
