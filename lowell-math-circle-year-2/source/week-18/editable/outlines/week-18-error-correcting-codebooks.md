Mathematical kernels

1. Reliable correction is separation. A codebook assigns a different fixed-length binary strip to each message. In transmission, at most one position may change from 0 to 1 or from 1 to 0; its location is not revealed. The receiver knows the codebook and sees only the received strip. Recovery is guaranteed exactly when every pair of codewords differs in at least three positions. If they differ in one position, one unchanged word could also be the damaged other word. If they differ in two, changing one of those positions creates a received strip one change away from both. Conversely, a strip within one change of each of two codewords would force those words to differ in at most two positions, which is forbidden. The mathematical object is therefore a family of disjoint “unchanged or one-change” neighborhoods. A code that merely notices something is wrong need not determine the message: 00 and 11 detect a single change, but received 01 could come from either. No assumption about likely errors is needed.

2. Constructions and counting prove an exact shortest length. For two messages, 000 and 111 correct one change by majority; two positions cannot suffice because their distance is at most two. For four messages the exact minimum length is five. Each length-n codeword has n+1 possible received strips, counting no change. A reliable four-message code needs four disjoint neighborhoods, hence 4(n+1) <= 2^n. At length four this would require 20 different strips among only 16. Any shorter successful code could be padded with fixed zeros to make a successful length-four code, so all shorter lengths are ruled out too. The independently constructed length-five codebook {00000, 00111, 11001, 11110} attains the bound on length: its six pairwise distances are 3, 3, 4, 4, 3, 3; its four neighborhoods contain 24 distinct received strips. For example, 00101 uniquely decodes to 00111. The other eight binary strips are outside the one-error promise, and need not be decoded. The inequality is a necessary bound, not a general existence theorem; do not infer a code merely because it fits the count. In particular, this argument proves shortest length for four messages, not the largest possible codebook at every length.

Suggested emphasis by level

- K–1: send and repair two-message picture strips, encountering both successful recovery and genuine ambiguity; match positions and count to three, with rules supplied orally.
- Grades 2–3: invent and challenge short codebooks under a hidden one-change rule; compare pairs of strips and distinguish spotting damage from recovering a message.
- Grades 4–5: connect a working codebook to a disjoint-neighborhood counting obstruction and prove a shortest length; powers of two may be represented by complete binary-strip catalogs.

Materials

For each of three tables: 30 two-sided counters or cards (one face a filled circle, the other an empty circle), ten reusable blank strips with five cells each, four distinct message-picture cards, two opaque folders to separate sender and receiver, pencils, erasers, and blank paper. Cells must be at least 2 cm wide for the supplied counters; if only pencil marks are used, they may be smaller. Make unused cells visibly unavailable when using shorter strips, and mark the left end so reversal cannot occur accidentally. Colors alone must not encode the bit. The codebook is public, but the sender's message card and the original strip must be hidden from the receiver; only the received strip crosses the screen. A changer may flip zero or one position, without indicating which. Erasure, moving positions, reversing the strip, an exposed original, or telling whether an error occurred would change the mathematical problem. Additional full-strip catalogs, if the writer uses them, must include the unchanged strip as well as every one-position change; provide blank paper rather than answer-sized bins that reveal the counting proof.
