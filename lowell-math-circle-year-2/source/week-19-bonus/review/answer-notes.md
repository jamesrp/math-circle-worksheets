# Week 19 final answers and assumptions

Shared Grades 2–5 packet. Entry requires matching symbols, distinguishing every-pair sharing from one symbol on all cards, and counting to sixteen. An adult may read. Later work uses containment, finite descent, and bounds certified by chain partitions. Physical 45 mm subset decks are separate from the small printed whole-deck pencil records. One copy of each subset card is used; symbol order is irrelevant. The three investigations are intersecting families (1–3), minimal rule triggers (4–6), and forbidding nested triples rather than pairs (7–8). All are new questions relative to the base antichain searches.

1. Maximum four. All cards containing A work: A, AB, AC, ABC. A card and its complement are disjoint, so at most one from each of four complement pairs may be chosen. Empty is explicitly forbidden, even as a singleton collection.
2. AB, AC, BC, ABC has four cards; every two share but none of A,B,C is on all four. This distinction is the goal; students may use the second full-deck record independently of the first.
3. Maximum eight. The eight cards containing A attain it. There are eight complement pairs of the sixteen cards and at most one from each is legal. An alternative with no common symbol is all size-three/four cards plus AB, AC, BC (five plus three = eight). The empty card remains excluded.
4. Triggers AB and D produce D, AB, AD, BD, CD, ABC, ABD, ACD, BCD, ABCD (ten). Triggers A and BC produce A, AB, AC, AD, BC, ABC, ABD, ACD, BCD, ABCD (ten). The whole-deck record externalizes each separate rule; marks mean works, unmarked means fails. Worked convention only: AC is contained in ACD, so trigger AC accepts card ACD.
5. Minimal triggers B and AC. Their supersets are precisely the ten listed working cards. Both are necessary: B works with no proper working subset, and AC likewise. Any trigger must itself work; a trigger inside B must equal B (empty fails), and a trigger inside AC must equal AC. Thus two are minimal.
6. Yes under the printed promise. Minimal means inclusion-minimal: a working card stops working whenever any one symbol is removed. It does not mean globally fewest symbols; for instance the printed A/BC rule needs both A and BC. Starting from any working card, remove symbols whenever it still works; because the deck is finite this ends at a minimal working card. Hence every working card contains a minimal trigger. Adding symbols preserves success, so every superset of a minimal working card works. The rule with no working cards has no triggers; the rule with the empty card working has the empty card as its sole minimal trigger and every card works. Redundant invented triggers may be deleted. These edge cases belong with adult discussion, not added student directions.
7. Maximum six, all one-symbol and two-symbol cards. A partition into chains empty/A/AB/ABC; B/BC; C/AC covers each card exactly once. A no-triple family takes at most two from each chain, yielding six. This differs from the base no-pair rule; nested pairs are explicitly allowed.
8. Maximum ten, all two- and three-symbol cards (six plus four), or all one- and two-symbol cards (four plus six). Upper-bound partition: empty/A/AB/ABC/ABCD; D/AD/ABD; C/AC/ACD; CD; B/BC/BCD; BD. Chain lengths 5,3,3,1,3,1 permit at most 2+2+2+1+2+1=10 selected cards. No fixed number of lanes is supplied on student pages. Adult can supply blank chain strips if the child chooses that argument. The empty card is permitted for these new triple-forbidding tasks.

Only these small cases are established; no general Sperner or k-Sperner theorem is claimed. Physical card preparation and classroom use remain untested.
