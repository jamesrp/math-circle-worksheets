Week 27: Pair everyone without a blocking pair

Unscheduled library slot, unpiloted. The genuine core is two-sided preference and stability, distinct from maximum-cardinality matching in Week 13 and unilateral route changes in atlas AP-23. This topic has a real prerequisite gate: understanding that two presently unpaired partners BOTH prefer one another to their current partners. The K–1 entry is optional, not an obligation to pad the topic with unrelated sorting. The writer chooses the problems; examples and proofs below are mathematical support.

Mathematical kernels

1. A stable perfect matching always exists in the strict complete two-sided model. There are n labeled objects on each of two sides. Every object ranks all n objects on the other side in a strict order, with no ties. A matching pairs every object once across sides. A blocking pair consists of two objects not currently together that each rank the other ahead of their current partner. Stability means that no such pair exists; it does not mean that everyone gets a first choice or that a total happiness score is maximized. Deferred acceptance constructs a stable matching. An unmatched proposer approaches its most-preferred receiver not yet approached; the receiver holds the better of its current proposer and the new one, rejecting the other. Held pairs are tentative. A receiver never becomes empty after holding someone, and its held proposer only improves. A proposer never repeats a proposal, so at most n² proposals occur. An unmatched proposer cannot run out of receivers: that would mean every receiver is holding someone, accounting for all n proposers. At termination all are paired. If proposer p preferred receiver r to its final partner, p already proposed to r and was rejected; r's final partner is at least as preferred as the one causing that rejection. Hence p and r cannot block. This is a complete finite existence and correctness proof; example-running alone would not establish the guarantee.

2. Stable does not mean unique, and choosing the proposing side matters. For A: X before Y; B: Y before X; X: B before A; Y: A before B, both AX/BY and AY/BX are stable. Letting A,B propose produces AX/BY; letting X,Y propose produces AY/BX. This checked example shows why stability cannot select a unique, side-neutral answer. A contrasting three-pair profile is A: X,Y,Z; B: X,Z,Y; C: Y,X,Z; X: C,B,A; Y: A,C,B; Z: B,A,C. It has exactly one stable matching, AY/BZ/CX; deferred acceptance reaches it in six proposals under the checked schedule. A stronger optional continuation is proposer optimality: under strict complete lists every proposer receives its best partner among all stable matchings, so varying the order in which free proposers act does not change the outcome. Proof idea for the adult: suppose the first rejection of a pair belonging to any stable matching rejects p at r in favor of q, and choose a stable matching S pairing p with r. If q preferred S(q) to r, q would already have been rejected by S(q), an earlier rejection of a stable pair. Therefore q prefers r to S(q), while r prefers q to p, blocking S. No stable pair is ever rejected; all choices preceding the final partner were rejected, proving optimality. This quantifier-heavy extension is optional, not a disguised requirement for the younger bands, and it says nothing about outcomes maximizing both sides' preferences.

Suggested emphasis by level

- K–1: optional two-pair entry only for children who can apply the adult-demonstrated “both prefer” rule using picture ranks; otherwise this topic is not their independent session.
- Grades 2–3: search and challenge small stable pairings with adult-read ranks, preserving the two-sided blocking condition rather than merely pairing first choices.
- Grades 4–5: explain why deferred acceptance ends stably and compare proposing sides; proposer optimality is available only when the first-rejection argument is genuinely accessible.

Materials

For each participating table: two distinguishable sets of four labeled tokens, eight complete preference strips with four rank positions each, eight blank replacement strips, four removable pairing links, sixteen small “already proposed” markers, paper, and pencil. Use large icons with consistent labels, at least 20 mm across, so preference orders can be read aloud without writing. For smaller markets remove the unused labels from EVERY preference strip; missing labels or tied ranks change the theorem. Holders must be visibly tentative, and rejected tokens must be returned to the unmatched pool. All preferences are public and fixed throughout an instance. Keep a record of proposals without requiring children to hold that history in memory. Match artificial labels or objects; do not solicit or rank real children's friendships, desirability, or personal preferences. Every object belongs to exactly one side and each match crosses sides. A unilateral wish to switch is not a blocking pair; both preferences must be checked. No lotteries, capacities greater than one, incomplete lists, or roommates model are silently included.
