## Mathematical destination and assumptions

For a finite connected undirected graph with strictly positive whole-number edge purchase prices, the least-total-cost subset connecting every vertex is a **minimum spanning tree** (MST). A purchase price is paid once per included edge, even if a traveler uses it many times. All places are fixed; only whole printed links may be bought; unmarked crossings are not places. Children optimize one connected purchased network, not an individual route. The concrete positive prices make removal of a loop strictly cheaper.

The MST algorithms work with equal prices. Equal prices can yield several optima, but do not force nonuniqueness. Distinct prices guarantee uniqueness. Negative weights are meaningful for abstract MST optimization but would allow a minimum connected subgraph to include a profitable extra cycle; zero weights may leave zero-cost cycles in a cheapest connected purchase. Thus the “every cheapest connecting purchase is a tree” claim uses strictly positive costs. Disconnected available graphs cannot connect all places; available-link connectivity must be checked before giving a feasible task. No Steiner points or unprinted new links are allowed.

## Proofs and certificates

**Cycle deletion and edge count.** An edge on a cycle can be removed while its endpoints remain joined by the rest of the cycle; therefore the whole connected network stays connected and its positive cost decreases. The cheapest network is acyclic. Start with n isolated places: each forest edge reduces the number of components by exactly one, giving n−1 edges when all become connected. The same edge count by itself is not a connectivity test.

**Safe cut exchange.** Suppose chosen forest F is contained in an optimal tree T. A cut partitions the places into two nonempty sides; assume no edge of F crosses it. Let e be any lowest-price available crossing edge. If e is not in T, add e; its unique new loop crosses the cut in another edge f. As no F edge crosses, f is not in F. Since e is cheapest across the cut, price(e)≤price(f). Replace f by e. The result is a spanning tree still containing F, with cost no greater than T, hence optimal. This proves that a cheapest link joining current components is safe and proves Kruskal by induction. “Always buy the cheapest link” is missing the different-components condition. “Choose everyone's nearest neighbor” can leave groups disconnected.

**Forced and forbidden choices.** If e is strictly cheaper than every other crossing edge of a cut, a tree omitting it becomes strictly cheaper after the preceding exchange, so every optimum contains it. If e is strictly more expensive than every other edge of a cycle, removing e from a tree that uses it leaves two components; the remaining cycle has another edge f between them, with price(f)<price(e). Swapping makes a cheaper tree. Thus every optimum omits e. With ties, say “safe for some compatible optimum” and “an optimum can omit it,” rather than claiming all optima make that choice. A required/forbidden argument must name an actual cut/cycle on the printed graph.

**A candidate-tree certificate.** A connected acyclic candidate T is optimal exactly when, for every unused available edge f, all prices on the T-path between f's endpoints are ≤price(f). Necessity: otherwise add f and remove a more expensive path edge. For sufficiency, deleting any candidate edge e splits T into two parts. Every available crossing edge f outside T has e on its T-path and therefore costs at least e. Starting from any competing tree T′ missing e, add e and remove an edge f from the resulting loop that crosses this cut. This f cannot be another edge of T; the exchange does not increase cost and increases agreement with T. Repeat until the competing tree becomes T. This supplies a complete certificate without enumerating every network or teaching formal algorithms. The proof is an adult/readiness continuation; comparing one actual exchange has much lower prerequisites.

**Uniqueness under distinct prices.** If two optimal trees differ, take the cheapest edge e in their symmetric difference, assumed in T1. Add e to T2. Its cycle has an edge f in T2 but not T1, otherwise T1 already contains a cycle. Distinct prices and the choice of e give price(e)<price(f). Replacing f makes T2 cheaper, a contradiction. Equal costs do not allow the strict inequality.

- Five places, AB=BC=AC=1, CD=DE=2, CE=3, AD=5, BE=6: every optimum uses CD and DE, plus two of the three triangle links; there are three optimum trees, each cost 6. Buying the first four cheapest available links can leave E out.

The cheaper whole network can make an A-to-place journey longer than the direct available link; this is a contrast clarifying the objective, not a new shortest-path investigation.
- Triangle AB=1, BC=AC=2: ties produce two MSTs. Triangle AB=AC=1, BC=2: ties still produce a unique MST. Distinct-price sufficiency and equal-price nonuniqueness should not be confused.
- Four places, AB=1, BC=2, CD=3, DA=4, AC=5: candidate AB,DA,AC costs 10. Adding BC and removing AC lowers it to 7; adding CD and removing DA lowers it to 6, the optimum. Every step changes one recoverable purchased network, not a moving reference state during a simultaneous comparison.
