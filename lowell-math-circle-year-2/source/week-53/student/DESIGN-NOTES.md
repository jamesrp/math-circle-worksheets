# Design scope and prerequisites

The object is one purchased network that connects **every** circled place, minimizing the sum of its bought-link prices. Prices are paid once per link. Trip length, walk steps, geometric line length and the number of bought links are not optimization targets. Only whole printed links and circled places are available; the unmarked crossing on page 9 is not a junction.

The combined sequence offers entry without equal band quotas. Pages 1–2 can support K–1 with an adult reading, paying/refunding and recording while children choose links and move a pawn to check reachability. This is a new, unpiloted younger adaptation. It is not labeled independent K–1 work and is not validated by the source's age 9+ recommendation. Young access consists of genuine edge choices, all-place connectivity and comparing concrete payment piles.

Pages 1–4: read a short prompt and letter labels, count dot prices 1–3, then read numerals 1–6 and add/count candidate totals. An adult can externalize arithmetic and recording. Reasoning progresses from making a connected purchase to cheaper alternatives and a checkable collection of tied optima. The small triangles contrast one cheapest choice with two. The four-place map in Problem 3 defeats merely buying the three cheapest links. Problem 4 has tied triangle choices plus forced links to two additional places. The compact maps are records; they are not intended for pawn play.

Pages 5–9: retain one unchanged reference purchase, compare integer prices, follow a path, and judge which exchanges preserve all-place connection. The reader must distinguish “every cheapest purchase,” “some cheapest purchase” and “none.” Problem 7 inverts optimization by assigning prices. The arbitrary-map optimality and uniqueness arguments in Problems 8–9 are readiness-dependent continuations; they can support a later visit. A child need not finish all pages in an hour. No sorting algorithm is supplied.

## Physical implementation constraints

Five reusable pair/trio kits match the supplied eleven-child, three-table context. Each kit has 30 equal-value counters approximately 12–15 mm across, ten purchased-link markers, a pawn with a small base (or a pointing finger), a dry-erase sleeve/tracing sheet and a marker. Counters go in an off-map payment tray; they are not laid along printed links. A purchased link has one on/off marker or bold trace. The bold map is the recoverable purchase record; count its cost after the candidate is complete. Returning a link refunds its printed price.

All fixed available-price sums are at most 26 and all fixed maps have at most nine links. The price-design maps allow six prices chosen independently from 1, 2, 3 and 4, so at most 24 counters and six markers suffice. Ten markers and 30 counters therefore also allow a child to buy every available link before returning any. Five kits use 150 counters and 50 markers. The planned shared demonstration adds 15 counters and four markers.

The payment/connectivity convention is shown before Problem 1 on a non-task X/Y/Z map: available links → XY and YZ bought and paid for (2+4=6) → a kept connected record. This purchase is deliberately not the cheapest on that map. The same dot prices also show their numerals before numerals are used alone. Page 5 shows the new return-and-buy operation on another non-task instance: cost 6 → buy UW for 3, cost 9 → return VW for 4, cost 5. Neither example supplies the complete optimum catalog for a numbered problem.

Main working maps have vertex circles 10.4 mm in diameter. On pages 1–6 and 9, nearest-place spacing is at least 51.0 mm. The price-design maps on page 7 have about 40 mm nearest-place spacing; the smaller page-8 working cases have 37.5 mm. Compact purchase records use 5.6 mm circles and smaller spacing and should receive pen traces only. The convention pictures are also compact; for the common launch the adult should draw/enlarge X/Y/Z to a working width of about 15 cm. This takes no specialized fabrication. Approximate printing/sleeving/kit preparation remains a 10–15 minute design estimate, not measured preparation time.

No physical pretest or classroom pilot was performed. In particular, the proposed pawn/marker fit, clutter when many links are bought, payment/refund handling and whether children keep the choices after the shared launch are untested. Digital verification does not establish these properties. Any later guide must carry these limits forward and distinguish proposed use from recorded classroom use.
