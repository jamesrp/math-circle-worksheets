Week 15: Nearest-site regions

Mathematical kernels

1. Equal distance makes a straight boundary. Work in the flat plane with straight-line distance and fixed, distinct point sites. For two sites A and B, the points equally far from them form the perpendicular bisector of AB; the two half-planes contain the points nearer A and nearer B. Folding A onto B produces this line, and reflection explains equality on it; comparison of the reflected distances explains which site wins on each side. A nearest-site region is the intersection of the half-planes in which that site is at least as close as every competitor, so it is convex: the straight segment between any two points in the region stays there. Boundaries are shared ties, not gaps or arbitrarily assigned winners. The mathematical object is the entire continuous region, not merely whichever sampled dots happened to be tested. Measuring several dots gives evidence; the bisector/half-plane argument certifies all points.

2. A third competitor can erase a two-site boundary. Only portions of pairwise bisectors on which no other site is closer belong to the nearest-site map. For A=(−2,0), B=(2,0), C=(0,2), the three closed regions are A: x≤0 and y≤−x; B: x≥0 and y≤x; C: y≥|x|. The A/B tie line x=0 survives only for y≤0, and the three nearest regions meet at (0,0). In particular, (0,1) ties A and B but belongs only to C's region, while (0,−1) really lies on the A/B boundary. These coordinates are facilitator checks, not required student notation. Adding a new site can only shrink each old site's region, because it adds one more distance comparison. With four sites at (±2,±2), the old regions are the four quadrants, with a four-way tie at the origin; adding a site at the origin gives that new site the diamond |x|+|y|≤2. Thus “exactly three regions meet” requires a general-position condition and is false for the square arrangement. Folded comparisons, overlapping allowed sides, and explicit points can establish these geometric facts without coordinate algebra. Delaunay triangulations, squared-distance formulas, and generic-position theory are optional adult context, not prerequisites or required extensions.

Sources: David M. Mount, University of Maryland CMSC 754, Fall 2021, Lecture 10, *Voronoi Diagrams*, pp. 1–3 (nearest-site definition, bisector halfspaces, convex cells, empty-circle ties, and the stated non-cocircularity hypothesis), https://www.cs.umd.edu/class/fall2021/cmsc754/Lects/lect10-vor.pdf. The source uses open cells; this outline uses their closures so children can keep shared boundaries visibly. The stated coordinate arrangements and insertion example are independent small checks, not copied source exercises.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1: compare actual lengths to fixed sites and explore where a change of nearest site occurs, using folds and spoken rules; no number reading or ruler arithmetic required.
Grades 2–3: build and inspect nearest-site regions from competing distance comparisons, especially true ties versus ties beaten by a third site; drawing and length comparison suffice.
Grades 4–5: reason about whole regions, their convexity, and what can change when a site is added; half-plane intersection can be explained with drawings rather than algebra.

Materials

For ten children, prepare 10 pencils and erasers, 10 straightedges, 10 lengths of non-stretch string about 12 inches long, 40 sheets of tracing paper or other translucent foldable paper, and 20 additional blank US Letter sheets. Supply 3 distinct colored pencils per child if available; labels or different hatching can substitute, so the mathematics must never depend on color alone. These are preparation quantities, not verified inventory. Site dots must have clearly marked centers; distances are measured to the centers, not the edges of a sticker or large icon. Working maps should be about 5–6 inches across, leave room for folds and straight boundaries, and be large enough to compare strings without fine measurement. Sites remain fixed within one map. A printed rectangular frame is just the part of the plane being shown; if a task studies the frame as a bounded region, state that explicitly. Ordinary straight-line distance is used throughout, not grid-step travel distance. Pages print single-sided on US Letter at 100%; coordinates, if used by the writer, must be rendered with equal horizontal and vertical scales. Folding and string comparison are exploratory tools; diagrams and exact symmetry must settle intended ties rather than small printing or measurement errors.
