Week 16: Three-color triangles

Mathematical kernels

1. Boundary rules force a three-color small triangle. Divide one large triangle into a finite, edge-to-edge collection of smaller triangles: cells meet only at an entire common edge or a vertex, with no holes, gaps, or unmatched T-junctions. Give the large triangle's corners three different labels, R, B, and Y. Every vertex on side RB may be only R or B; similarly side BY permits only B or Y and side YR only Y or R. Interior vertices may have any label. Sperner's lemma says that the number of small triangles whose three vertices have all three labels is odd, so at least one exists. Labels belong to vertices, not to the triangular faces. The restriction is on the whole outer side, not just its end corners. In the standard equal-spacing subdivision with two small edges on each large side, all 8 permitted colorings have exactly one three-color cell. With three small edges per large side, the 192 permitted colorings have 1, 3, or 5 three-color cells: respectively 108, 72, and 12 colorings. These finite checks are examples, not the proof for arbitrary triangulations. This is Sperner's lemma about a triangulated triangle, distinct from Sperner's antichain theorem in the existing atlas.

2. Doors turn the coloring guarantee into a path and a parity argument. Call an edge with labels R and B at its ends a door. Along the large RB side, the label begins R and ends B, so the number of changes, and hence boundary doors, is odd; the other two sides have none. A small triangle has exactly one door if it has all three labels, exactly two if it uses R and B but not Y, and otherwise none. Counting doors once from each adjoining triangle gives T+2U=B+2I, where T counts three-color triangles, U counts two-door triangles, B boundary doors, and I interior doors. Thus T and B have the same parity, and T is odd. This equality can be shown by pairing actual door incidences, without symbolic algebra. For a concrete search, enter a boundary door and keep leaving a two-door triangle through its other door. A finite nonbranching path ends at a three-color triangle or at another boundary door. A chosen entrance need not find a three-color triangle; boundary-to-boundary paths pair entrances, and the odd number of entrances guarantees an unpaired route reaches one. All-boundary exits cannot account for an odd number. A path or parity explanation is the elementary mathematical destination; no fixed-point theorem, limits, or rent-division assumptions are required.

Counts for the standard two- and three-step meshes are independently enumerated for this outline.

Suggested emphasis by level: K–1: place and change vertex labels under visible side restrictions and search for unavoidable all-three cells, with spoken rules and no arithmetic prerequisite.
Grades 2–3: explore why changing legal colors does not remove the last all-three cell, using R/B doors and concrete paths; recognizing odd/even through pairing is enough.
Grades 4–5: connect the boundary changes to the complete path/parity certificate, including paths that exit again and the importance of every boundary condition; symbolic equations are optional.

Materials

For ten children, prepare 10 sets of three colored pencils (R, B, Y), 10 graphite pencils and erasers, and at least 20 extra blank US Letter sheets. For the three youngest children, prepare 3 reusable sets of counters, each containing 15 of each of the three labels (135 counters total); use about 0.4-inch counters and printed vertex spacing of at least 0.65 inches. These are preparation requirements, not verified supplies. Counter boards must have at most 15 vertices unless more counters and room are supplied. Every color must also have a distinct letter or simple symbol so labels can be distinguished in grayscale and by children who do not distinguish the colors. Fix the three outer corner labels and show the permitted pair along each complete side. Working meshes should occupy about 5–6 inches across and have generous vertex dots without obscuring edges. All subdivided edges must match across adjacent triangles; a vertex where another edge ends must be a vertex on both sides. For tracing door paths, leave room inside each triangle and across the middle of its edges. Pages print single-sided on US Letter at 100%. Actual counter placement, recoloring, and path tracing are the concrete actions; coloring entire triangle faces would change the object and lose the theorem.
