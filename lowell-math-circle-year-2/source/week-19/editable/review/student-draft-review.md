# Independent adversarial review

## Verdict

The packet is mathematically sound and visually clean. I found no incorrect deck, false optimum, impossible requested construction, clipping, or overlap. It needs a small task-design pass rather than a mathematical rewrite. The most concrete departures from the organizer's standard are the very short, separately numbered four-card row tasks and the overt proof-method cue in grades 4–5 Problem 5. Keep the successful diagrams, contrasting starting collections, and substantial final questions.

## Revisions, in priority order

### 1. Combine the undersized four-card row tasks with their eight-card counterparts

**Locations:** K–1 page 4, Problems 6–7; grades 2–3 page 3, Problems 4–5.

The four-card row task is a complete mathematical question, but it is unlikely to supply the required five minutes of experimenting on its own. By this point children have already studied the entire two-symbol deck and the incompatible singleton cards. Putting the empty card, one singleton, and the two-symbol card together leaves only the other singleton. There is very little remaining search, especially for the actual third-grade group.

The eight-card case is a useful extension and is already immediately adjacent. Make these two cases one substantial numbered problem instead of treating the first as a separate five-minute task. Preserve the open workspace and the permission for a card to stand alone. Do not replace this with a diagram containing two or three preallocated rows. This is a local numbering/task-size repair; the packet as a whole has enough mathematical content for the hour.

### 2. Remove or soften the explicit proof-method cue

**Location:** grades 4–5 page 4, Problem 5: “Explain how a complete arrangement in rows limits the size of every collection from that deck.”

This announces the key relationship that the child is meant to discover. The child is left to justify the announced method, but no longer to decide how the new arrangement might settle the original maximum problem. That is the most direct tension with “The method belongs to the child” and the instruction to leave solution ideas to the adults.

The surrounding construction problems are not themselves worked answers: children still have to find their own chain partitions, and no target number of rows is supplied. Keep that distinction. The narrowly targeted revision is to remove this standalone cue and let the later request for an explanation ruling out every larger collection carry the connection. An adult can supply this question orally if needed. If the writer deliberately retains it as a separate mathematical objective, recognize that this is a more guided version of the lesson than the organizer's stated default.

### 3. Tighten the youngest band's row instructions

**Locations:** K–1 page 4, Problems 6–7.

These comply with the literal two-sentence limit, but Problem 6's first sentence combines using every card, minimizing the number of rows, and testing containment between successive cards. It is long and nested for a task that must work after one hearing. Problem 7 immediately repeats most of the same explanation.

While combining the two cases, simplify the sentence structure and state the row rule once. Keep “all” and the minimization goal explicit, and retain singleton rows as allowed. Do not add an example chain or a numbered plan in order to explain the wording.

### 4. Small wording and workspace polish, not blockers

- **Grades 4–5 page 4, Problem 5:** If retained, “that deck” has a singular referent after a problem displaying two decks. Say explicitly whether the explanation is requested for both decks or for either one.
- **Grades 4–5 page 4, Problem 5:** It has only about an inch of comfortable writing space below its two-line prompt. An oral explanation is realistic in this setting, and a short written explanation can fit, so this is not a layout failure. Removing or relocating the cue would also remove this cramped allocation.
- **K–1 page 1:** The four three-card groups are distinguished by spacing alone. They are readable as four horizontal cases, and the contrasts are good. Preserve that separation in any reflow; merging them into a tighter array would make “each group” less clear.

## Mathematical checks

In the following review-only notation, A is the circle, B the triangle, C the square, and D the star. This notation need not be added to the student pages.

- The four-, eight-, and sixteen-card deck diagrams each contain every subset exactly once, including the empty card. The displayed order does not pre-sort the deck into ranks or give away a chain partition.
- The packet's definition forbids containment, not overlap. K–1 Problem 1's third group, AB / AC / BC, is a particularly useful check that shared symbols are permitted. Its four displayed case maxima are 2, 2, 3, and 2.
- For the two-symbol deck there are exactly five nonempty legal collections: four one-card collections and the collection consisting of A and B. This validates K–1 Problem 2 and grades 2–3 Problem 1, including their deliberate exclusion of the zero-card collection.
- For the three-symbol deck the maximum is 3. There are exactly two maximum collections: A / B / C and AB / AC / BC. There are nine legal two-card collections. This validates the youngest band's enumeration and “different collection just as big” tasks, and the middle band's request for every largest collection.
- The three-symbol starting collections ABC; A; AB; and A / BC extend to maxima of 1, 3, 3, and 2 respectively. The latter is a useful nontrivial locally unextendable two-card collection. The illustrated starting collections are all legal.
- Removing any one of the eight three-symbol cards still leaves a legal three-card collection. The two disjoint maximum collections above establish this directly, so K–1 Problem 9 is true for every removal, including the empty and full cards.
- Complete chain partitions exist with 2, 3, and 6 rows for the two-, three-, and four-symbol decks. Each row really is nested, and the certificates in the source cover every card exactly once. Since the packet also has legal collections of those sizes, the row minima and collection maxima agree in all the assigned cases.
- The maximum for the four-symbol deck is 6, with exactly one maximum collection: all six two-symbol cards. Independent exhaustive enumeration found 168 legal collections including the zero-card collection. Thus grades 4–5 Problem 9 has a definite, manageable target; it is not an open-ended search for many unrelated six-card families.
- The four-symbol starting collections ABCD; A / BCD; and AB / AC extend to maxima of 1, 2, and 6 respectively. The contrast in grades 4–5 Problem 3 is valid and strong.
- With A required, the maximum is 4. Two examples are A / B / C / D and A / BC / BD / CD. The earlier three-symbol problem gives a natural way to establish the upper bound: any other chosen card must avoid A. This makes grades 4–5 Problem 8 a substantive extension rather than merely another unconstrained maximum search.
- No student page claims the general form of Sperner's theorem from these small cases, introduces factorials or binomial notation, or confuses this theorem with the triangulation-labeling result.

The certificates are not printed as answers, and the sixteen-card construction problems explicitly require using every card exactly once. Keep both features.
