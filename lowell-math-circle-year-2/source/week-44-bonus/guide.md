# Mathematical overview

Start R0,B0 and choose uniformly among current counter identities, returning every draw. In the usual copying rule, two red-draw totals 0,1,2 have probabilities 2/6,2/6,2/6. If each returned draw instead adds one counter of the OTHER color, those totals have probabilities 1/6,4/6,1/6. With no addition they have probabilities 1/4,2/4,1/4. In this two-draw comparison, opposite reinforcement promotes switching and balanced draw totals; copying promotes repeating. No general balance law follows from this one comparison. The six add-a-counter histories each have probability (1/2)(1/3), while the four no-add histories each have (1/2)(1/2). Equal histories must be compared within their own mechanism, then via ratios; the different raw denominators cannot be ignored.

Under copying, after a history with r red draws and b blue draws, the current bag is (r+1)R,(b+1)B. The next red chance is (r+1)/(r+b+2), not the original 1/2. The order does not affect this forecast because the count bag is the same. After four draws the red chance is 1/6,2/6,3/6,4/6 or 5/6. Extremal design is attainable by BBBB or RRRR; a tied forecast requires two of each color. Different orders with the same totals give the same next-draw prediction. This is dependence across draws, not evidence for independent trials with a fixed p.

Start one counter of each of three colors and copy the drawn color. After n draws the added-count vector (r,b,g) ranges over all nonnegative triples summing to n. Each vector has chance 1/C(n+2,2). Each particular word has probability 2r!b!g!/(n+2)!, so words need not be equally likely. There are (n+2)!/2 equal marked-identity histories and n! histories per vector. For n=2: twelve histories, six vectors, two each. For n=3: sixty histories, ten vectors, six each. The final bag counts are (r+1,b+1,g+1); original colors never vanish. The theorem assumes one initial counter per color, one same-color addition per returned draw and uniform identity selection. Other initial bags or feedback rules can give different laws.

Grades 2-3 can compare short feedback rules and make concrete next-draw bags. Grades 4-5 can sort three-color histories, explain equal vector weights and extend the count law. K-1 can enact the opposite-color addition and build strongest/weakest/tied bags with adult reading and counting to six; the complete marked probability comparison remains a readiness-dependent continuation. Experiments produce conjectures; exact marked histories and count-product arguments establish the chance claims.

# Problem 1 (page 1)

For copying the complete identity histories are (R0,R0),(R0,R1),(R0,B0),(B0,R0),(B0,B0),(B0,B1). The middle two give one red and one blue draw, hence 2/6=1/3.

For opposite reinforcement: (R0,R0) has two red draws; (R0,B0),(R0,B1),(B0,R0),(B0,R1) have one red; (B0,B0) has zero red. These six are equally likely. The no-add column has two spare lines; the six printed lines in a column do not assert six legal histories under every rule. Thus one of each has chance 4/6=2/3, greatest among the three rules. Note that added counter B1 exists after R0, and added R1 exists after B0. The complete no-add stories are RR,RB,BR,BB, all equally likely, giving one of each in 2/4=1/2. Fractions can be compared using six and four equally weighted models or an adult-supported common denominator; raw successes 4,2,2 alone are not a chance proof.

With opposite reinforcement final bags and draw totals reverse the usual copying correspondence: RR ends 1R/3B, RB/BR end 2R/2B, BB ends 3R/1B. All original colors persist under every rule. Hints: after the first R, inspect which identities are available next; keep the two additions visibly different. Extension: compare three draws and ask whether the opposite rule always favors the most balanced totals; do not claim a general law from two draws.

New versus base: base middle/upper Problem 3/2 compares copying with no additions. The new mechanism is opposite-color reinforcement and its balance-promoting distribution. The familiar two controls support the new comparison and are not separately counted as investigations.

# Problem 2 (page 2)

Strongest red: RRRR, final 5R/1B, next red 5/6. Weakest red: BBBB, final 1R/5B, next red 1/6. Tied: any of RRBB,RBRB,RBBR,BRRB,BRBR,BBRR, final 3R/3B, next red 1/2. None can forecast certainty because both original colors persist.

Other attainable forecasts are 4R/2B (2/3) and 2R/4B (1/3). For example RRRB and RRBR give the first, RBBB and BRBB the second. The extreme forecast has only one four-draw color story; every intermediate forecast has multiple orders. Children may give “five of the six counters” rather than symbolic fractions. A live partner bet can test the executable forecast but cannot establish its exact probability through a small sample.

Hint: put the current counters in a visible row and ask how many would win the next red bet. Extension: after a red next draw, update a formerly tied bag and its forecast; show that later evidence changes the prediction again. These are continuations within the forecasting investigation.

New versus base: the base enumerates finishing bags and equal-count story chances. This reverses the direction to design a predictive bag and forecast the NEXT random draw conditional on the current state, with extremal and tied targets.

# Problem 3 (page 3)

The twelve printed stories are all original identities available after the first draw plus that draw's one new copy. Each has probability 1/(3*4). The six final bags, in R/B/G order, are (3,1,1),(1,3,1),(1,1,3),(2,2,1),(2,1,2),(1,2,2). Each gets two identity histories. A repeated word such as RR has two histories (R0,R0),(R0,R1); a mixed word RG has one, but its reversed word GR adds the second history to the same final bag.

After three draws, added vectors are permutations of (3,0,0), of (2,1,0), and (1,1,1): three plus six plus one = ten. Their final counts add one to every coordinate. Each vector has six of the sixty marked histories. For (3,0,0), the one color word has 1*2*3=6 identity histories; for a (2,1,0) vector, three word orders each have 2!=2 histories; for (1,1,1), six distinct orders each have one history. Those three types cover all nonnegative triples summing to three.

General adult explanation: for fixed vector (r,b,g), each color word has the same product r!b!g! of available-color choices. There are n!/(r!b!g!) words, so multiplying gives n! marked histories per vector. Total histories are 3*4*...*(n+2)=(n+2)!/2, yielding 1/C(n+2,2). Children can work with paired rearrangements and grouped small cases; factorial notation is not required.

Hints: ignore identity labels only AFTER reconstructing the final bag; keep a counter of each original color in the tray record. Extension: arrange the vectors as points in a triangular count board; ask what neighboring points mean when one added color is changed. This is an optional next representation, not required alongside concurrent draw tallies.

New versus base: the base proves the binary total law, including arbitrary n. Three colors introduce a two-dimensional count vector, triangular number of states and equal weight across count bags despite unequal ordered-word weights.
