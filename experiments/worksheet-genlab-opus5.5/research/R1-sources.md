# R1: How good math-circle problem sheets are written (notes from the reference sources)

Scope: K–5, one-hour sessions, small groups with mathematicians nearby.
Read on the device: `external-resources/` and `lowell-math-circle-year-1/` only. I did not open year-2, plans/, tmp/ or experiments/.

Legend. **[S]** means the source says or shows this, and quotes are verbatim. **[I]** means it is my inference. Locations: Rozhkovskaya = "Roz L#" (Lesson number; "AtL" = its "At the lesson" section) or "Roz M.#" (Manhattan part). Bay = *Math Circle by the Bay*, cited by printed page where known, else "PDF p." EFM = Early Family Math. JRMF = Julia Robinson Math Festival guides. MfL = Math for Love (Finkel). Lowell = organizer's year-1 handouts ("Lowell PS#.#" = Problem Set.problem).

What I read:
- Rozhkovskaya, *Math Circles for Elementary School Students*: the full text (15 Berkeley lessons, all "At the lesson" notes, and the 13 Manhattan chapters).
- Bay: the preface, all 93 "Teacher" notes, chapters 1–2 in part, and chapter 6 (warmups and challenges).
- *A Decade of the BMC*: the introduction only.
- MfL: the remote-curriculum letter, the Pilgrim's Puzzle lesson, and about 6 of the Hindu columns.
- NYT Numberplay: the Broken Calculator, Polka Dot, Squirrel-Frog, Hopscotch, Circle-Toss, Balance and Primo columns.
- EFM: both introductions, about 8 games and Puzzles of the Week A–F.
- JRMF: about 8 guides, including the Map Coloring and River Crossings teaching guides and 3 beginner versions.
- Beast Academy: front matter and a scan of the instructions only, because the OCR is poor.
- Lowell: all 11 DOCX handouts, the pattern-block PDF and the subtract-shapes .tex.

Not read: Imbalance Problems, Smullyan, the UW olympiad files and the advanced-math PDFs.

## 1. Source-by-source notes

### 1.1 Rozhkovskaya: BMC-Elementary 2009 (grades 1–3, plus some 5-year-olds) and Kansas "Level I"

**Setting [S].**
- About 40 kids in grades 1–3, split into two groups of 20 on identical plans. Two extra instructors attended, and parents could stay "under the condition of their active involvement" (Intro).
- "along with five-year-old kids who worked with their parents were students with very solid math skills" (L3 AtL§1).
- Stankova's foreword: her daughter joined "as a kindergartner".

**Problem format [S].**
- A lesson is 2–5 short topic blocks under plain headings ("Secret code", "Problems with matchsticks", "Pizza"). Each block has numbered "Problem L.k." items.
- There are 5–12 numbered problems per lesson. Statement lengths run from about 10 to 80 words, with a median of roughly 30 (my rough count).
- Rules are given as dash lists. Example from Problem 3.1: "– Cow and Cat took the aisle seats. – Dog's seat number and Cow's seat number were bigger than Horse's seat number. Place the animals on the chairs of the row."
- Asks are direct questions or imperatives: "Who wins in this game and why?" (7.1); "Is it possible to put together a square out of one hundred little squares?" (4.3); "Code your name. Write a coded letter to your friend (and teach him or her how to read it)." (2.4).
- Named characters carry the problems: Mister Jameson's sausages, Signor Primavero's pizza, Mister Spy, the clowns Yep and Nope, the gnomes, Mister Johnson's ice-cream cart.
- Hints are explicit and concrete: "Hint. Put three pegs of one color and three pegs of another color on the stones in the picture." (11.6); "Hint. What do we usually write at the very end of a letter?" (2.7).

**Amount of work and fast finishers [S].**
- "for every lesson, I prepared additional problems for those participants who quickly completed the main task. As a rule, the additional problems were not discussed in class." (L3 AtL§1)
- Many items are marked "not discussed in class" or "left as a homework": 3.2, 3.10, 4.6, 6.5, 7.4–7.5, 8.2, and most of 11.6–11.8. Roughly a third to a half of each lesson's numbered problems were never debriefed. [count is mine]
- L1: "We only completed one or two drawings of knots at the lesson, which was fewer than I intended. The second stage proved difficult."
- Typical rhythm: "Usually students spent a few minutes trying to solve the problem themselves, and after that we discussed the problem on the board." (L3 AtL§1)

**What worked [S].**
- Explicit procedures: "The activities with explicit algorithms (such as this one) never caused any difficulties for our participants. It is recommended that the kids keep track of completed steps of the code by crossing out the arrows" (L2 AtL§1).
- Listening rather than reading: "not everyone in the class was yet a fluent reader! … They had to understand sophisticated logic situations and complicated rules just by listening, and they did this with great success!" (L2 AtL§2)
- Guaranteed-success manipulative puzzles: frogs with marbles. "In such a version it is impossible to fail to solve the problem… everyone who starts to think about the problem is guaranteed to get satisfaction from being able to solve it." (L11 AtL§3)
- Logic grids: the knights and dragon lesson "went very smoothly… participants not only answered each question correctly, but they provided very clear and well-organized arguments" (L12 AtL§1).
- A 'baby' Bulls-and-Cows game: "the level of difficulty turned out to be exactly right for the young mathematicians" (L15 AtL§1).
- Games even after the outcome was known: asked "Would you like to play this game with your friends?", "the kids enthusiastically chorused 'Yes!'" (L7 AtL§1).
- Physical, social endings: drawing circles at the board ("for some parents it was difficult to take their kids home!", L4), K'nex polygons (L7), paper snowflakes (L12, L14).
- Warm-up scaffolding of a "fewest questions" problem: one hidden marble and one question, then up to 4 marbles and two questions, before 8 marbles and three (L10 AtL§3).

**What did not work [S].**
- Long reading: "This age group does not enjoy reading long texts, and for this reason we limited exercises with homonyms to Problem 4.5." (L4 AtL§3)
- An unclear ask: Flower pots, where "We spent a significant amount of time trying to understand the question of the problem." (L6 AtL§3)
- Trick questions: "Since then, I try to avoid problems 'with hidden tricks' at the math circles for young students." First graders "carefully read every word… honestly climbed with the caterpillar each foot" (L6 AtL§4).
- Added precision: a numeric version of the two-board bridge "would not be suitable… the introduced numbers could confuse students"; the expected answer stayed a picture (L5 AtL§4).
- Geometry "theorem experiments": "I cannot recall any case where any of the kids were much surprised by this remarkable fact." Parents liked them and kids did not. Also: "first through fourth graders comprehend algebraic abstractions easier than geometric concepts." (L6 AtL§4)
- Notation: powers "produced even more confusion" (L3 AtL§2).
- Clerical load: "writing numbers in the cells of the table took a lot of time… some kids rigorously colored every cell… Soon the class was split into two groups" (L8 AtL§2). Pizza: "It is a challenging task to draw neat straight lines… so we considered only at most four cuts." (L9)
- A game that ran too long: "it would have been better to make the lanes shorter—six or seven boxes instead of ten." (L15)
- Rules that are hard to police: in the "A not A" game, "soon we gave up the rules" (L9).
- Missing definitions: forgetting to define even and odd for group 2 meant "some students… could not progress" (L5 AtL§1).
- Cultural assumptions: bunny versus hare, and bear hibernation (L10). Santa as a "random" chooser: "Santa Claus never gives gifts randomly" (L13).
- Materials: craft sticks were too big for the desks, so the matchstick problems were dropped (L5). With no mirrors, imagining two mirrors was too hard (L12).
- Reading on a tired day: "I retold the problem in my own words and stressed the hidden information" (L8 AtL§1).
- Kids solved logic puzzles "in their heads, but they could not explain their answer… A table is more illustrative than a long explanation in words." (L3 AtL§1)

**Sequencing [S].**
- Play the game first, then smaller cases ("Who wins in the game with two points? With four points?… six?… eight?"), then a guess, then a strategy argument (L7).
- Physical squares, then the sequence, then "Do you see any patterns?", then "one hundred little squares?" (L4).
- A whole chapter on impossibility (M.12 "No solutions"): "This is like playing hide-and-seek in a room where no one is hiding." It is followed by short "Is it possible to put marbles in three boxes so that…" items. Students also make problems (M.4 "Make your own problem", M.3.6, M.9.3).

**Withholding [S].** "I avoid giving this classical problem [1+…+100] … to leave the participants the opportunity to find the solution on their own" (L9 AtL§1).

### 1.2 *Math Circle by the Bay* (Givental, Nemirovskaya, Zakharevich; grades 1–5)

**Setting [S].** "Weekly sessions are 50–55 minutes long, with 25–30 students (and two instructors) per group", in groups for grades 1–2 and 3–4 (Preface p. viii).

**How they teach [S] (Preface pp. ix–x).**
- "we vary the pace and topics by interlacing the main theme with warmup problems, discussion of the homework, and occasionally hands-on projects… Warmups also help to cope with late arrivals. We keep a stack of challenging problems for the kids who are ahead of the rest of the class."
- **"It is very important to balance two conflicting requirements: making problem statements unambiguous for the children and at the same time easy to understand. Presenting problems as funny or even silly stories excites the kids and keeps them engaged. Most of our problems have names or characters associated with them; when we later address such problems by these names, the kids easily recall them. We always have a bag of manipulatives."**
- "Quite a few kids need to overcome their fear of making a mistake." Their inside joke: "I made a mistake. I am done with the first step of solving the problem."
- "Many kids struggle with verbalizing their ideas, even after successfully solving the problem."

**Young versus old [S].**
- "In many cases we cover the same material in grades 1–2 … as in grades 3–4 …, although at a slower pace and in lesser depth. Surprisingly, … the difference between the older and the younger groups is not that significant." (p. x)
- "even the first and second graders were writing such monstrous fractions as 1/256 without hesitation" (PDF p. 120).
- Against that: "The next four problems are easily solved by older children, but are surprisingly difficult for many younger ones" (age problems, PDF p. 164). "For younger students, who are not comfortable with multiplication, this problem should be simplified" (Pythagoras).
- They do not budget time: "we do not believe it makes sense to estimate how much time a particular topic will take." Five themes take at least two years, so the pace is slow and deep.

**Statement format [S].**
- "Problem 2.4. Four Ice Cream Flavors. Ann can put two scoops of ice cream in her cone side-by-side: [picture] She must select two distinct flavors out of vanilla, chocolate, pistachio, and strawberry. How many different ice cream cones can she make?" Each problem is numbered, named and 1–3 sentences long.
- Teacher note: "Without the picture above many kids place the scoops one on top of the other" (PDF p. 41). The picture exists to remove an ambiguity.

**Word choice [S].**
- "We prefer not to use the word 'proof' and use the word 'explanation' instead" (PDF p. 23).
- "We use the words SAME SHAPE SAME SIZE instead [of congruent]" (PDF p. 120).
- "We intentionally do not use words 3-by-3-by-3 cube because some children use it to compute the answer without understanding the geometry" (PDF p. 31).
- "we number rows and diagonals… starting with one instead of the usual zero" (PDF p. 88).
- "We never write such long expressions… Instead we break the calculations into parts" (PDF p. 144).
- "Usually when we introduce mathematical terms in class we do not expect that the kids would remember them… The word isomorphic is an exception: the students love this word."
- For a cube, the word "side" is ambiguous, so they insist on "edge" and "face".

**Hints held back [S].** "We do not color the balls or include the picture on the right in the problem statement… The handout with the right picture… worked as the final hint." (PDF p. 34)

**Workspace and materials [S].**
- "we give the class handouts with 12 copies of uncolored beads" (PDF p. 38).
- "To avoid anxiety about regular graph paper being too small we insisted on using paper without grids" (PDF p. 129).
- "The second part of this problem takes a lot of time and works well as homework" (PDF p. 32).

**Read-aloud tricks [S].** "The next three problems are intended for the first or the last lessons of the year. They should be read to the children, not written down." These are the coins, candles and hot-dog-ends problems. The hot-dog problem: younger kids "were very happy and giggled as they realized that even a half of a hotdog has two ends" (PDF p. 158).

**Warmups [S].**
- "In our experience children enjoy solving the next few problems. We give them as warmups when the main topic is challenging." Example: "Problem 6.21. Place three chairs in a square room, so that there is a chair by each wall." (PDF p. 159)
- Warmups are given "a week or two before" a topic.

**Sequencing [S].**
- The isomorphism arc runs beads (2.1), then Mumbo words (2.3), then ice cream (2.5), then "2.6 … The answers to the three previous problems… are the same. Is there a reason for this?"
- "The following problems are more challenging. They should be done only if the students are comfortable with the problems in the previous sections."
- The meta-narrative they tell kids: "We studied the simplest examples first. We figured out the pattern. We invented a systematic way of reasoning. We came up with an argument that works for all cases."
- Hint style: questions such as "What can be the first step in building the strip?" or "For which face is counting balls the easiest?"

**Not this [S].** "While Olympiad-style problems are very important tools… we do not need these tools… We use Olympiad-style problems only occasionally as warmups and challenges." (p. ix)

### 1.3 Math for Love / Dan Finkel (NYT Numberplay, *The Hindu* columns, the Pilgrim's Puzzle lesson, the remote curriculum)

**Design criteria [S] (NYT Broken Calculator).**
- "1. A worthy mathematical question or problem, with a low barrier to entry and a high ceiling, 2. A safe atmosphere, free of judgment, 3. Time."
- "working with teachers or students I usually change the question to: What numbers are possible to get on the calculator and what numbers are impossible? That way, people can mess around and get positive feedback. You made 12 on the calculator? Great!"
- On a 3rd-grade demo: "The moment the kids saw that they were in control of how they approached the question, they couldn't get enough."

**Scaling down [S].**
- Polka Dot: "If using 11 cards is too hard, they can try using three, five or seven cards to start, and slowly work their way up; there's an easy way to pepper successes in as they approach the larger challenge."
- Circle-Toss: "You can actually change the numbers to between 10 and 30… and you have a much easier problem."

**Format [S].**
- NYT puzzles give the rules as short separate lines ("Button A added 7. / Button B added 5. / Button C took the square root."). One question follows, then "Bonus:" and "Super Bonus:".
- The Hindu columns use the blocks "THE PUZZLE" and "RESEARCH QUESTION": "Every puzzle here is… just the beginning of a larger, more beautiful, exploration, so after each puzzle, I've included a direction for research."
- Some state the target count: "Find all 20 grid squares that can be formed in the 4 by 4 dot grid."
- A worked example comes before the puzzle: "For example, it takes just three moves to switch one black and one red checker", plus "But be careful: it is possible to hit a dead end."

**Pilgrim lesson [S].**
- The student worksheet states the tax rules as four bullets, then asks numbered questions.
- The teacher notes add a "Simplified Pilgrim's Puzzle" starter and "Any reduction in the tax they're able to achieve should be celebrated!"
- Hints are posed as questions, for example "What happens if the pilgrim circles a single block?"

**Remote curriculum (K–5) [S].**
- "A full lesson will typically take 30–60 minutes… There are also optional challenges… Students will have opportunities to spend longer on math, but don't need to."
- "Don't worry if they don't have all the answers! What's important is that kids engage with the math ideas and play."
- Materials for K–2: crayons, dice, snap cubes, pattern blocks, counters and dot cards. Grades 3–5 swap in Cuisenaire rods.

### 1.4 Early Family Math (EFM, with Math for Love)

**Games [S].** These are teacher-facing cards with the fields Math Concepts / Materials / Players / Set up / Play / Goal / Example / Discussion and Tips / Variations. K–1 guidance:
- "If your players are not comfortable with numerals yet, use a pile of objects." (K-3 Nim)
- "Keep the activity grounded by using objects during all the steps." (K-1 Mystery Change)
- PK–K items are oral and whole-group (Counterexamples, Which One Doesn't Belong).

**Puzzles of the Week (student-facing) [S].** The form is very consistent:
1. A 1–3 sentence rule.
2. A worked example, often an example and a non-example side by side ("In the diagram on the left, the boxes with numbers 2 and 3 share a side… In the diagram on the right, no two consecutive numbers share a side").
3. "THE CHALLENGE:" in one sentence.
4. "EXPLORATION:" as an open extension, often "Make these puzzles for your friends."

The ask forms used:
- Impossibility: "If you can, find ten coins that add up to 43. If you can't, describe why it is impossible." and "Show how this can be done or describe why it is impossible."
- Extremal: "What is the fewest number of dots you can remove…", "Place as many X's as you can…"
- Exhaustive: "Count the number of all the rectangles…"

**Teacher notes [S].**
- "you can play around with it and eventually come up with the answer, and that is perfectly fine for a young child"
- "can be solved enjoyably and satisfactorily with a lot of trial and error"
- On an open problem: "that should not keep a student from playing around with it"

### 1.5 Julia Robinson Math Festival (JRMF)

**Student instruction sheet [S].**
- A one-line objective, then "Rules:" as 1–5 numbered one-sentence rules. Example: "A party is any group of squares that are connected (if two squares only touch by a corner, they are not connected)." Ambiguities are pre-empted inside the rule.
- Task sheets are mostly pictures plus numbered short questions. Apple Picking: "1. Start by making a row of apples that look like the picture. 2. Can you find a strategy that helps you win every time? 3. Does it matter if you go first or second?" Konigsberg: "Derrick solved puzzle 8 by starting at a and ending at g. Can you solve puzzle 8 starting at a different point?"

**Sequenced impossibility and prediction (River Crossings) [S].**
- "Question 1: One of these two puzzles is impossible. What makes it impossible?"
- "Question 5: Can you predict if Puzzle 9 is possible or impossible before trying to solve it?… how would you explain why to a friend?"
- Teacher guide: "Introduce Puzzle 2, which is an impossible puzzle. Let students struggle… until someone questions whether it's possible. This is a great AHA moment."

**Timing per activity [S].** Introduction (5 min), Explore (30 min), Discussion (5–10 min, "Try to have at least 3 students share out").

**Leader stance [S].**
- "Introduce the activity without overexplaining it and without telling what strategies students might want to use."
- "Use the first puzzle to demonstrate the rules. Have the student help you solve the second challenge. Have the student explore the next challenges."
- "For many students, it can be less intimidating to have each Puzzle/Question printed on a separate piece of paper."
- Answer keys are labeled "Please don't share these strategies with students!"

**Beginner versions (PreK–2) [S].**
- "'beginner' does not mean 'easy'". The objective is simplified to one goal, for example "Cover all the squares with the blocks", with 1 rule.
- Pages are almost wordless. The beginner Apple Picking task page reads in full: "You can play any of the games below, or make up your own!"

### 1.6 Lowell year 1 (organizer's 11 handouts, Fall 2025; grade band not stated in the files I was allowed to read)

**Format [S].**
- Header: "Math Circle Problem Set N. Name:". Items are "Problem N.k:" with no section headings.
- 5–8 problems per set. Set 1 has 11 unnumbered items.
- Median statement length is 30–60 words per set, with a range of 7 to about 250 (Protagoras paradox, PS11.1).
- Mostly 1st and 2nd person: "Here is what I remember", "I asked my class".

**Edits to borrowed problems [S], which show the organizer's register.**
- From Roz 3.1 to PS2.1:
  - "took the aisle seats" became "took the seats on the ends".
  - The bare dash list became "Here is what I heard afterwards:" plus bullets.
  - "Place the animals on the chairs of the row" became "Write the name of each animal above the chair it sat in."
  - "Mouse refused…" became "Mouse was scared and refused…".
- Bay's "Mumbo people" became "the abbas" monsters. Hephaestus/Zeus gems and the "ice cream monster" carry the same isomorphism arc, ending with PS6.6 "The answer to each of problems 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, and 6.5 is the same. Is there a reason for this?"

**Carry-over and running out [S].**
- "(This is the same as problem 4.6 last week, but it's here again since we didn't discuss it.)" (PS5.6)
- The card-dealing problem recurs in PS5.7, PS6.7 and PS7.8.
- "If time permits: billiard problems from the book" (PS9). Billiards then appear in PS10 and PS11.
- "Problem 9.8 (bonus homework)". "Tree problem (to be explained in class)" (PS2.5).

**Building across weeks [S].**
- Nim 20 stones (PS2.3: "Play some games with the other students and try to figure out how to win… What if there are a different number of stones than 20?").
- Then PS3.1 recaps what the class found and gives a scaffold: "Make two lists: one list for the winning numbers… 4 is a losing number". It asks for 23 and 24 stones, then "Mr. Five… changes the rules… take 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 stones."

**Worked traces and notation offered [S].**
- Card-deal trace in bullets (PS5.7).
- "For instance, she could go three blocks east and then three blocks north (EEENNN)" (PS4.6).
- "Labeling note: We can use the letters S and C to save space" (PS5.5).

**Workspace [S].** Sixteen blank grids, with "If you run out of space below, you can draw more grids on the back." (PS4.6) Fill-in tables answered "YES, NO, or CAN'T TELL" (PS5.1–5.2).

**Vocabulary after the idea [S].** "Fun fact: mathematicians call these 'types' of things equivalence classes." (PS4.5)

**Social prompts [S].** "Put them in the middle of the table for the class to compare." (PS3.4)

**Prediction ladder [S].** "Draw the path of a ball on 1 x 2, 2 x 3, 3 x 4, and 4 x 5 tables… Where do you think the ball would end up on a 99 x 100 table?" (PS10.5)

**Wordless sheet [S].** `subtract_shapes2.tex` is a 20-pt sheet of picture equations ([shape] + [shape] = [empty box]), four per page, additions before subtractions. It is adapted from Bay Problem 6.1.

**Weaker items [I].**
- PS8.2–8.3 "Express a mapping between F_6 and F_5 + F_4" uses undergraduate jargon.
- PS10 numbers two problems "10.1".
- "(see provided worksheet)" and "to be explained in class" lean on the teacher.
- PS11.1 runs to 252 words.

I have no data on which year-1 items kids liked most.

### 1.7 Others (glanced)

**A Decade of the BMC [S].** A high-school circle with 2-hour sessions of 20–30 students. Problems carry margin icons for "Warm-up", "Exercise", "Problem" and "Open question". Formal proofs are deliberately postponed "until students have been exposed to at least two or three intriguing mathematical topics." Little bears on K–5.

**Beast Academy [S].**
- Each puzzle type has a fixed name ("Cross-Number puzzle", "Fraction Fill").
- An "EXAMPLE" with a worked solution comes before a "PRACTICE" block that has one imperative instruction line covering several puzzles.
- "Some problems in this book are very challenging. These problems are marked with a ★… Every problem marked with a ★ has a hint!" A pencil icon means "you should write an explanation."

## 2. Cross-cutting findings

### 2.1 Phrasing

**Length [S].** Statements are 1–3 sentences, about 15–60 words; the long ones are rule setups. Rozhkovskaya's median is about 30 words, Lowell's 30–60, and EFM and JRMF challenges are 1–2 sentences. Very long statements are rare and tied to logic puzzles with clue lists.

**Voice [S].**
- Plain declaratives. Teacher-as-narrator in the first person is common ("I have a few marbles in my pocket", "Here is what I heard afterwards").
- The second person "you" or "Can you…?" also appears.
- Characters are named, and stories are short and often silly (Bay preface).
- Nothing gushes. There are no exclamation-heavy praise lines in the student text. Excitement comes from the situation (a puppy chewed the calculator; foxes eat rabbits). [S plus I]

**Headings [S].**
- A title and a number are typical.
- Optional fixed labels: "Rules:", "THE CHALLENGE:", "EXPLORATION:", "Bonus:", "Hint."
- Bay names problems ("Four Ice Cream Flavors"). Rozhkovskaya groups by topic heading. Lowell uses only numbers.

**Rules [S].** Rules are a separate numbered or bulleted list of one-sentence rules. Where kids predictably misread, the clarification goes inside the rule (JRMF corner-touching; Lowell "NOTE: we're not allowed to have any trapezoids go over the edge").

**The ask [S].** One explicit question or imperative at the end. It is often concrete about the output: write the name above the chair; color; draw it below; YES/NO/CAN'T TELL. Typical ask forms:
- "How many…?"
- "Is it possible…?"
- "Who wins and why?"
- "Does it matter if you go first or second?"
- "What is the fewest/smallest…?"
- "Can you predict…?"
- "Make your own…"

### 2.2 How much work per hour; how sessions end
- **Rozhkovskaya (grades 1–3) [S].** 5–12 numbered problems per lesson, including labeled extras. Only about half to two-thirds were discussed. Fast finishers got extra problems that were not debriefed. Lessons often ended with a hands-on or social activity (crafts, a board performance, a game).
- **Bay (grades 1–5, 50–55 min) [S].** A mix of warmup, homework discussion, the main theme and an occasional hands-on project. A stack of challenge problems is kept for kids who are ahead. Long parts move to homework.
- **JRMF [S].** One activity in about 45 minutes: 5 minutes of intro, 30 of exploration through about 7 sequenced questions covering about 14 small puzzles, and 5–10 of whole-group share-out.
- **MfL remote curriculum [S].** A lesson is 30–60 minutes with optional challenges.
- **Lowell [S].** 5–8 problems per sheet, routinely not finished. Undiscussed items are explicitly carried to the next week, "If time permits" items are deferred, and there is a bonus homework item.
- **[I]** Writers plan more than fits and label the excess. Sessions run out of time rather than out of problems. The debrief covers the core and about one extension, so the sheet must make clear which items are core.

### 2.3 K–1 specifically

Evidence that K–1 kids are capable (underestimated) [S]:
- Stankova at first thought 2nd grade "too young… Next there will be math circles for kindergartners!!" She later saw her kindergarten daughter attend.
- Bay: the grade 1–2 versus 3–4 difference is "not that significant"; first and second graders wrote 1/256.
- Rozhkovskaya: non-readers handled "sophisticated logic situations and complicated rules just by listening". The baby Bulls-and-Cows game was "exactly right".
- JRMF: "beginner does not mean easy."

Evidence of overload [S]:
- Long reading; notation (powers); multi-step drawing (the second stage of the knots, neat pizza lines); filling large tables; imagining mirrors without mirrors.
- Statements whose question itself was unclear (flower pots).
- Trick questions, which first graders do not experience as tricks.
- Too-long games (race lanes).
- Bay: age word problems are "surprisingly difficult for many younger ones".

What works for K–1 [S]:
- Explicit procedures (code pictures).
- Manipulatives with a guaranteed solution (frogs).
- Two-player take-away games with small piles ("If your players are not comfortable with numerals yet, use a pile of objects").
- Building and counting (squares from paper squares).
- Short oral riddles.
- Wordless picture equations (Lowell subtract-shapes).
- Placing chairs or objects by a rule (Bay 6.21–6.23).
- Logic with 3–4 named animals and a picture of the seats.

Reading [S]:
- The adult reads or retells (Rozhkovskaya L8).
- Trick problems are read, "not written down" (Bay).
- Beginner pages are almost wordless (JRMF).
- Video carries the instruction for K (MfL).

**[I]** For K–1, the page is a prop for an adult reader. The challenge can be real (logic, strategy, impossibility), but the text load, numbers and fine-motor demand must be small.

### 2.4 Sequencing and how hard questions are posed

**Concrete to idea [S].**
1. Play or build.
2. Small cases, systematically.
3. Look for a pattern ("Do you see any patterns?", "Will the pattern continue forever?").
4. Predict a large case (99×100; 100 little squares).
5. Explain why, or compare structures ("same answer—why?").
6. Change a rule (Mr. Five; frogs that cannot go backwards; Trionia).
7. Make your own.

**Hard-question forms [S].**
- Impossibility: "Is it possible…?" (Roz M.12); "If you can… If you can't, describe why it is impossible." (EFM); "One of these puzzles is impossible. Which one?" (JRMF). Finkel reframes the question as "which are possible and which impossible", so that every attempt yields information.
- Find all / how many: "How many different…?"; giving the target count when exhaustiveness is the point ("Find all 20 grid squares"); "Did you miss any paths?" (Lowell 5.4).
- Fewest or most: "smallest number of guards" (Roz M.6.4); "as few colors as possible" (JRMF); "fewest number of buttons" (Finkel Bonus); "in three questions" (Roz 10.6).
- Strategy: "Who wins… and why?"; "Can you find a strategy that helps you win every time?"; "Does it matter if you go first or second?"
- Prediction before trying: "Can you predict if Puzzle 9 is possible or impossible before trying to solve it?"; "Without writing them all out, how could we find…" (Lowell 7.7).

### 2.5 What did NOT work (consolidated) [S]
- Long texts.
- Unclear question wording.
- Hidden-trick problems for young kids.
- Numerical precision added for rigor.
- New notation.
- Geometry "theorem demos" that kids could not find surprising.
- Fine-motor or clerical tasks (big tables, many precise lines, 6-part crafts done alone).
- Overlong games.
- Rules too subtle to enforce.
- Undefined "easy" terms.
- Culture-dependent premises.
- Missing or wrong-size materials.
- Expecting written or verbal justification without a structure such as a table.
- Parents' "It is not math!" complaints when there was no arithmetic; Rozhkovskaya treated this as a positive expectation-setting moment.

### 2.6 Explicit advice on writing handouts [S]
- Statements must be unambiguous and easy to understand at once; use silly stories and named characters (Bay).
- Pictures disambiguate; withhold the hint-picture from the statement (Bay).
- Use everyday words over terms; "explanation" over "proof"; number from one; avoid formulas in the statement such as "3-by-3-by-3" (Bay).
- Do not overexplain or tell strategies; demonstrate rules with the first puzzle; consider one puzzle per page (JRMF).
- Use a low floor and high ceiling; ask "which are possible or impossible" for positive feedback; offer smaller versions to "pepper successes"; Bonus and Super Bonus tiers (Finkel).
- Rule, then example and non-example, then THE CHALLENGE, then EXPLORATION (EFM).
- Track steps by crossing out; size grids and set starting points so drawings fit; a table for logic puzzles (Rozhkovskaya).
- Star the hard ones and give them hints; ask for written explanations on specific items only (Beast Academy).

## 3. Synthesis

### (a) Writing principles for student pages (checkable)

1. **One idea per item, short.** Setup is at most 3 sentences plus one ask. Target 15–60 words for K–3 and at most about 90 for grades 4–5. A rule list counts separately. *Check: word count per item.* [Roz, Lowell, EFM, JRMF]
2. **Rules go in a separate numbered or bulleted list, one rule per line, one sentence each.** *Check: no rule is buried mid-story.* [JRMF, MfL Pilgrim, Lowell PS2.1/4.1, Roz 3.1]
3. **Show a worked instance of the rules before the challenge.** Use an example, and a non-example where kids will misread. *Check: every new rule set has an "For example…" or picture.* [EFM PotW, Finkel, Lowell PS4.6/5.7, Roz L1 WRONG/CORRECT, BA]
4. **End with exactly one explicit ask that names the output.** For example "Write…", "Color…", "How many…?", "Is it possible…?" *Check: the last sentence is the ask; the kid knows what to put on the paper.* [Lowell's edit of Roz 3.1; JRMF; Roz]
5. **Use light, named, slightly silly framing (1–2 sentences) that actually drives the math.** Prefer animals, monsters, a teacher "I". Avoid premises that need cultural or real-world knowledge. *Check: delete the story; if the math is unchanged and the story added more than 2 sentences, trim.* [Bay preface; Roz L10, L13; Lowell]
6. **Use everyday words; terms come only after the idea, or never.** Write "seats on the ends", "SAME SHAPE SAME SIZE" and "explain why", not "aisle", "congruent", "prove". Avoid notation such as exponents, n, or C(5,2). *Check: no term a grade-level kid would not say aloud.* [Bay pp. 10, 107; Lowell PS2.1, PS4.5; Roz L3]
7. **Pre-empt known misreadings inside the statement.** Use a picture, an "(only touching at a corner doesn't count)" clause, or a "NOTE:". *Check: list the 1–2 likeliest misreadings and confirm each is blocked.* [Bay ice cream; JRMF; Lowell PS4.3]
8. **Never put the key insight in the statement, the picture or the title.** Hints live in adult notes or a separate hint line. *Check: the statement does not name the strategy or theorem.* [Bay PDF p. 34; JRMF; Roz L9]
9. **Low floor: the first item of the set can be solved by trial or by acting it out.** Ideally failure is impossible for item 1. *Check: could a kid who knows nothing start within 1 minute?* [Roz L11; JRMF beginner; EFM notes]
10. **Climb by small cases written onto the page** (2, 4, 6 points; 3, 5, 7 cards; 1×2…4×5) **before the big or general case.** *Check: the set contains at least 2 small instances before any "general" or large question.* [Roz L7; Finkel Polka Dot; Lowell PS10.5; Bay]
11. **Include one prediction or generalization item that brute force cannot reach.** For example "99 × 100 table", "100 little squares", "Without writing them all out…", "before trying". *Check: present.* [Lowell PS7.7/10.5; Roz 4.3; JRMF Q5]
12. **Include one "is it possible?" item and phrase it neutrally.** For example "If you can…; if you can't, explain why it is impossible", or "One of these is impossible—which?" *Check: at least one item whose answer may be "no".* [EFM; JRMF; Roz M.12; Finkel]
13. **Use extremal and exhaustive asks deliberately.** "Fewest/most…?", "How many different…?", and give the total ("Find all 20…") when the point is being systematic rather than counting. [Roz M.6/M.13; EFM; JRMF; Finkel Hindu]
14. **For games, ask "Who wins, and how?" / "Does it matter if you go first or second?"** after "Play a few games with a partner." *Check: games have a play instruction plus a strategy question.* [JRMF; Lowell PS2.3; Roz L7]
15. **Vary one rule rather than switching context** (Mr. Five, backward-free frogs, 2 rubies instead of 3). Optionally add "These answers are the same—why?" *Check: at least one item reuses the previous setup.* [Lowell PS3.3/6.2–6.6; Bay 2.6; Roz 11.6-a]
16. **Label core versus extra.** About 4–6 core items for an hour (fewer for K–1), plus clearly marked Bonus or ★ items for fast finishers that need not be debriefed. *Check: the core alone fits about 35–40 minutes of group work; extras are labeled.* [Roz L3; Bay preface; Finkel Bonus; BA ★; JRMF 30-minute explore]
17. **Give room and props.** Blank grids, pictures to mark, tables with headers, cut-out templates, a named manipulative ("Use counters"). *Check: every "draw/write/fill" has space or a template on the page.* [Bay PDF p. 38; Lowell PS4.6/5.1; Roz L2]
18. **Keep clerical and fine-motor load small.** Cap tables (at most about 4×4 for K–2), number of drawings, cuts and lines. No repetitive 6-part crafts alone. *Check: estimated non-thinking time under 20% of the item.* [Roz L8, L9, L14, L15]
19. **Ask for reasons on selected items, with a structure.** "How would you explain this to a friend?", "Why do you think so?", plus a table or list scaffold for logic. *Check: 1–3 "why" prompts per set, not on every item.* [Roz L3, L12; JRMF; BA pencil icon]
20. **K–1 pages are read aloud.** At most 2 short sentences per item, numbers within 10–20, mostly pictures and physical moves (place, color, move counters, flip). Use plain-procedure tasks. *Check: every K–1 item can be done after hearing it once.* [Roz L2, L8; Bay PDF p. 158; JRMF beginner; EFM; Lowell subtract-shapes]
21. **No written "gotcha" tricks for young kids.** If you use one, mark it read-aloud or warm-up. [Roz L6; Bay PDF p. 158]
22. **Write the social moves into the page.** "Play with a partner", "Put them in the middle of the table to compare", "Make one for your friends". [Lowell PS2.3/3.4; EFM; Roz 2.4]
23. **Close with a "make your own" or open "Exploration" line.** [Roz M.3.6/M.4/7.5/M.9.3; EFM EXPLORATION; JRMF "Make your own…"; Finkel RESEARCH QUESTION]
24. **Stable numbering and memorable names** so the group can say "the sausage problem" next week. *Check: unique numbers; each item has a nameable hook.* [Bay preface; Lowell numbering; BA puzzle names]

### (b) Anti-patterns an editor would cut
- An expository paragraph or "lesson" before the first problem, or explanations of a concept the kids are meant to discover. [Roz L4; Bay]
- Technical vocabulary or notation in the student text: exponents, "mapping", "isomorphic" in the ask, n, C(n,k), "prove", "congruent", "aisle". [Bay; Roz L3; I: Lowell PS8.2–8.3]
- The strategy or theorem named in a title, picture, or "Hint" printed beside the problem. [Bay; JRMF; Roz L9]
- Rigor padding: extra numbers or conditions added to close loopholes that make the statement harder to parse (the 2 m 10 cm bridge). [Roz L5]
- An ask that is unclear about what the kid should produce (flower pots), or several asks crammed into one item, more than about 3 questions. [Roz L6; I]
- Hidden-trick riddles in print for K–2. [Roz L6]
- Big fill-in tables, many precise drawings, long games, or one kid doing a repetitive craft alone. [Roz L8, L9, L14, L15]
- Story fluff that does not affect the math, and story premises that depend on cultural knowledge (Santa, hibernation, sports rules). [Roz L10, L13; I]
- Cutesy register: exclamation-heavy pep ("Wow! Let's go, mathematicians!"), emoji, "Great job!" on the page, or a story longer than the math. [I: absent from every source sheet]
- Headings that read like a curriculum ("Learning Objective", "Warm-Up Time!", "Let's Explore!") or multiple subheadings per item. [I: sources use a title plus numbers plus at most "Rules / Challenge / Exploration / Bonus"]
- Undefined "easy" terms the rest of the set depends on (even/odd). [Roz L5]
- Placeholders that only the original teacher can decode ("Tree problem (to be explained in class)", "see provided worksheet" with no worksheet), and duplicate numbering. [I: Lowell PS2.5, PS8.4, PS10]
- Items over about 100 words for grades 2–3, or over about 2 sentences for K–1, unless they are clue lists. [I: Lowell PS11.1 at 252 words; Roz L4]
- "Experiments that confirm a theorem" as the main event for young kids, where they are told the surprising fact rather than meeting a question. [Roz L6]
- More core items than the hour holds, with no core/extra distinction. [I: Lowell carry-overs; Roz's unspoken extras]

## 4. Caveats and open questions
- No source gives a tested count of "problems per hour by grade". The numbers above are reconstructed from lesson contents, "not discussed" notes, JRMF timings and Lowell carry-overs. [I]
- Rozhkovskaya's book problems include expository text written for parents and teachers. Her in-class handouts were likely terser than the book. [I]
- The year-1 Lowell sheets have no grade label or outcome notes in the folder I was allowed to read. "Kids liked them" comes from the task brief, not from the files.
- K–1-specific written problem sheets are scarce in the sources. Most K–1 evidence is from oral or manipulative activities (EFM PK–K, JRMF beginner, Rozhkovskaya's 5-year-olds working with parents, Bay grades 1–2). [S plus I]
