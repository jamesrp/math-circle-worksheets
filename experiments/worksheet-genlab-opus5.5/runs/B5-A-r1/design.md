# Week 1 design: Tiling with pattern blocks

This document is for the writer of the student pages. It fixes the mathematics: which problems, in what order, with which boards, what children record, the intended answers, and what the adult at the table should watch for. Every count and claim below was checked by computer (section 5). Exact board coordinates are in Appendix B.

---

## 0. Conventions shared by all three bands

**The grid.** Every board is a region of the triangular grid whose small triangle has 1-inch edges (2.54 cm), printed at 100% scale. Draw the grid lines inside each board in light gray and the outline heavy. A small triangle "points up" or "points down". Heights: one row of small triangles is 0.866 in tall.

**Blocks used.** Green triangle (1 small triangle), blue rhombus (2), red trapezoid (3), purple chevron (4: it is exactly two blue rhombi joined into an arrowhead, and it covers 4 of the 6 small triangles around one grid point), yellow hexagon (6). All five are symmetric under flipping over, so flipping a block never matters.

**Blocks not used.** Pink right triangles, teal kites and gray darts do not fit this grid. They are fine during the free handling at the start but should be off the tables during work time.

**Big blocks.** The 2× and 3× blocks must never be placed on a board: a 3× green triangle covers the side-3 triangle board in one piece and would break several problems. The adult may hold a big block beside a board for comparison (the side-2 and side-3 triangle boards are the 2× and 3× green; the side-2 hexagon is the 2× yellow; K-1's "big" shapes in Problems 1 and 5 are exactly the 2× blocks). Put the packet-wide rule "use the small blocks" in each band's first-page rules.

**Packet-wide rules (state once, at the top of page 1 of each band).** Blocks lie flat inside the outline and fit the lines of the grid. They may not overlap or stick out past the edge. (Grades 2–3 add: they may not cover a black triangle. Grades 4–5 add: two ways of covering a board are different if some block is in a different place.) "Cover" means every small triangle of the board is under a block.

**Recording.** In K–1 and grades 2–3 a child records by drawing on the actual-size board itself (draw along the grid lines where block edges are, then lift the blocks), by shading leftover green triangles, or by writing a number or circling yes/no. In grades 4–5, where many arrangements of the same board must be kept, the board stays clean for building and the child draws each arrangement on small copies (half size is fine, since no block goes on them). Small copies used only for drawing are labelled as drawing copies so that nobody tries to put blocks on them.

**Whole-group demonstration (suggested).** Use a board that appears on no page, such as a strip of 6 small triangles (a 3-by-1 parallelogram). Show that blocks sit exactly on the grid lines, that blocks may not overlap or stick out, and that the strip can be covered by 3 blues and also by 2 reds. Then show how to record by lifting the blocks and drawing along the lines. Do not demonstrate a flip, a chain, or anything about which boards are impossible.

**Who sits where (a suggestion only).** The K–1 notes below are written so that the parent volunteer can use them without mathematical background. The grades 4–5 sequence asks for the most explanation (flip routes, chains, parity), so it suits a mathematician.

**Mathematical spine.** Kernel 1 (coloring obstruction and best packings) is grades 2–3's main line and appears in K–1 Problems 1–2. Kernel 2 (rhombus tilings of hexagons, flips, ribbons) is grades 4–5's main line and appears in K–1 Problem 3, where it is reduced to "how many places can the one standing blue go?". On the grades 4–5 pages, "ribbon" is called a **chain**, which is the plain word.

## 1. K–1 (two kindergartners, one first grader)

### 1.1 Thread

Children decide whether a shape can be covered (kernel 1: the big triangle "is the size of two blues" but cannot be covered by them). They find the fewest leftover triangles, the fewest blocks, and all the ways (kernel 2 at small scale: long hexagons covered by blues have 2, 3, 4 ways). They also work out how to win a placing game. Every problem is posed with a picture, a block, and one or two short sentences, and the answer is a yes or no, a number, or a drawing. By the end a child has seen that "big enough" does not mean "possible", that the obvious first move is not always the best one, that "all the ways" can be finished and you can know you are finished, and that going second can be the winning choice.

### 1.2 Problems

Each problem shows small pictures of the blocks it allows next to the text.

**K1 Problem 1 — Can blue blocks cover it?**
- *Task (read aloud):* "Cover each shape with blue blocks only. Can you do it?"
- *Boards (actual size; each 2 rows = 1.73 in tall):*
  a. small hexagon, the shape of one yellow block (6 small triangles), 2.00 × 1.73 in;
  b. big triangle with 2 on each side (4 small triangles), 2.00 × 1.73 in;
  c. big rhombus with 2 on each side (8), 3.00 × 1.73 in;
  d. big trapezoid, double-size red (bottom 4, top 2, slanted sides 2; 12 small triangles), 4.00 × 1.73 in.
- *Child does:* places blues; tries again and again on the ones that will not work.
- *Records:* circles yes or no under each shape (about 0.5 in of height per board).
- *Answers:* a yes (3 blues, 2 ways); b **no**: only 1 blue fits and 2 small triangles stay empty, although the shape is as big as 2 blues; c yes (4 blues, only one way); d **no**: at most 5 blues fit and 2 small triangles stay empty, although the shape is as big as 6 blues.
- *Adult watches for:* blocks sneaking over the edge to make b or d "work" (hold to the rule). On b and d ask "What is left over? Can you move things to fix it?" A child may notice that the leftover triangles always point up, like the big triangle. Enjoy that observation, but do not explain it.

**K1 Problem 2 — As few green triangles as you can**
- *Task:* "Cover the big triangle with blue blocks and green triangles. Use as few green triangles as you can."
- *Boards:* triangles with 2, 3, 4 on each side (2.00 × 1.73, 3.00 × 2.60, 4.00 × 3.46 in).
- *Records:* "How many greens?" box under each (about 0.6 in).
- *Answers:* 2, 3, 4 greens (with 1, 3, 6 blues). In every best covering all the greens point up.
- *Adult watches for:* first tries usually use more greens, so ask "Can you use fewer?". After the second triangle, ask "How many do you think the next one needs?" Reasonable lines to try on the side-3 triangle: put greens in the three corners, and the middle is a small hexagon for 3 blues.

**K1 Problem 3 — All the ways**
- *Task:* "Cover the shape with blue blocks in a different way on each copy. Draw around the blocks to show each way."
- *Boards:* three long hexagons, each 2 rows tall, with flat top and bottom: bottom edge 1, 2, 3 in (top edge the same, short slanted ends 1 in). Sizes 2.00, 3.00, 4.00 in wide × 1.73 in. Give **one more copy than there are ways**: 3 copies of the first, 4 of the second, 5 of the third (see the page plan in Appendix A).
- *Records:* drawing on the copies.
- *Answers:* 2, 3 and 4 ways. In every way exactly one blue stands up (a diamond pointing up and down) and all the others lie flat, and the standing blue can be in 2, 3 or 4 places. The spare copy cannot be filled in a new way.
- *Adult watches for:* repeats (lay the copies side by side and compare), and a child counting a mirror image as "the same". On a board that stays still, blocks in different places make a different way. Ask "Is there another way? How do you know?". The standing blue is the key a child may find, but it is not to be given.

**K1 Problem 4 — Fewest blocks**
- *Task:* "Fill the shape with yellow, red, blue and green blocks. Use as few blocks as you can."
- *Boards:* a. triangle with 3 on each side (3.00 × 2.60 in); b. hexagon with 2 on each side (4.00 × 3.46 in); c. triangle with 4 on each side (4.00 × 3.46 in).
- *Records:* "How many blocks?" box under each.
- *Answers:* a **3** (three reds in a pinwheel; only 2 such coverings). Putting the yellow in the middle, the only place it fits, gives at best 4 (yellow + 3 greens). b **6** (three yellows and three blues; only 2 such coverings). A yellow in the very middle gives at best 7 (yellow + 6 reds); at most 3 yellows ever fit. c **5** (one yellow, three reds, one green; only 3 such coverings). Without a yellow the best is 6.
- *Adult watches for:* the yellow-first habit. That this habit fails is the point of the problem. Ask "Can you do it with one fewer?" and "Does the yellow have to go there?". Allowing purple would not change any answer.

**K1 Problem 5 — Big blocks from small blocks of the same color**
- *Task:* "Make each big shape using only small blocks of its own color. Can you do it?"
- *Boards (each with the matching small block pictured):* a. big green (triangle with 2 on each side), greens; b. big red (same shape as Problem 1d), reds; c. big yellow (hexagon with 2 on each side), yellows; d. big purple (double-size chevron, 4.00 × 3.46 in), purples.
- *Records:* yes/no circle and "How many blocks?" box.
- *Answers:* a yes, 4 greens; b yes, 4 reds (one way only); c **no**: by size it needs 4 yellows, but at most 3 fit; d **no**: by size it needs 4 purples, but at most 3 fit.
- *Adult watches for:* children may stop at "it doesn't fit". Ask how many they think it should take (4, the same as for green and red) and how many fit. Children may also link this back to Problem 1, where the big green and big red could not be covered with blues.

**K1 Problem 6 — A game: last block wins**
- *Task (two sentences):* "Take turns with a partner putting one blue block on the board; whoever puts down the last block wins. Is it better to go first or second?"
- *Boards:* a. small hexagon (yellow-block shape, 2.00 × 1.73 in); b. strip of 8 small triangles in one row (a parallelogram, 4.50 × 0.87 in); c. strip of 10 small triangles (5.50 × 0.87 in).
- *Records:* tally marks under "first player won" and "second player won" for each board.
- *Answers:* a: the **second** player can always win. After any first move, 4 triangles are left in a row, and covering the middle two leaves no room. b: the first player can win, for example by covering the middle two triangles, which leaves 3 and 3 so that exactly two more blocks fit. Covering triangles 2–3 or 6–7 also wins. c: the first player wins only by covering the middle two triangles (5 and 6), then copying each move of the partner on the other side.
- *Adult watches for:* let children play many fast games. Ask "Where did the winner put the first block?" On the strip of 10, ask "What if you always copy your partner?".

### 1.3 Time budget (minutes)

| Problem | Quick child | Typical child |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Can blues cover it? (4 shapes) | 5 | 8 |
| 2 Fewest greens (3 triangles) | 7 | 10 |
| 3 All the ways (3 long hexagons, 9 coverings + spare copies) | 9 | 13 |
| 4 Fewest blocks (3 shapes) | 9 | 12 |
| 5 Big from small (4 shapes) | 7 | 10 |
| 6 Game (3 boards, many games) | 8+ | 10 |
| **Total** | **45+** | **63** |

A typical child reaches Problem 4 in forty minutes. A quick child has 45 minutes or more of real work, and Problems 4–6 reward getting far: the two hardest "fewest" boards, the two impossible big shapes, and a game whose strategy has to be discovered.

### 1.4 Why the level is right

- **Easy entry:** every task begins by putting blocks on a shape. No reading is needed because an adult reads one or two short sentences, and the pictures show which blocks to use. The records are yes/no, a number, or drawing around blocks.
- **Real mathematics:** possible versus impossible (Problems 1 and 5, where the size is right but it cannot be done), best possible (Problems 2 and 4, where the first idea is not the best), all the ways and knowing the list is complete (Problem 3, with the spare copy that cannot be filled), and how to win (Problem 6, where the second player wins on the hexagon and "middle, then copy" wins on the strip of 10).
- **Same ideas as the older bands at a smaller scale:** Problems 1–2 are grades 2–3's kernel on boards of 4–16 triangles, and Problem 3 is grades 4–5's "all tilings of a hexagon" on hexagons with 2–4 tilings.
- **As much to do as the others:** six problems over about ten pages.

## 2. Grades 2–3 (four third graders)

### 2.1 Thread

Children find boards that blue rhombi cannot cover even though the number of small triangles is even. They find that on the big triangles the fewest leftover greens equals the side length. They then explain both: every blue covers one up-pointing and one down-pointing triangle, so a board with more ups than downs cannot be covered, and the difference is the least number of greens (kernel 1, the triangular version of the mutilated checkerboard). They test the idea on hexagons with two holes, and prove that 7 greens are needed on the side-7 triangle. They then meet two refinements. The purple chevron can never beat the blue (each chevron is two blues), and it is sometimes worse, because it covers 4 triangles at a time. On the hourglass boards ups equal downs but the boards still cannot be covered, because only a few blues can cross the narrow join. The last problem asks the child to design the smallest board that needs 3 greens. The words "up" and "down" never need to appear on the pages: children will invent their own names.

### 2.2 Problems

**23 Problem 1 — Which boards can blue rhombi cover?**
- *Task:* "Cover each board with blue rhombi. Draw your covering on the board. If a board cannot be covered, write 'cannot' under it."
- *Boards:* A. hexagon with 2 on each side (24 small triangles; 4.00 × 3.46 in); B. triangle with 3 on each side (9; 3.00 × 2.60); C. triangle with 4 on each side (16; 4.00 × 3.46); D. double-size purple chevron (16; 4.00 × 3.46).
- *Records:* rhombi drawn on the board, or "cannot" (one line under each).
- *Answers:* A yes (20 different coverings); B cannot (9 is odd); C **cannot**, although 16 is even; D yes (exactly one covering, forced from the tips).
- *Adult watches for:* B is settled by the odd count, which is good to hear said aloud. On C the child will try many times; ask "What is always left over?". Do not explain yet. Problems 3 and 4 ask for the explanation.

**23 Problem 2 — As few greens as possible**
- *Task:* "Cover each triangle with blue rhombi and green triangles. Use as few greens as you can. Shade the greens on the board and write how many."
- *Boards:* triangles with 3, 4, 5 on each side (3.00 × 2.60; 4.00 × 3.46; 5.00 × 4.33 in).
- *Records:* shading, plus a number box under each board.
- *Answers:* 3, 4, 5 greens (3, 6, 10 blues). In any best covering all the greens point up.
- *Adult watches for:* children improving their first answers, and the pattern "greens = side length". Ask "Could the side-4 triangle be done with 3 greens?". Some children will notice the shaded greens all point the same way.

**23 Problem 3 — Hexagons with two holes**
- *Task:* "Each board is the big hexagon with two small triangles cut out (the black ones). Which boards can be covered with blue rhombi? Draw the covering on each board that can be covered. For each board that cannot, explain why."
- *Boards:* hexagon with 2 on each side (4.00 × 3.46 in) with two holes printed solid black. The board has a left and a right corner at mid-height, each made of an upper and a lower small triangle.
  a. holes: upper half of the left corner and lower half of the right corner;
  b. holes: upper half of the left corner and upper half of the right corner;
  c. holes: an inner down-triangle up and to the right of the center, and the inner up-triangle that is its reflection through the center (down and to the left);
  d. holes: that same inner down-triangle, and its mirror image across the vertical middle line (up and to the left).
  Exact positions are in Appendix B.
- *Records:* covering drawn on the board, or about 4 lines of writing per impossible board.
- *Answers:* a yes; b **no**; c yes; d **no**. In b both holes point up, which leaves 10 up and 12 down. Each blue covers one of each kind, so at most 10 blues fit and 2 triangles stay empty. In d both holes point down. In fact on this hexagon every pair of holes with one up and one down can be covered (all 144 such pairs), and no same-direction pair can (all 132).
- *Adult watches for:* a and b look almost the same, and c and d look almost the same, so the comparison is the clue the board itself gives. Children may notice that a and c have holes placed opposite each other through the center while b and d are mirror pairs. This is true here, but it is not the reason. Push for the reason.

**23 Problem 4 — The biggest triangle**
- *Task:* "Cover this triangle with blue rhombi and as few green triangles as you can. Explain why nobody could use fewer greens."
- *Board:* triangle with 7 on each side (49 small triangles; 7.00 × 6.06 in, filling most of the page).
- *Records:* shading and number box, and about 5 lines for the explanation (this may run onto the next page).
- *Answers:* 7 greens (21 blues). Explanation: there are 28 up-pointing and 21 down-pointing triangles, and every blue covers one of each, so at most 21 blues fit and 7 up-triangles stay uncovered. Another version a child can find: every row has one more up than down, and there are 7 rows.
- *Adult watches for:* children predicting 7 from Problem 2 before covering. Ask them to check it, and then to explain why not 6. Counting 49 triangles one by one is error-prone, and counting row by row is the natural fix.

**23 Problem 5 — Purple chevrons**
- *Task:* "Now use purple chevrons and green triangles only (no blue rhombi). Cover each triangle using as few greens as you can. Compare with blue rhombi: did purple ever need fewer greens than blue? Could purple ever need fewer greens than blue, on any board? Explain."
- *Boards:* triangles with 3, 4, 6 on each side (3.00 × 2.60; 4.00 × 3.46; 6.00 × 5.20 in).
- *Records:* number boxes, a small comparison table (side 3, 4, 6; greens with blue, greens with purple), and about 4 lines.
- *Answers:* purple needs 5, 4, 8 greens. Blue needs 3, 4, 6 (side 6 was not on Problem 2, and children can predict it). Purple is **never** better, on any board, because each chevron can be replaced by the two blues it is made of, which leaves the same greens. Purple is sometimes worse because it covers 4 triangles at a time, so the number of triangles minus the greens must be a multiple of 4: on side 3, 9 − 3 = 6 fails and 9 − 5 = 4 works; on side 6, 36 − 6 = 30 fails and 36 − 8 = 28 works.
- *Adult watches for:* side 6 is hard (7 chevrons and 8 greens). Children who explain "purple is two blues" have the main point. The "multiple of 4" point is the extra.

**23 Problem 6 — Hourglass boards**
- *Task:* "Cover each board with blue rhombi and as few green triangles as you can. Explain why you cannot use fewer."
- *Boards:* each is a triangle with 3 on each side (pointing up) plus an upside-down triangle with 3 on each side above it, touching it along the up-triangle's upper-left side.
  a. the two triangles touch along 2 small edges (4.00 × 3.46 in);
  b. the two triangles touch along 1 small edge (3.50 × 4.33 in).
  Both boards have 18 small triangles, 9 pointing up and 9 pointing down.
- *Records:* shading and number box, and about 4 lines.
- *Answers:* a 2 greens; b 4 greens. The counts are equal, yet neither board can be covered. The bottom triangle has 6 ups and 3 downs, and blues inside it cover at most 3 of its ups. Any other blue covering one of its ups must cross the join, and only 2 blues (a) or 1 blue (b) can cross. So the bottom part keeps at least 1 (a) or 2 (b) empty ups, and the same happens to the downs in the top part.
- *Adult watches for:* children who mastered the counting argument predict 0 and are surprised. That surprise is the point of the problem. Ask "Where are your greens? Which part of the board?".

**23 Problem 7 — Design the smallest board (challenge)**
- *Task:* "On the grid page, draw a board in one piece (small triangles joined along their edges) that needs at least 3 green triangles however you cover it with blue rhombi and greens. Use as few small triangles as you can. How many did you use?"
- *Board:* a full page of actual-size triangle grid (about 7 × 8 in).
- *Records:* drawings on the grid; a number box.
- *Answers:* **7** small triangles: the triangle with 3 on each side with one corner blue removed (that is, a corner triangle and the triangle next to it). It has 5 up and 2 down. It is the only 7-triangle board that works (up to turning and flipping), and no board of 6 or fewer works.
- *Adult watches for:* children start with the side-3 triangle (9) and try trimming. Removing one corner triangle drops the need to 2 greens, but removing a corner blue keeps it at 3. Make sure boards are in one piece along edges.

### 2.3 Time budget (minutes)

| Problem | Quick child | Typical child |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Which boards (4 boards) | 6 | 9 |
| 2 Fewest greens (3 triangles) | 7 | 11 |
| 3 Hexagons with holes (4 boards + explanations) | 7 | 11 |
| 4 Side-7 triangle + explanation | 7 | 11 |
| 5 Purple chevrons (3 boards + explanation) | 8 | 12 |
| 6 Hourglasses (2 boards + explanation) | 7 | 10 |
| 7 Design the smallest board | 6+ | — |
| **Total** | **48+** | **64+** |

A typical child reaches Problem 4 in forty minutes. The quick child meets the chevron, the hourglass, and an open design problem.

### 2.4 Why the level is right

- **Easy entry:** Problem 1 is just "cover these with blues". Two of its boards work, one fails for an obvious reason (odd), and one fails for a reason that is not yet clear.
- **Concrete before explanation:** the impossibility is met in Problems 1–2 and used in Problem 3, where the near-identical pairs make the reason visible. It is explained in Problems 3–4 and generalized after that.
- **Real challenge:** the explanations are genuine proofs (counting ups and downs, or rows). The chevron needs two different reasons. The hourglass shows that the counting argument is not the whole story. The design problem has a surprising answer (7, not 9) that is verified to be best.

## 3. Grades 4–5 (two fourth graders, one fifth grader)

### 3.1 Thread

Children find all blue-rhombus coverings of small hexagons. They learn the flip, map how the coverings of one hexagon connect, and find the farthest-apart pair. They then discover that every covering has a **chain**: the rhombi that climb from the bottom edge to the top edge, read as a word of L's and R's. The chain is a code for the covering. It counts coverings (C(b+c, b) for a hexagon with bottom side 1), and it explains flips: each flip swaps one neighboring L and R in one chain. That one fact proves that some flip routes are shortest and that every round trip has an even number of flips. The last problem uses two chains to count the 20 coverings of the regular hexagon with 2 on each side. This is kernel 2: tilings, flips, ribbons, and their use for counting and for proofs about flips. Hexagon sides below are given as (bottom, lower right, upper right). The top, upper-left and lower-left sides repeat these. All hexagons have flat bottom and top edges.

### 3.2 Problems

**45 Problem 1 — All the ways**
- *Task:* "Find all the ways to cover each hexagon with blue rhombi. Draw each way on a small copy. For hexagon C, explain how you know you have found every way."
- *Boards (actual size, for building):* A (1,1,1), the yellow-block shape, 2.00 × 1.73 in; B (1,2,1), 2.50 × 2.60 in; C (1,2,2), a tall hexagon 3.00 × 3.46 in.
- *Drawing copies (half size):* 3 of A, 4 of B, 8 of C.
- *Records:* drawings, and about 4 lines of explanation.
- *Answers:* 2, 3 and 6 ways. Every covering of C uses 8 blues: 2 lying flat leaning right, 2 lying flat leaning left, and 4 standing. A sample completeness argument: the blue on the bottom edge leans left or right, and in each case follow upward.
- *Adult watches for:* duplicates and missing ones, and children who list randomly. Ask "How are you making sure?".

**45 Problem 2 — Flips and the map**
- *Task:* State the move with a picture (the yellow-block shape filled both ways, with a double arrow): "When three blue rhombi make the shape of a yellow hexagon, you may lift them out and put them back the other way. This is a flip." Then: "(a) Number your six ways of hexagon C. Make a map: a dot for each way, and a line between two dots when one flip changes one way into the other. (b) Which two ways need the most flips to get from one to the other? How many flips?"
- *Board:* hexagon C from Problem 1.
- *Records:* a map space of about half a page, and an answer box.
- *Answers:* the map has 6 dots and 6 lines: a square, with one extra dot hanging off each of two opposite corners. The two hanging dots are farthest apart, 4 flips. These are the ways whose chain letters (Problem 3) are RRLL and LLRR.
- *Adult watches for:* children finding where a flip is possible, that is, spotting the hexagon-shaped trios. The two "end" ways allow only one flip each.

**45 Problem 3 — Chains**
- *Task:* "Start at the short bottom edge of the hexagon. The rhombus sitting on it lies flat: its top edge is flat too. Climb through that top edge into the next rhombus, which also lies flat, and keep climbing until you reach the top edge. These rhombi form a **chain**. Write R for each rhombus in the chain that leans right and L for each one that leans left, from the bottom up." Show one example picture: hexagon (1,3,1), which is used nowhere else, with the chain shaded and labelled R, L, R, R (Appendix B). Then: "(a) Write the chain letters for each of your six ways of hexagon C. (b) Could two different ways have the same chain letters? Explain. (c) What happens to the chain letters when you make one flip?"
- *Records:* a 6-row table (way number → letters) and about 4 lines.
- *Answers:* (a) the six ways give the six different orders of L, L, R, R. (b) No: once the chain is fixed, the part of the hexagon to its left and the part to its right can each be filled only by standing rhombi, and in only one way, so the letters decide the whole covering. (c) One neighboring R L becomes L R, or the reverse, and the rest of the chain stays the same.
- *Adult watches for:* chains that wander, which happens when a child steps into a standing rhombus. Every rhombus touching a flat edge from above lies flat.

**45 Problem 4 — Counting with chains**
- *Task:* "How many ways are there to cover each of these hexagons with blue rhombi? Explain how you know each count is right."
- *Boards (actual size):* D (1,3,2), 3.50 × 4.33 in; E (1,3,3), 4.00 × 5.20 in; F (1,4,4), 5.00 × 6.93 in.
- *Records:* a number box and space for lists or work (about a third of a page each). Explanation lines after F.
- *Answers:* D **10**, E **20**, F **70**. The chain has as many R's as the lower-right side and as many L's as the upper-right side. Every order of those letters is the chain of exactly one covering. So the count is the number of orders: 10 = C(5,2), 20 = C(6,3), 70 = C(8,4). A child may find the Pascal pattern: orders starting with R plus orders starting with L, so 10 = 6 + 4 and 20 = 10 + 10.
- *Adult watches for:* systematic listing of words. Seventy is too many to list comfortably, which rewards counting by a rule.

**45 Problem 5 — Shortest flip routes**
- *Task:* "The start picture is printed on the board. Build it with blue rhombi. Using only flips, change it into the finish picture. Use as few flips as you can. Write the letters of the spots you flipped (see the key) and how many flips. For (c), explain why nobody can do it in fewer flips."
- *Boards:* three copies of the regular hexagon (2,2,2), 4.00 × 3.46 in, each printed with its start covering. Next to each is a half-size finish picture. On each page goes a half-size key picture of the hexagon with its 7 inner grid points lettered: A center, B right, C upper right, D upper left, E left, F lower left, G lower right. These are the only places a flip can happen. Coverings are given in Appendix B by their chains (left chain, right chain):
  a. start RLLR, RLRL → finish LRLR, LRRL;
  b. start RLRL, RRLL → finish LLRR, LRLR;
  c. start RRLL, RRLL → finish LLRR, LLRR.
- *Records:* a line for the letters and a number box per part, and about 4 lines for (c).
- *Answers:* a **2** (F, G); b **6** (e.g. F B D E G C); c **8** (e.g. A F B D E G C A). Explanation for (c): count the pairs "R before L" in the two chains. The start has 4 + 4 = 8 and the finish has 0, and each flip swaps one neighboring R L, so it changes the count by exactly 1. In the cube picture, each flip adds or removes one cube, and one picture is a full 2×2×2 box while the other is an empty corner. No other pair of coverings of this hexagon is 8 apart.
- *Adult watches for:* in all three parts the finish is the start turned one sixth of a turn. Children may expect that to be cheap, and the answers 2, 6, 8 are the surprise. Some children will see stacked cubes.

**45 Problem 6 — Round trips**
- *Task:* "Start from the covering printed on the board. A round trip is a list of flips that brings the board back exactly to the start. (a) Find a round trip of 4 flips and a round trip of 6 flips, never flipping the same spot twice in a row. Write the letters. (b) Can a round trip have an odd number of flips, like 3, 5 or 7? Explain."
- *Board:* the regular hexagon printed with the start covering, chains LRLR, RLRL. From it, flips are possible at B, C, D, E, F and G (six choices, the most of any covering), and the key picture is shown.
- *Records:* two letter lines, and about 5 lines for (b).
- *Answers:* (a) e.g. B E B E (4 flips; 18 such round trips exist) and B D B F D F (6 flips; 84 exist). (b) **No.** Each flip changes the number of "R before L" pairs in the chains by exactly 1, up or down, and to return to the start the ups and downs must cancel. With cubes: each flip adds or removes exactly one cube.
- *Adult watches for:* round trips that only undo the last flip. The rule excludes those. A child who answered Problem 5(c) has the tool for (b), so ask "What does a flip do to the chain letters?".

**45 Problem 7 — How many ways for the regular hexagon? (challenge)**
- *Task:* "How many ways are there to cover the hexagon with 2 on each side with blue rhombi? Explain how you know you have found them all."
- *Board:* a blank regular hexagon (2,2,2), 4.00 × 3.46 in, and a large work space.
- *Answer:* **20**. Each covering has two chains, one from each bottom edge, each an order of 2 R's and 2 L's. The chains cannot cross, and in letters this means that at every height the left chain has used at least as many L's as the right chain. Counting the pairs by the left chain: LLRR 6, LRLR 5, LRRL 3, RLLR 3, RLRL 2, RRLL 1, total 20.
- *Adult watches for:* 6 × 6 = 36 as a first guess, followed by the discovery that chains collide. That discovery is the heart of the problem.

### 3.4 Why the level is right

- **Easy entry:** Problem 1 starts with hexagons that have 2 and 3 coverings.
- **Ideas in concrete form before they are named:** the chain idea is already used in Problem 1's completeness argument ("the bottom rhombus leans left or right…") before Problem 3 names it, and flips are used physically before chains explain them.
- **Real challenge:** proving a list complete, finding that a code counts objects (binomial coefficients and Pascal), proving a route shortest, proving that round trips are even, and counting non-crossing pairs of chains.
- **Every claim is checkable by hand at this size:** 6 coverings, a map of 6 dots, routes of at most 8 flips.

## 4. Ways a problem could break, and how the design prevents them

- **Big blocks or off-grid pieces.** A 3× green covers the side-3 triangle in one piece, and pink, teal and gray pieces do not fit the grid. The rules allow only small blocks, and the off-grid pieces are cleared from the tables.
- **Covering a hole.** In grades 2–3 Problem 3 the holes are printed solid black and the rule forbids covering them.
- **Using the wrong blocks.** Each task names its blocks and shows their pictures. In grades 2–3 Problem 5 the task says "no blue rhombi".
- **"All the ways" boards.** These are fixed in place, so a covering and its mirror image are different. The 4–5 rules say so. In K–1 the adult says so if it comes up.
- **Unreachable finish.** In grades 4–5 Problem 5 every covering of the regular hexagon can be reached from every other by flips (checked), so no start/finish pair is impossible.
- **Odd round trips.** In grades 4–5 Problem 6 an odd round trip is impossible, which is the point. Even ones exist in both requested lengths, avoiding any immediate undo.
- **Games.** The games in K–1 Problem 6 always end, after at most 3, 4 or 5 blocks.
- **Spare copies.** The spare copy in K–1 Problem 3 is meant to stay unfilled. That is the point.
- **Grades 2–3 Problem 7.** "One piece, joined along edges" rules out boards that need many greens only because they fall apart into separate triangles.
